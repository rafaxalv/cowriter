# Style study: Ernest Hemingway

Text-based study made with the voice-generator method (October 2026). Shared by every book; the fit to one book belongs in that book's Author Library.

## 1. Text and caveats

- **Book**: *A Farewell to Arms* (1929), `library/hemingway/a-farewell-to-arms.txt` (Standard Ebooks, from the 1929 first edition). Original English.
- **Narration**: first person, past tense, retrospective (Frederic Henry, an American ambulance officer on the Italian front, 1915–18). The techniques below transfer to close third person.
- **Quality**: clean. Swear words appear as "⸻", as censored in the first edition.
- **Sample**: 12 sampled passages, plus the two decisive violent events added by hand (the mortar wound, ch. IX; the shooting of the sergeant, ch. XXIX). Rescored on 6 fresh passages.

What stands out: the shortest sentences and the fewest commas of the group, almost no semicolons, a small and repeated vocabulary, and the most "saw / heard / looked". The text reports perception, not interpretation. The named-emotion rate (4.0 per 1,000) looks average, but it is inflated by the lovers' dialogue ("I love you", "lovely"); in the narration, named feelings are rare.

The one exception to the short sentences is description: landscape and troop movement run in long sentences chained with "and", with few commas (the opening chapter, the roads to the front).

## 3. How events are told

**The event as a whole.** Hemingway enters violent events through routine. The wound comes in the middle of a meal: the men are eating cheese and drinking wine, and the shell arrives inside that sentence. The killing of the sergeant starts as a practical problem: a car is stuck in the mud and two sergeants refuse to cut brush. The setup is the work in hand, told in plain practical steps; the reader does not know it is setup.

The peak is told at the speed it happens, or faster. The shooting takes five sentences, all action. Catherine's death, the end of the book, is compressed into one: "I went into the room and stayed with Catherine until she died." The **exception** is the narrator's own near-death: the mortar blast is slowed and expanded into sensation ("a roar that started white and went red and on and on in a rushing wind"), the only place where the prose leaves the body.

The exit comes right after the outcome, with no reflection. After the shooting the next line goes back to the task: "We've got to get the brush quickly." The meaning of an event arrives later and sideways: the killing returns two chapters on as the drivers' joking ("Bless me, father, I killed a sergeant"), and the narrator says nothing about it.

**The action from start to end.** Every physical step is shown, in order, joined by "and" or full stops, each step a short clause with a concrete verb: open the holster, take the pistol, aim, fire, miss, fire three times, drop one, reload. Practical sub-steps are kept, even absurd ones: Bonello's pistol does not fire until he is told to cock it. The result is stated as a fact ("he was dead already. I made sure he was dead."). Granularity is high for what the body does and nil for what the mind makes of it.

## 4. How feelings reach the reader

- **Mostly withheld or carried by the body and by behaviour**: tiredness, thirst, hunger, itching wounds, a man who "did not say anything". At the moment of greatest pain, the narrator orders food in a café.
- **Spoken, with the meaning under the words**: Catherine's fear of dying runs through banter about "lovely temperatures" and haircuts after the birth. Repetition carries the feeling ("I won't talk." "Don't brag, darling.").
- **Named rarely, plainly, never explained**: "I was still angry", "I felt faint with loving her so much", "The exhilaration was all gone". A single flat clause with no cause attached.
- **Thought quoted in a few words**: "What the hell, I thought."
- **Displaced onto an anecdote or the weather**: the ants on the burning log, told while Catherine is dying, carries his helplessness without a word about it; rain is constant and never commented on.
- **Timing**: during the event, almost nothing; the feeling shows afterwards, in what he does.

## 5. What is explained and what is left

Very little is explained. The narrator never says why he shoots the sergeant, whether he regrets it, or what the war means; he reports, and the reader judges. Catherine's dread is never stated by the narrator. Gaps are mostly **permanent**: the book does not come back to explain them. The rare retrospective comment is factual, not moral ("I did not know until later that beds could be made with men in them"). Dialogue is high in subtext: people talk about temperatures, clothes, horses and money while the real subject (death, desertion, love) stays unsaid. Key information is given once.

## 7. Techniques a voice can borrow

1. **Violence inside routine**: start the event in the middle of an ordinary task, and let the violence interrupt the sentence the task is in.
2. **Steps, not meaning**: give every physical step of the act in order, in short clauses joined by "and"; give no step of what it means.
3. **Back to work**: end the scene on the next practical action, not on a reaction.
4. **Delayed echo**: let the meaning of an event return later in someone else's mouth (a joke, an offhand remark), with no comment.
5. **One flat clause**: when a feeling must be named, name it once, plainly, with no cause ("I was still angry.").
6. **Talk around it**: in the most charged dialogue, have characters discuss something small and practical.
7. **Displace the feeling**: an image or anecdote beside the event (the ants on the log) carries what the character cannot say.
8. **Compress the worst**: tell the most terrible moment in one plain sentence, and spend the words on the minutes after.
