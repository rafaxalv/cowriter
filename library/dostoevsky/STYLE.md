# Style study: Fyodor Dostoevsky

Text-based study made with the voice-generator method (October 2026). Shared by every book; the fit to one book belongs in that book's Author Library.

## 1. Text and caveats

- **Book**: *Crime and Punishment* (1866), `library/dostoevsky/crime-and-punishment.txt`, in Constance Garnett's English translation (1914), from Standard Ebooks.
- **Translation**: the surface (sentence length, punctuation, vocabulary, Victorian register) is partly Garnett's. Event structure, the handling of feelings and the explanations survive translation and are the reliable part of this study.
- **Narration**: third person, very close to Raskolnikov, with excursions into Luzhin, Svidrigaïlov, Sonia and Dounia. The narrator sometimes generalises and comments, and the epilogue is narrated from a distance.
- **Quality**: clean. The file opens with the translator's preface, which was left out of the sample.
- **Sample**: 11 sampled passages plus the murder (Part I, ch. 7), added by hand as the decisive event. Rescored on 6 fresh passages.

What stands out: the most dialogue, and dialogue that is loud (questions, exclamations, long tirades); the most named emotions, thinking verbs, adverbs of manner and explanation markers. A book about a murder with the fewest violence words: the violence is mental. Every measure points the same way: the inner life is spelled out.

## 3. How events are told

**The event as a whole.** Dostoevsky enters the decisive event after a long build-up, and the build-up is the subject. By the time of the murder the reader has followed the plan, the rehearsal and the axe hung in a noose inside his coat. The scene itself opens on a practical detail of the plan (the old woman turns to the window to untie the fake pledge), and the blow falls only when she turns back.

The peak is slowed down. The killing is told blow by blow, with every physical detail (the grey, greasy plait, "the blood gushed as from an overturned glass") and the state of his hands and head at each step. The aftermath is longer than the act: the fumbling with keys, the purse on its string, the second killing (Lizaveta), then the fever that fills the following chapters. The exit is never clean: events spill into delirium, confession, dreams and other people's talk. The **exception** is Svidrigaïlov's suicide, built through a long wandering walk in the fog and cut on the exact moment: "Svidrigaïlov pulled the trigger." End of chapter.

**The action from start to end.** Granularity is very high, but the action is a record of the mind: each step comes with a state ("His hands were fearfully weak", "A sudden giddiness came over him"), a mistake or a reversal (he lifts the axe a second time and does not bring it down; he keeps trying keys he knows are wrong). "Suddenly" marks every turn. Parentheses and hindsight are slipped into the action ("He remembered afterwards that he had been particularly collected and careful"). The result: the reader watches a man failing to be the cold actor of his own theory.

## 4. How feelings reach the reader

- **Named and explained, at full strength**: "a strange, surprising sensation of a sort of bitter hatred for Sonia passed through his heart". The narrator then interprets it: it was not the real feeling; it only meant that the moment had come. Emotions come with intensifiers ("dreadfully frightened", "unendurable").
- **Bodily signs as well**, always with the feeling named beside them: he turns pale, his lips work, Sonia's heart "throbbed and sank".
- **Thought, in every form**: long quoted monologue (his tirade against his sister's marriage runs for a page), free indirect thought, and the narrator's psycho-narration of whole periods ("A strange period began for Raskolnikov...").
- **Spoken**: characters confess, accuse and argue out their feelings at length. Feelings are rarely held back.
- **Timing**: during the event, and again afterwards in recollection ("Recalling that period long after, he believed...").

## 5. What is explained and what is left

Most things are explained, often several times over: motives (Luzhin's fear of being "shown up" gets a full paragraph of background), causes and states of mind. The narrator foreshadows openly ("He did not know that the new life would not be given him for nothing") and closes the book by naming its next subject. What is left open is the deepest question: why he really killed. Theory, poverty, pride and illness are all put forward, by him and by others, and none settles it. The gap is filled with too many explanations rather than none. Dialogue usually says what it means, at length; the exceptions are the most menacing characters (Svidrigaïlov's "I am going to America" before he shoots himself; the investigator's games), where subtext rules.

## 7. Techniques a voice can borrow

1. **The plan before the act**: let the reader know the plan and the props, so the act is watched with dread, step by step.
2. **Action as a symptom**: give each physical step a state of the body or mind (weak hands, giddiness), and let the actor make mistakes.
3. **The second blow not struck**: a reversal or hesitation at the peak (lifting the weapon again, not bringing it down) shows the mind better than any comment.
4. **Aftermath longer than the act**: give more words to what the act does to the person than to the act.
5. **The mistaken feeling**: a character feels one thing and the narrative shows it was another ("he had taken the one feeling for the other").
6. **Cut on the trigger**: once in a book, build an event slowly and cut on the exact instant, with no aftermath.
7. **"Suddenly"** as the hinge of a scene: rationed, it marks the turn from plan to chaos.
