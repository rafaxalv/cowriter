# Research Compilation: Slavery in Brazil and the Paraguayan War

*Everything gathered in this chat session (1 October 2026), in one place. Part A is the timeline, Part B covers the Zuavos Baianos and Dom Obá from the shared source document, Part C collects voices of the enslaved, Part D lists open questions and discrepancies, Part E lists sources.*

**Confidence tags:** (Solid) = broad scholarly consensus; (Debated) = supported but argued over or estimates vary; (Contested) = weak evidence or disputed interpretation; (Folklore) = oral tradition or legend, kept because it can be used as in-world "what people say."

# Part A. Timeline of slavery in Brazil

## A1. Long arc, 1500s to 1850

  - **1530s–1550s.** Africans are brought to Brazil from very early in the colonial period. (Solid) Brazil received roughly 4–5 million Africans, depending on the estimate, far more than any other American destination. About 1.7 million arrived between 1800 and 1850, the same as in the whole 18th century. (Solid on scale; estimates vary)
  - **c. 1605–1695.** Palmares, a network of runaway communities (quilombos) in the northeast, survives for most of the 17th century. (Solid)
  - **1822.** At independence, about 1.5 million of Brazil's 3.5 million people were enslaved. Slavery was backed by nearly every part of society, including the Church.
  - **1824 Constitution.** It protects property rights. Slaves, as property and non-citizens, were exempt from military recruitment. This matters for the war.
  - **1831.** The Feijó law declares Africans arriving after that date free, but is barely enforced. (Solid) Imports fell to about 26,000 in 1831–36, then jumped to 201,140 in 1836–40. (Debated source; the pattern is widely accepted)
  - **1835.** The Malê revolt, led by Muslim Africans, breaks out in Salvador.
  - **1850.** The Eusébio de Queirós Law ends the Atlantic trade under British naval pressure. (Solid) Slavery itself continues, and an interprovincial trade moved an estimated 5,000–10,000 slaves a year from north to south from the 1850s to the 1870s.

## A2. The Paraguayan War (1864–1870)

### Phase 1, 1865–66: slaves stay out (Solid)

  - By law and practice, slaves were not recruited. Owners freed them on condition of enlisting.
  - Slaves reached the ranks in four ways: donations by owners, substitutes for pressed men, later government-compensated emancipations, and running away to enlist as free men.
  - Fugitives who enlisted were routinely returned to their owners unless they had distinguished themselves. Kraay found 36 such returns in Bahia and 140 in Rio.
  - 630 donated slaves came from Rio de Janeiro city, where pressure to contribute was strongest.
  - Slave substitutes were rare. Free recruits could buy their way out for 600$000, which was cheaper than a slave.

### Phase 2, Nov 1866–1868: the state buys freedom for soldiers (Solid)

  - On 5 November 1866 the Council of State debated recruiting slaves. Four members opposed it, fearing it would raise hopes of abolition and expose Brazil's "impotence" before the world. Six favored it.
  - Councillor Pimenta Bueno argued for sparing the "most civilized and virtuous" class and sending slaves instead. Kraay says this attitude "smells of genocide," though not that it was policy.
  - The next day, Decree 3725 freed government-owned slaves fit for service, along with the wives of those drafted. Pedro II urged the policy personally and freed 67 slaves of the Imperial Household.
  - From 1867 the government bought slaves from owners, freed them, and enlisted them for nine years. Navy agents toured the northeast advertising for "robust" men.
  - Owners were paid roughly market price, partly in government bonds that had fallen to about 88% of face value. Bahian prices rose almost 50% over three semesters.
  - Most sellers owned a single slave. Dealers specialized in arranging these sales.

### How many? (Debated, with a strong archival case)

  - The official count is **6,905** (4,003 army, 2,902 navy), confirmed by Kraay's archival checks in Bahia and Rio. That is about 0.86% of Brazil's male slaves and 4.4% of army mobilization.
  - Bahia and Rio city supplied about 60% of those whose origin is known. The coffee provinces supplied almost none.
  - The freed men were healthy young men, mostly in their early twenties. (Solid)
  - Two higher figures circulate. The 20,000 estimate traces back to a French doctor writing in the 1880s. Chiavenato's claim of 60,000–100,000 Black deaths is not supported by the archives. (Contested)

### Paraguay's side

  - Paraguay passed a free-womb law in 1842. Children born after 1 January 1843 were "libertos" but owed service to the owner until 24 (women) or 25 (men). No one is known to have been freed this way before the war took them to the battlefield.
  - Abolition came in 1869; sources give different dates (one decree is dated 2 October 1869). Sources say only about 450 slaves were left to benefit. That figure comes from popular and secondary sources, so verify it before using it. Slavery was constitutionally abolished in 1870.
  - Kraay notes that Brazil's occupation commander, the emperor's son-in-law, oversaw the 1869 abolition, but sees it as hardly significant given how few slaves remained.
  - Many men of the Afro-Paraguayan Kamba Cuá community were drafted and suffered heavy losses. (Moderate-quality source)
  - Gap: I did not find solid sources on the Argentine and Uruguayan sides.

## A3. After the war

  - **1870.** Owners began recapturing runaways who had enlisted and been demobilized. The Justice Ministry ruled they must be treated as free men, but owners could still prove claims and seek compensation. One man, Simão, was found in 1874 as José Pinto de Carvalho, a sailor, and brought in to testify on his former master's claim.
  - Kraay finds that all known re-enslavement attempts involved runaways, not men freed for the war. The "re-enslaved veterans" story is partly a myth. (Debated)
  - Men freed in 1867–68 served nine-year terms, so many did not return to civilian life until the late 1870s.
  - Veterans came home to the same poverty. Manuel Pedro da Silva, freed in 1868, was beaten and stabbed in 1878 after a dispute over his uniform. His officer cited his slave origins as proof he lacked deference.
  - Many veterans settled in Rio. Dom Obá's newspaper articles demanded political participation, and veterans were prominent in the capoeira gangs of postwar electoral politics. Dom Obá died in 1890.
  - **1871.** The Free Womb Law frees children born to slave mothers. Opponents cited wartime manumissions as proof that owners supported gradual emancipation, so they argued state intervention was unnecessary.
  - **1885.** The Sexagenarian Law frees those over 65, subject to compensation. (Solid)
  - **Oct 1887.** The Army Club petitions Princess Isabel to be excused from hunting runaways. (Solid) Robert Conrad argues that officers who fought in Paraguay developed an antipathy to slavery. (Debated) Kraay finds little evidence that the war significantly changed military attitudes toward slavery and calls the common view overly ideological.
  - **13 May 1888.** The Lei Áurea frees about 700,000 people, with no compensation to owners and no land or support for the freed. Brazil is the last country in the Americas to abolish slavery. (Solid)
  - **1889.** The Republic is proclaimed, with support from planters who resented not being compensated.
  - **Dec 1890.** Finance Minister Rui Barbosa authorised destruction of most government records relating to slavery. The order itself is solid. The motive is contested: the popular explanation that he was blocking owner compensation claims appears in citations only from the 1970s on, and other accounts say he wanted to erase the national "stain."

# Part B. The Zuavos Baianos and Dom Obá (from the shared source document)

*The shared document combines text from Claudio Moreira Bento ("O Exército e a Abolição"), Hendrik Kraay ("Os companheiros de Dom Obá"), and a municipal page from Uruguaiana (COPIR) on Cândido da Fonseca Galvão. Summarized here in my own words.*

## B1. The Zuavos Baianos

  - A unit made up of Black men, with uniforms modeled on the French colonial Zouaves of Algeria. They left for the war zone in May 1865 on the ship "São Francisco" (formerly the American "Cotopaxi"), in two companies, along with engineer lieutenant André Rebouças, later a leading abolitionist. (Solid, per Bento)
  - Pedro II and War Minister Angelo Ferraz saw them off in Rio de Janeiro. They later took part in the siege and surrender at Uruguaiana, with the emperor present.
  - The Bahian government's decision to raise Black companies in 1865 is still unexplained. It broke with the army's formal color-blind policy: segregated militia units had been abolished in 1831, and the last racial exclusion in recruitment was lifted in 1837. (Solid, per Kraay)
  - The proposal came from outside the army. Quirino Antônio do Espírito Santo offered on 26 January 1865 to organize a corps of "crioulo" volunteers, invoking the 17th-century Black commander Henrique Dias. He set up at Forte do Barbalho on 1 February, and a subscription was approved to buy uniforms.
  - Numbers: 11 Zuavo companies totalling 638 men and one company of Couraças of 80 men left Bahia before March 1866, per the shared text. (See Part D for the conflicting count.)
  - Many leaders of the Bahian mobilization were veterans of the 1822–23 war of independence: José da Rocha Galvão (lieutenant-colonel, commander), José Elói Buri (captain of the Couraças, who resembled leather-clad cowboys of the sertão), Domingos Mundim Pestana, and Colonel Joaquim Antônio da Silva Carvalhal, a key organizer and head of the Society of Independence Veterans.
  - Francisco Moniz Barreto, a popular Bahian poet, wrote the "Hino dos zuavos baianos" for the first company's departure. Its tone is patriotic and links the men to Henrique Dias and to the French Zouaves.

## B2. What happened to the officers and men

  - Few of the older veterans survived. Quirino died in Montevideo's hospital in November 1865. Pestana, sick and blind, returned to Salvador by February 1866 and died two years later. Rocha Galvão was killed at the first Battle of Tuiuti (24 May 1866). Buri died of cholera in late 1867.
  - A contemporary described Quirino as an old Black man his soldiers respected like a father. (Per Kraay, citing a stopover at Desterro)
  - Other officers: Lt. Manoel Teodoro de Jesus died of cholera. Lt. Augusto Francisco da Silva died of shrapnel wounds in March 1867. Captains Marcolino and Maniva, Ensign Bernardino de Sena Trindade and Lt. Balbino Nunes Pereira were discharged for unspecified physical incapacity.
  - Captain João Francisco Barbosa de Oliveira commanded the Third Company, served the whole campaign, was wounded twice, never took leave, and was reportedly among the troops who killed Francisco Solano López at Aquidabã. No ex-Zuavo rose above captain.
  - Cadet Constantino Luiz Xavier Bigode was captured after the Battle of Curupaiti and spent over two years as a prisoner working at the Ybicuí foundry. He was freed in 1869 and promoted to ensign in 1870.
  - José Soares Cupim Júnior enlisted on 1 February 1865, rose from sergeant to captain, was praised at Curuzu, was wounded at second Tuiuti (24 September 1867) and again at Lomas Valentinas, and died on 13 January 1869. In 1871 his widow Panfília Luiza Tolentino Soares received an annual pension of 720 mil-réis.
  - Parliament approved pensions for the widows of four more Zuavo officers (including Quirino's widow, Sabina Joana do Espírito Santo).
  - In 1870 three Voluntários da Pátria battalions returned to Bahia to celebrations and were dissolved. Many soldiers were discharged without back pay. Few Zuavo veterans were among them, and the press mentioned only Captain Barbosa.
  - Most veterans rejoined the free poor population of color they came from. In the early 20th century Manoel Querino recorded their names and deeds from oral tradition.

## B3. Stories and quotes worth keeping (some are folklore)

  - (Folklore, via Manoel Querino) Captain Marcolino, a capoeira, climbed the Paraguayan wall on a soldier's back, tore down a Paraguayan flag and raised the green-and-yellow one, proclaiming himself the Black Bahian Zouave. The Zuavos' role in storming the Curuzu trenches is also cited from Arthur Ramos's "O Negro como Soldado."
  - (Solid as a record of opinion) Count d'Eu, in his 1865 military journey, called them the most beautiful troop in the Brazilian army, all Black including the officers, and not worse officers for it. He spent a long time talking with them.
  - (Solid as a record of opinion) In April 1866, diplomat Francisco Otaviano wrote from Buenos Aires celebrating Brazilians of every color after the allied crossing of the Paraná. This opens the question of whether the war created a shared national, racially mixed experience. (Debated)
  - (Debated, from Bento) The Zuavos initially belonged to roughly 57 Voluntários da Pátria battalions that were later merged down to 19, and the Zuavo troops were absorbed into other units after Osório dissolved them.

## B4. Dom Obá II (Cândido da Fonseca Galvão)

  - Born possibly in 1845 in Lençóis, Bahia, son of Benvindo da Fonseca Galvão, a freed African of the Yoruba nation. Probably from a well-off family, possibly from diamond mining. (Debated on dates and wealth)
  - Enlisted as a volunteer just before the decree creating the Voluntários da Pátria (the municipal page dates his enlistment to early January 1865). He served in one of the Zuavo companies, reached the rank of ensign (alferes), and was recognized as an honorary officer for bravery. (Solid on service; exact dates need checking)
  - Moved to Rio de Janeiro in the mid-1870s, where he became a folkloric, sometimes caricatured figure, but was revered as a prince by many Afro-Brazilians in the suburbs, free and enslaved. The title "Obá II d'África" is Yoruba-derived.
  - He wrote articles in Rio newspapers in the 1880s defending the monarchy, attacking slavery and racial discrimination, and used his war service as proof of belonging to the Brazilian nation. He attended Pedro II's public audiences and spoke to the emperor directly.
  - He wore dress uniform, top hat, white gloves and an ensign's hat at a time when few Black men wore shoes. Some saw him as a reference point for those seeking freedom, others as eccentric ("amalucado").
  - After the monarchy fell in 1889, the republicans stripped his rank. He died in July 1890. (Solid)

# Part C. Voices of the enslaved

Direct statements are rare. These are the main ones.

  - **Esperança Garcia, 1770 (Piauí).** A petition asking to return to her husband and children. She reported that her son was beaten until blood came from his mouth. It is the earliest known slave petition in Brazil. (Solid)
  - **Mahommah Gardo Baquaqua, 1854.** Kidnapped in West Africa in 1845 and sold to a baker in Pernambuco, who beat him when he failed to sell his loaves and enforced piety with a whip. He escaped in New York in 1847. It is the only known book-length narrative by a Brazilian slave. (Solid, though co-written with an editor, Samuel Moore)
  - **Luiz Gama, 1880.** In a letter to Lúcio de Mendonça he says his father sold him at age 10. "Meu pai, não ouso afirmar que fosse branco." He was sold in 1840 and walked from Santos to Campinas, where buyers rejected him for being from Bahia. Gama later freed hundreds of enslaved people through the courts. (Solid)
  - **Petitions from the war, via Kraay.** Thomaz de Aquino, facing 200 lashes in 1867, asked to be sent to the army, saying "I have long had a vocation for brawling." He was refused because only masters could offer a slave's services. Quinto repeatedly ran away to enlist, and when a recruiter finally took him, his master gave up and asked for compensation. Manuel Pedro da Silva's 1878 petition asked for the respect due to a citizen who had served his country.
  - **Hino dos Zuavos baianos (1865).** Written by the poet Francisco Moniz Barreto, not by an enslaved person, but it shows the rhetoric of the moment.

# Part D. Open questions and discrepancies

  - **Size of the Bahian Black companies.** The shared text says 11 Zuavo companies with 638 men plus 80 Couraças. Kraay's 1997 footnote counts 742 men in nine companies organized by Carvalhal, plus one company each organized by two others, and says the total cannot have exceeded 1,000. Check the primary roster he cites (Arquivo Público do Estado da Bahia, maço 3675).
  - **Dom Obá's enlistment date.** The municipal page says early January 1865 and ties it to the volunteer decree. Check against a primary source.
  - **Number of slaves freed for the war.** 6,905 is archival; 20,000 and 60,000–100,000 circulate in older or polemical works.
  - **Paraguayan abolition.** Dates vary (1869 decree, 1870 constitution), and the figure of about 450 freed slaves is only from popular sources.
  - **Did the war make the army abolitionist?** Conrad says yes; Kraay finds little evidence. The 1887 Army Club petition is solid, but its cause is debated.
  - **Rui Barbosa's burning of records.** The order is solid; the motive is not.
  - **Gaps.** Argentine and Uruguayan sources; 1888-era oral testimonies; Paraguayan sources on the war's social consequences.
