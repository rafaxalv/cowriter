# Paraguayan War (Guerra do Paraguai) – Research Notes

*Compiled 2 October 2026 for the book project. Everything is sorted by credibility, and low-reliability material is deliberately kept as usable hearsay/folk legend. Items from the full texts of Kraay and Maestri and from the "Dados gerais" doc were read directly; items marked (summary) were taken from search-result summaries only and should be checked against the originals.*

## 0. How to read the tags

  - **\[Documented\]** – backed by archival or official records, cited by serious scholarship.
  - **\[Scholarly claim\]** – an academic's interpretation; credible but contested.
  - **\[Tradition\]** – oral or folk tradition recorded by a chronicler; plausible, not verified.
  - **\[Legend/propaganda\]** – nationalist, revisionist, popular or period-propaganda material; use as story, not fact.

## 1. Consensus facts (safe to state)

  - War ran from late 1864 to 1 March 1870. Paraguay (Francisco Solano López) vs the Triple Alliance of Brazil, Argentina and Uruguay (treaty of 1 May 1865).
  - Trigger: Brazilian intervention in Uruguay; López seized the Brazilian steamer Marquês de Olinda (Nov 1864), invaded Mato Grosso (Dec 1864), then Argentine Corrientes (1865).
  - Key events: Riachuelo (June 1865); Uruguaiana surrender (18 Sept 1865); Tuiuti (24 May 1866); Curuzu (3 Sept 1866); Curupaiti (22 Sept 1866, Allied disaster); fall of Humaitá (1868); Lomas Valentinas (Dec 1868); Asunción occupied (Jan 1869); Piribebuy (12 Aug 1869); Acosta Ñu / Campo Grande (16 Aug 1869); Cerro Corá (1 March 1870, López killed).
  - Commanders: Caxias, Osório, Mitre, Tamandaré, Barroso; Conde d'Eu in the final phase.
  - Consequences: Paraguayan territorial losses, occupation until 1876, political rise of the Brazilian army (which fed into 1889), and a push for abolition because slaves were freed to serve.

### Rank 3 – Historical primary sources (real, each with a bias)

  - **Manoel Querino** (served in the war; wrote down the Marcolino tradition early 20th century).
  - **Conde d'Eu, *Viagem Militar ao Rio Grande do Sul* (1865)** – first-hand praise of the zuavos.
  - **Centurión (Paraguayan), Taunay and Cerqueira (Brazilian) memoirs** – vivid; written decades later in Taunay's and Cerqueira's cases; each has self-serving elements.
  - Also worth finding: Washburn, Burton, Rebouças's war diary, Resquín.

### Rank 4 – Contested / revisionist, widely read

  - **Júlio José Chiavenato, *Genocídio Americano* (1979)** – British-imperialism conspiracy thesis. Doratioto says no document shows the British government wanted a war here. Popular with the public, rejected by most academics.

### Rank 5 – Paraguayan national memory (strong, one-sided)

  - O'Leary (1922) hagiography; Paraguayan school manuals; newspaper pieces on the Conde d'Eu and Acosta Ñu (summary).

## 3. Text-by-text extraction

### A. "Dados gerais" doc – Os Zuavos da Bahia (Bento / Kraay excerpt) and the Cândido da Fonseca Galvão page (COPIR Uruguaiana)

  - Zuavos baianos: a unit of Black men with showy uniforms resembling French zouaves of Algeria; arrived in the theatre of war in May 1865 (per Bento) aboard the "São Francisco" (ex-"Cotopaxi"). Lieutenant André Rebouças is said to have gone with them.
  - Farewell in Rio attended by Pedro II and the War Minister. The zuavos met the Emperor again at the siege of Uruguaiana.
  - Capitão Marcolino story: climbs the wall on a soldier's back, replaces a Paraguayan flag with the green-and-yellow one, announces himself as the Black Bahian zuavo (Querino). Praised in orders of the day and the press.
  - Conde d'Eu's praise: the most handsome troop in the army, Black officers who were not worse for it.
  - Kraay's section: Quirino volunteers on 26 Jan 1865; 11 companies, 638 men, plus 80 couraças; the unit's veterans of Independence; Dom Obá II; fates of officers; widows' pensions; Querino as memory-keeper.
  - COPIR page on Dom Obá II: says he was born possibly in 1845 in Lençóis, son of a freed African of Yoruba nation; volunteered; "decorated for great bravery"; moved to Rio in the 1870s, wrote newspaper articles, wore a gala uniform; the Republic stripped his rank; he died in July 1890. **This contradicts Kraay** (see section 4).

### B. Kraay (2012) – the key source for the zuavos

**Core facts \[Documented\]**

  - Why Bahia created Black companies is a mystery; it went against the army's colour-blind policy since 1837. The proposal came from outside the army (Quirino, a veteran of 1822–23).
  - Colonel Joaquim Antônio da Silva Carvalhal was the key organizer: Liberal, abolitionist, helped soldiers' families, freed a child slave in 1870 with Captain Barbosa handing over the letter.
  - First and second companies reached Uruguaiana in time for the Paraguayan surrender on 18 Sept 1865.
  - Curuzu (3 Sept 1866): the Bahians and Pernambucans led the assault; the 66-year-old Pernambucan captain Maniva was among the first over the trenches.
  - Captain Barbosa served the whole war, wounded twice, among the troops who killed López at Aquidabã.
  - Cadet Constantino Bigode was captured after Curupaiti and spent over two years as a prisoner at the Ybycuí foundry; his father had been the last commander of the Black militia battalion, killed in the Sabinada aftermath.
  - Numbers: more than a thousand men in the Black companies overall (zuavos, couraças and later sapadores, who were mostly freed slaves); Bahia mobilized 10,189 men by March 1866.

**Plot twists**

  - The government used the companies and then dissolved them in 1866; officers were scattered across eight different battalions on 30 Oct 1866.
  - Osório sent some zuavos to hospital orderly duty; Kraay says this smacks of slaveholder attitudes about Black men's proper work.
  - No zuavo officer ever rose above captain.
  - Many "volunteers" were not: Kraay says most Brazilians who served did so unwillingly.
  - Zuavos were rowdy: newspaper complaints about guards making noise at night, harassing Black women, throwing fireworks; street boys who joined were called "zuavinhos".
  - Slaves used the zuavos to escape owners. One slave sent to buy banana leaves returned in uniform; another, Simão, drowned fleeing forced recruitment.

**Marcolino José Dias \[Documented + press accusation\]**

  - A conservative paper accused him of being a police spy and election enforcer ("headbutts"), leading a gang to intimidate the only opposition deputy. Kraay says whether true or not, it shows his place in party-political street life, tied to the capoeira world.
  - Popular figure in Salvador in 1867; survived an assassination attempt in September 1868 after the conservatives returned to power.
  - Dismissed as interim commander of the provisional zuavo corps on 1 Jan 1866; honorary officer rank in June 1867.
  - A later chronicler says he ended as a street sweeper, then library doorman; died 1888; took part in abolitionism and in the Sociedade Protetora dos Desvalidos.

**Dom Obá II (Cândido da Fonseca Galvão) \[Documented, Kraay's reading\]**

  - Brought thirty volunteers from Lençóis to Salvador and was promoted to alferes the day before embarkation.
  - Dismissed 12 Jan 1866 by General Porto Alegre for habitual bad behaviour and rowdiness; told a diplomat two councils of war had cleared him; discharged in March 1866 for poor health.
  - Folklorists later said his service record was clean and praiseworthy. Kraay finds no proof he fought: no documents, a claimed fire destroyed his papers, no mention in orders of the day. He concludes he likely never returned to service.
  - Still got the honours of alferes in 1872; the Republic revoked them after his monarchist demonstration in Dec 1889.
  - Two mysteries remain: how he got the honours, and why captains Barbosa and Marcolino, who knew the truth, did not contradict the legend. Kraay's tentative suggestion: solidarity with a comrade they thought wronged (speculation).

**Controversy: why so many Black soldiers?** Chiavenato reads it as a genocidal cannon-fodder policy, especially after systematic slave recruitment from late 1866. Others, citing diplomat Francisco Otaviano's cheer for white, Black, mulatto and caboclo Brazilians, see a shared national experience. Paraguayan propaganda portrayed all Brazilian soldiers as "monkeys".

**Errors Kraay corrects**

  - Eduardo Silva wrongly identified the 24th Volunteers battalion as zuavos and wrongly attached the Third Company to it.
  - Barroso's theory that the uniform honoured a Rio-born French zouave killed at the Malakoff in Crimea was never invoked in Bahia.
  - Only Bahia created zuavo companies; the Pernambuco company took the name later.

### C. Maestri (2013) – Piribebuy, 12 August 1869

  - **Context:** After Lomas Valentinas, López rebuilt his force from veterans, elders, adolescents, women and even children. The Conde d'Eu, age 27, took command in 1869.
  - **Paraguayan patriotic version \[Tradition\]:** O'Leary (1922) – women took the guns once the men fell, coconuts were used as cannon shot, d'Eu beheaded the commander and burned the hospital with hundreds inside; repeated in school manuals.
  - **Centurión \[Scholarly, with caveats\]:** 1,600 defenders, mostly boys, vs about 20,000 attackers; three assaults over five hours; commander Caballero defiantly refused two offers of quarter; General Mena Barreto shot in the groin; d'Eu ordered two prisoners beheaded; soldiers then killed the defenceless; hospital fire perhaps from the bombardment. Centurión was not present and wrote decades later.
  - **Brazilian official version \[Documented, self-serving\]:** 19,000 Imperial, about 900 Argentine, 1,000 Uruguayan troops; about 50 cannons; assault at 8:30 after two hours of bombardment; breach in 15 to 45 minutes; about 700 Paraguayan dead and 69 Allied dead.
  - **Maestri's argument:** The ratio of 700 dead to just over 300 wounded points to executions, especially of adolescents and children. Taunay's and Cerqueira's later memoirs admit executions and describe their own interventions; Maestri says it's hard to know whether these edifying stories were real or comforting reconstructions. The beheading order is plausible, but the quoted words are probably popular invention. Hospital fire: undecided. Imperial memoirs treat looting and rape of Paraguayan women as nearly normal.
  - **Colour for the book:** Taunay took a luxury Don Quixote that belonged to López and drank his champagne; soldiers carried away Madame Lynch's silver; Taunay's memoir says Mena Barreto fell into the arms of the woman who followed him into battle; Pedro Américo's 1871 painting glorified the count.
  - **Maestri's minority claim \[Scholarly claim\]:** López's family and elites had been conspiring since 1867 to remove him and surrender.

### D. Whigham (2002) (summary)

  - Describes the war as bloody and inexcusable though it paved the way for more modern societies.
  - Argues the war's causes lie in the political ambitions and nationalism of the main actors.

### E. The death-toll debate \[Scholarly claim, still open\] (summary)

  - Vera Reber (1988) argued Paraguay's losses were exaggerated.
  - Whigham and Potthast (1999), using a newly found 1870 census, argued the old high estimates (roughly 60 to 69%) were basically right.
  - Kleinpenning argued the census was unreliable and recalculated 43 to 52%.
  - No academic demographic work puts the loss below 7% or above 69%.
  - Doratioto says 90% of the men died.

*Correction note: an earlier draft in this chat wrongly attributed the low estimate to Whigham and Potthast. It was Reber who argued lower; Whigham and Potthast argued for a much higher toll.*

### F. Doratioto vs Chiavenatto (summary)

  - Doratioto: no official document shows British government desire for a war; the conspiracy thesis caught on in Brazil because the left, which saw US imperialism as its enemy under the dictatorship, wanted another villain; in Argentina it took root on the right.
  - Critics of Doratioto: Maestri (appears to be the interviewee in the Outras Palavras piece) calls it a restoration of patriotic theses.
  - Argentine Marxists Peña and Rivera questioned the imperialism thesis in the 1950s and 60s.
  - A July 2026 BBC News Brasil piece reports the war is back at the center of a diplomatic crisis. Not yet read; worth checking.

## 4. Where the sources contradict each other

| Topic | Version 1 | Version 2 | Assessment |

| Dom Obá II's service | COPIR page: decorated for great bravery, honorary officer | Kraay: dismissed Jan 1866 for rowdiness, no proof he fought | Kraay is archival and stronger. The legend itself is a good story. |
| Uniform origin | Barroso: Crimea zouave from Rio | Kraay: never invoked in Bahia | Unknown; Kraay says he doesn't know. |
| Piribebuy duration | Centurión: 5 hours, 3 assaults | Imperial reports and Maestri: 15 to 45 minutes | Official and eyewitness evidence favors the short version. |
| Piribebuy hospital fire | Paraguayan tradition: d'Eu ordered it | Centurión: possibly the bombardment; one Paraguayan source blames a subordinate | Unresolved. |

| Cause of the war | Chiavenatto: British imperialism | Doratioto/Whigham: regional politics and ambitions | Mainstream rejects the conspiracy thesis. |

## 5. Hearsay / folk-legend bank (usable in the book as what someone says)

  - **"The Count ordered the hospital burned and prisoners beheaded"** \[Tradition\]. One Paraguayan source blames subordinate Vitorino Carneiro Monteiro and says the count was often away from the fighting. The exact words attributed to him are probably invented (Maestri).
  - **Acosta Ñu, the children's battle** \[Legend/propaganda\]: a bishop publicly stated about 3,000 children died and asked Brazil for forgiveness; a Substack piece says 3,000 children faced 20,000 soldiers. Unsourced. A Paraguayan historian says the battle was barely remembered for about thirty years and was recalled from about 1900.
  - **Marcolino on the wall** \[Tradition\]: exact words are folklore even though the deed was praised in orders of the day. An academic article on capoeira in the war says Querino spread narratives of dubious support. A general-audience article says he was freed in 1845 and decorated in 1869 with the Order of the Cruzeiro; Kraay does not mention this. Unverified.
  - **Dom Obá's "clean service record"** \[Legend\]: spread by folklorists, believed by his comrades in Rio.
  - **"Monkeys"** \[Propaganda\]: the Paraguayan insult for Brazilian soldiers.
  - **Soldiers' songs and looting** \[Documented in memoir\]: a camp song celebrating the sexual violence at Piribebuy (allude to it, don't quote). Champagne from López's cellar; Madame Lynch's silks.
  - **Zuavo hymn** \[Documented\]: Francisco Moniz Barreto wrote a hymn for the first company's embarkation; Querino recorded a slightly different version. Text in Kraay p. 136–137 and in O Alabama 1 March 1865.
  - **Henrique Dias myth** \[Documented rhetoric\]: speeches compared the zuavos to the 17th-century Black commander who fought the Dutch.

## 6. Family history (from the author)

  - **Paraguayan women fled to Brazil so their sons would not be sent to war** \[Tradition, family oral history\]. Consistent with documented patterns: Maestri says that in the Cordillera many men and women hid in the woods to avoid mobilization and sought imperial protection, with desertion rising from July 1869; a Paraguayan newspaper in 1870 worried that thousands of women were emigrating to Corrientes (summary, Argentina, after the war).
  - Timing matters: before 1869, crossing the lines was very dangerous; after Asunción fell in January 1869, it was more realistic.
  - Open question: when and where did she cross (Mato Grosso, the south, toward Corrientes)?
