# Paraguayan War (Guerra Grande): full research file for the book

*Compiled 2 October 2026 from the research chat. Reliability tags: \[DOCUMENTED\] = backed by scholarship or primary sources; \[CONTESTED\] = historians disagree; \[FOLKLORE\] = oral tradition or propaganda, no documentation found. Items marked (\*) come from general knowledge and were not verified in searches. Poems are linked, not reproduced.*

## 0. How to use this file in the book

  - This is a "truth ledger": it separates what is known from what is rumor, so the novel can blend history into the story without step-outs while you still know which parts are solid.
  - Low-reliability material (folk legends, propaganda, hearsay) is kept on purpose: it works well as things a character says, believes or repeats. Section 5 gives the main example.
  - Anything tagged \[DOCUMENTED\] can be narrated as fact; \[CONTESTED\] and \[FOLKLORE\] are better placed in characters' mouths.

## 1. The Brazilian side: the Zuavos baianos and Dom Obá II (from the source document "Dados gerais")

  - \[DOCUMENTED\] The Zuavos baianos were companies of Black volunteers from Bahia, dressed in uniforms inspired by the French colonial zouaves of Algeria. Quirino Antônio do Espírito Santo proposed raising them on 26 January 1865 and set up at Forte do Barbalho on 1 February. Eleven companies (638 men) and a company of "couraças" (80 men) embarked from Bahia before March 1866 (Hendrik Kraay).
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Two companies reached the theatre of war in May 1865 aboard the "São Francisco" (ex-"Cotopaxi"), with engineer André Rebouças, later a leading abolitionist.
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] The unit was unusual: the Army had been formally colour-blind since 1837 and the last segregated units were abolished in 1831. Why Bahia raised Black companies in 1865 is, in Kraay's words, still a mystery.
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Zuavos took part in the siege and surrender at Uruguaiana and in the capture of the Curuzu trenches. Capt. Marcolino reportedly climbed the enemy wall on a soldier's back, replaced a Paraguayan flag with the Brazilian one and announced himself as the "black Bahian zuavo" (as told by Manoel Querino; oral tradition recorded in the early 20th century).
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] The Conde d'Eu, in 1865, praised them as the finest troop in the army, composed entirely of Black men, officers included. In 1866 diplomat Francisco Otaviano celebrated Brazilians "white, black, mulatto or caboclo".
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Costs: old Independence veterans such as Quirino (died at Montevideo, Nov 1865) and Rocha Galvão (killed at Tuiuti, May 1866) did not survive; officers died of cholera, bomb splinters and disease. No ex-zuavo rose above captain. Capt. João Francisco Barbosa de Oliveira served the whole campaign, was wounded twice and was among the troops who killed López at Aquidabã (his own post-war petition). Cadet Constantino Luiz Xavier Bigode was captured after Curupaytí and spent more than two years as a prisoner working at the Ybycuí foundry. José Soares Cupim Júnior was wounded three times and died on 13 January 1869; his widow received a pension of 720 mil-réis a year from 1871. Parliament granted widow pensions for four other zuavo officers.
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] After the war the veterans returned to the free poor population of colour they had come from; in Salvador the zuavos were remembered through oral tradition.
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] **Dom Obá II (Cândido da Fonseca Galvão)**: born c. 1845 in Lençóis, Bahia, to Benvindo da Fonseca Galvão, an African freedman of the Yoruba nation; served as a zuavo and held the rank of alferes; moved to Rio in the 1870s; reverenced as a prince by Afro-Brazilians in the suburbs; wrote articles in the court newspapers defending the monarchy and attacking slavery and racial discrimination; dressed in a gala uniform, top hat and white gloves; lost his rank after the Republic in 1889 and died in July 1890.
  - Sources in that document: Hendrik Kraay, "Os companheiros de Dom Obá"; Claudio Moreira Bento, *O Exército e a Abolição*; COPIR Uruguaiana page on Cândido da Fonseca Galvão.

## 2. How Paraguayans have understood the war: three schools

  - **Liberal / Legionnaire view (c. 1870 to the 1920s).** Blames Francisco Solano López alone: an expansionist tyrant who destroyed his country. Rooted in the Allied theses and the exiled "Paraguayan Legion" that fought with the Allies.
  - **Lopizta / nationalist view (from the 1920s).** A strong early reaction; Juan E. O'Leary (1879-1969) is considered the initiator of the "positive lopizta" historiography. López as a patriot defending sovereignty. Hardened after the Chaco War (1932-35); made the official story under Stroessner (1954-89). 1 March (López's death at Cerro Corá, 1870) became Heroes' Day (\*).
  - **Revisionist / dependency view (from the 1960s).** Boosted by León Pomer, *La guerra del Paraguay: ¡Gran negocio!* (1968). Argues British economic interests worked through Brazil and Argentina to destroy a state-led, independent Paraguay. \[CONTESTED\] Largely rejected by specialists today.

That López attacked first is not disputed. What is disputed is whether it was defensive (nationalist) or a reckless gamble (liberal and most modern academic views).

## 3. Origins (as Paraguayans tell it)

  - Isolated, self-sufficient state since Dr. Francia (1814-40) and Carlos Antonio López (1844-62): state estancias, iron foundry at Ybycuí, railway, telegraph, high literacy for the region.
  - River problem: the Paraguay and Paraná rivers were the only route to the world, with the outlet controlled by Brazil and Argentina.
  - Border disputes with Brazil (Mato Grosso) and Argentina (Misiones).
  - Uruguay: Brazil intervened in the civil war (Blancos vs. Colorados) in 1864. López issued an ultimatum to Brazil on 30 August 1864; Brazilian troops invaded Uruguay in October; López declared war and invaded Mato Grosso in November; he declared war on Argentina in March 1865 after being refused passage through Misiones.
  - López hoped for support from Urquiza (governor of Entre Ríos) and overrated Argentina's internal divisions. His actions produced the very alliance he feared.
  - The Treaty of the Triple Alliance (May 1865) had a secret clause on dismembering Paraguay; once revealed it became central to Paraguayan memory.

## 4. During the war and after

### The war (Paraguayans call it Guerra Grande)

  - 1864-65: Paraguayan advances (Mato Grosso, Corrientes); loss of the fleet at Riachuelo (June 1865); surrender at Uruguaiana.
  - 1866-68: Curupaytí (Paraguayan victory), long siege of Humaitá (fell 1868).
  - 1868-70: retreat; Lomas Valentinas; Asunción occupied and sacked from 1 January 1869; Acosta Ñu (16 August 1869); López killed at Cerro Corá on 1 March 1870.
  - Acosta Ñu: about 3,500 poorly armed Paraguayans, mostly boys aged nine to fifteen, plus old men and the wounded, faced about 20,000 Brazilian and Argentine veterans. \[CONTESTED\] Some scholars (e.g. Julio José Chiavenatto) accuse the Conde d'Eu of ordering fires that killed fallen soldiers, including a field hospital.
  - Women ("residentas") kept farms and supply going and followed the army; Eliza Lynch led a group of them.
  - López's terror: executions of real or imagined conspirators, including his own brothers, and torture of his mother and sisters when he suspected opposition. Slaves were conscripted and units of children appeared.

### Demographics

  - \[CONTESTED\] Older figure: about 60% of the population died (Whigham and Potthast 1999); some historians say up to 90% of men.
  - \[CONTESTED\] Revision: a peer-reviewed study (Reber, *Hispanic American Historical Review* 68:2, 1988) estimates the war cost 8.7 to 18.5 percent of the pre-war population. Present the figures as a range.
  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Postwar census (via Potthast-Jutkeit 1991): 221,079 people remained: 106,254 women, 86,079 children, 28,746 men. Sex ratio estimates for the period: Washburn 7 women per man; Potthast 4; Ganson 3. By the 1886 census, 1.4 women per man.

### Consequences

  - **Short term:** Brazilian occupation of Asunción until 1876; Argentine-Brazilian rivalry over Paraguay; territorial losses (about 40,000 km2 in the 1872-76 treaties (\*)); debt; sale of state lands (\*); many women turned to prostitution (an unfinished theatre was converted to a brothel where 400 women worked and lived).
  - **Long term:** the Colorado and Liberal parties (1887 (\*)); the war as foundational national trauma ("el naufragio nacional"); the Chaco War as "redemption" (\*); persistent resentment of Brazil and Argentina; academic work linking male scarcity to later social patterns.

## 5. Family tradition: rape by Brazilian soldiers and Black children after the war

**Tag: \[FOLKLORE\]. No documentation found.** No record turned up of systematic rape by Black or formerly enslaved Brazilian soldiers, or of a wave of Black children.

  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Brazilian forces occupied Asunción from 1 January 1869 until 1876; Argentine, Uruguayan and Paraguayan Legion troops were there too; many Brazilian soldiers were freed slaves; extreme shortage of men; widespread prostitution; looting of Asunción.
  - Wartime sexual violence probably happened (it does in most wars) but is poorly recorded; women's voices are scarce in the record. Absence of records is not proof it didn't happen.
  - Paraguayan wartime propaganda racialized the enemy (section 6), so a story of Black soldiers assaulting Paraguayan women fits that propaganda.
  - Paraguay already had an Afro-descendant population before the war (colonial slaves and free pardos; Afro-Paraguayan communities such as Kamba Kuá (\*)), so mixed-race children after 1870 cannot automatically be traced to the occupation.
  - War memory in Paraguay focuses on battlefield atrocities (e.g. Acosta Ñu), not on rape by occupiers.
  - **For the story:** this works as something a character says or believes (a rumor told by a Paraguayan, or repeated by a Brazilian soldier's neighbor). The poem "Nenia" (section 9) shows the same stereotype already in a published Argentine poem.
  - **If you ever want to test it:** parish baptism registers (1870-76) in Asunción and nearby towns; Archivo Nacional de Asunción; Brazilian army discipline files (Arquivo Histórico do Exército, Arquivo Nacional); photo albums *Lembranças do Paraguay* and *Excursões ao Paraguay* at the Biblioteca Nacional, Rio.

## 6. Racist propaganda in the Paraguayan wartime press

  - \[DOCUMENTED\] Three government papers: **El Centinela** (Asunción, 25 April 1867 to 10 February 1868, 63 issues; facsimile reissue 1964), **Cabichuí** (from 13 May 1867, printed at the front, roughly 95 issues) and **Cacique Lambaré** (in Guaraní).
  - Cabichuí: each issue carried a caricature of wasps stinging a black soldier; the racial makeup of the Brazilian army was a major theme; Pedro II and Brazilian soldiers were frequently drawn as monkeys. Brazilians were constantly called "macacos".
  - El Centinela called the three Allies "monkey, dog and cat" and referred to Brazilians as "worms", "macacos" and similar, and called the Brazilian army effeminate.
  - Contributors included Col. Juan Crisóstomo Centurión, and López himself wrote for Cabichuí, so this was state propaganda.
  - Article on the images (Portuguese): "O gênero da nação nas gravuras da imprensa de guerra paraguaia: Cabichuí e El Centinela, 1867-1868" (Dialnet).
  - The Argentine side also had anti-Brazilian racism (an Argentine paper depicted Brazilians as monkeys in 1920), but war-era Argentine papers were not researched.

## 7. Memoirs and primary sources

  - Juan Crisóstomo Centurión, *Memorias o reminiscencias históricas sobre la guerra del Paraguay* (\*). Participant and propagandist. Chapter on Cerro Corá online at Portal Guaraní: https://www.portalguarani.com/373_juan_crisostomo_centurion__/13110_cerro_cora_autor_juan_crisostomo_centurion_.html

  - Francisco Isidoro Resquín, *Datos históricos de la guerra del Paraguay contra la Triple Alianza*. The only Paraguayan general who left written testimony. Centurión said his account of Cerro Corá is inaccurate.
  - Eliza Lynch's memoirs; George Thompson's account (English engineer in the Paraguayan army) (\*).
  - El Centinela collection (Portal Guaraní): https://www.portalguarani.com/993_periodico_de_guerra_el_centinela/9066_el_centinela_coleccion_del_semanario_de_los_paraguayos_en_la_guerra_de_la_triple_alianza__1867_.html

## 9. Poems: where to read or buy them

One edition glosses "cambá" as "los negros", and the poem says the fiancé was killed by the cambá. Directly relevant to the family story because it casts Black Brazilian soldiers as killers.

## 10. Story threads and open research

  - Contrast: Paraguay conscripted and freed slaves; Brazil's Black soldiers (including the Zuavos baianos) served an empire that still held slavery.
  - Contrast Paraguayan "macaco" propaganda with Brazilian praise of the Zuavos and the later neglect (no ex-zuavo above captain; widows' pensions).
  - Voices to build characters from: the residentas, the boy soldiers of Acosta Ñu, the occupying soldiers, a Bahian veteran back in a poor neighborhood, a Paraguayan woman repeating the "cambá" rumor.
  - Still to research: women's testimonies (Potthast, Ganson); Afro-Paraguayan communities (Kamba Kuá) and what already existed before the war; Talavera's verses; Guaraní-language war songs; war-era Argentine press; the timeline of slavery in Brazil and what happened to slaves during and after the war.
