## Prologue — The Fog

<!-- 00.001 -->
My grandmother died after years with Alzheimer's. All her life, small memories slipped out of her "in between conversations": distant memories handed down from earlier generations, or lived by herself. With the disease, the forgetting came first. Then, little by little, things turned around: forgetting became the rule, and lucidity the exception. Those were the saddest moments. In one of them, somewhere between lucidity and the fog, she held my aunt's hand and said: "My mind is not working properly. Will you please take care of me?"

<!-- 00.002 -->
Her life was hard from the start. In her stories there was a river, wide and deep, and a fragile canoe that took her to school. Then came the animals: jaguars, caimans, snakes. About her father she said less. The two never got along, and she left home very young. "Moça de família só saía de casa casada," people said back then; a girl from a good family left her father's house married, or not at all. She left without marrying. She went to live in other people's houses, and nobody in the family can say whose. She was completely alone. I never asked how old she was.

<!-- 00.003 -->
She became a nursing assistant, got married and had four children: one boy and three girls. She was not the most affectionate mother. My mom, my aunts and my uncle carry many bad memories of growing up with her. She was not easy. She could be really mean. She had learned that somewhere.

<!-- 00.004 -->
I saw some of it with my own eyes. Her repeated accusations that someone had stolen her things were sometimes so absurd they seemed funny. The best of all: someone had stolen her underwear.

<!-- 00.005 -->
The fear of being robbed was strong. When I was a kid, she kept a shotgun in her bedroom. More than once she heard a noise on the roof and fired into the air. Probably a cat, or a rat; we will never know. But the shots worked. The noise stopped. At least for that day.

<!-- 00.006 -->
In her last years she hardly spoke. The disease took her memories and, along with them, everything she was.

<!-- 00.007 -->
As I write, my grandfather, her former husband and the father of three of her children, has the disease too.

<!-- 00.008 -->
The fog around memory seems to haunt this family. But not only because of the disease.

<!-- 00.009 -->
While she was still lucid, my grandmother told stories about her grandfather, who had been a slave. Nobody remembers his name. We tried to check those stories, but it was already too late: many of her siblings had died, many documents had been lost. In the end, it wasn't Alzheimer's that erased the memory of an entire generation of my family. In our reality, memory was never really valued. Surviving came first. Remembering was left for later, and later hardly ever came.

<!-- 00.010 -->
I'm here to change that.

## PART I — BAHIA (1846–1865)

### 1. Good Teeth

<!-- 01.001 -->
They landed at night, on a beach, far from the harbour. Nobody lit a lamp. Men whispered in a language she did not have yet, and walked them through sand and thorn to a house, and from the house, two nights later, to this warehouse. Nobody wrote anything down. Nobody asked her name.

<!-- 01.002 -->
Vinegar at dawn. They threw it on the line by the bucket, like washing down a floor. It got into the cuts the irons had made, and it burned, and nobody screamed, because screaming had stopped working on the ship.

<!-- 01.003 -->
The warehouse stank. Tar. Salt fish. Piss in the corners. Two hundred bodies' worth of sweat soaked into the planks, and the heat cooked it all together until the air was something you chewed.

<!-- 01.004 -->
The buyers came in at noon. They walked the line slowly, hands behind their backs, the way men walk past horses they are not sure about. One poked a boy's belly with his cane. One lifted a woman's arm and let it drop. They talked over the captives' heads, and laughed, and spat on the floor.

<!-- 01.005 -->
The old man came last, and the smell got worse. Wet tobacco. Last night's rum, still coming out through his skin. Rotten teeth. His shirt had been white once. He stopped in front of Luzia and took her jaw in his hand like it was his.

<!-- 01.006 -->
His thumb went in her mouth. Over the top teeth. Over the bottom. Slow, like a man counting coins. It tasted of everything he was.

<!-- 01.007 -->
"Good teeth," he said.

<!-- 01.008 -->
"All of them," said the dealer. "Count them.”

<!-- 01.009 -->
"I'm counting.”

<!-- 01.010 -->
He was done counting. The hand did not leave. It slid to the side of her neck and stayed there, warm, damp, heavy, while he looked at her like something he had already decided to buy. His breath came into her face in waves. She had nowhere to turn. She did not try.

<!-- 01.011 -->
She did not understand the words. She understood the hand.

<!-- 01.012 -->
The light came through a crack between two planks, as thin as a blade. Dust turned in it, never landing. She starred there the entire time. She was tired, more tired than afraid. Thirty-some days in the dark of the ship, and now this. She wanted only for it to be over. Whatever it was. Let it be over.

<!-- 01.013 -->
"Fourteen," the old man said, pinching the skin of her arm the way you test fruit. "Fifteen at most.”

<!-- 01.014 -->
"Fourteen is good," said the dealer. "Fourteen lasts.”

<!-- 01.015 -->
"Fourteen eats.”

<!-- 01.016 -->
The dealer did not laugh. He was looking at her now too. Not at her teeth. At her face. He looked a long time, the way a man looks at a coin that might be gold. Then he said one word to the boy at the door, and the boy took her by the wrist and pulled her out of the line, and the old man's hand fell off her like a dead fish slipping back into the water.

<!-- 01.017 -->
Behind the warehouse, in a yard full of flies, two women washed her again. Not with vinegar this time. With water, and soap that smelled of ash, and a rough cloth that took the ship off her skin in grey rolls. They put a clean white cloth on her, and tied up her hair, and rubbed oil on her arms until they shone. Nobody told her why. One of the women, older, with a face like dried leather, said something softly in a language Luzia almost knew. It might have been a prayer. It might have been: poor thing.

<!-- 01.018 -->
When they brought her back, the dealer wiped the board with his sleeve and chalked a new number. Bigger. The old man saw it, and swore, and said a number of his own. The dealer shook his head. The old man said another. The dealer shook his head again, smiling now, as if they were old friends playing a game.

<!-- 01.019 -->
Then a third man spoke from the door. Thin, quiet, hat in his hands. He had not walked the line. He said one number and did not look at her once.

<!-- 01.020 -->
The dealer drew a line under it.

<!-- 01.021 -->
That was all. After so many days, it was very quick. The old man spat, and went back down the line, to the others. The thin man paid. Someone took her by the arm and walked her out through a door into a light so white that for a moment she saw nothing at all. Then the city came up out of the white: houses climbing a cliff, one on top of another, walls and roofs and towers, and down at the bottom of everything, a bay so bright it hurt.

<!-- 01.022 -->
She kept her face still. She did not look back at the line. She was fourteen, and tired, and she wanted only for the day to end. It would not end for a long time.

## Epilogue — What Remains

<!-- 99.001 -->
Grandma died in 2023, after suffering from Alzheimer's for many years. We don't know the last time she told this story. We only know that we were too busy trying to make a living, and did not ask more questions while we still could.
