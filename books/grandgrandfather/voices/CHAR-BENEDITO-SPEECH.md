## Who and when

How he talks in every chapter where he speaks (ch. 2–17), and in his quoted thoughts. His name (working name Benedito) never appears in the text. The narration around his lines stays in the chapter's narrator voice (NARR-MAIN). Character Bible: "Benedito, the man without a name".

## Portrait

  - **Portuguese is his own language.** Brazil-born (*crioulo*) on a sugar estate in the Recôncavo; he speaks the plain Portuguese of the enslaved there. Yoruba reaches him only through Luzia's songs and prayers, which he hums without understanding them all. *(Bible)*
  - **The masters' words too.** He grew up beside Tonico, the master's son, and knows many words and forms of the lords' world: polite formulas, church words, the way a senhor gives an order. As a boy he mimics them for fun; as a man he can use them coldly, with the whites, when it serves him. *(Rafael, 2026-11-03)*
  - **Few words.** Watchful, quiet, proud in a way he learned to hide; funny only with a few friends (Bastião, the roda), cold with everyone else. *(Bible; the short turns are inference)*
  - **Anger in one line.** Explosive when disrespected ("moleque" is enough); not angry day to day. *(Bible)*
  - **Reading widens his words.** He learns to read in the war from Dom Obá's newspapers. *(Bible; the newspaper and army words are inference)*
  - **No answer to Latin.** Rich, he gives orders, but loses every argument to Carmen's quotes; his only answers are Luzia's sayings and Kalunga's jokes, which she now calls senzala talk. *(Bible, ch. 16)*
  - **Old age** loosens his speech: memory fails, never named as a disease. *(Bible, ch. 17)*
  - **Recognisable in one line**: few words, a saying of Luzia's at the right moment, and numbers (he counts things; he says healing steps aloud, as his mother did).

## Author's choices

  - **How distinct**: clearly recognisable, not performed.
  - **Language**: fluent plain Bahian Portuguese; Yoruba only for the sacred (songs, prayers, a cry under shock). He also knows many words of the lords from his childhood with Tonico.
  - **Figures of speech**: from work, the body and luck.
  - **Change over time**: his speech follows his life (boy → youth → soldier → poor free man → senhor → old man). Each chapter row below gives offsets from the base; a chapter without a row uses the nearest earlier row (changes persist).

## Key ideas

  - **What makes him recognisable**
      - Says less than he knows; the silence after his line is part of it.
      - Concrete: counts, measures, names the thing (heads of cattle, days, coins, steps).
      - One saying of Luzia's or Kalunga's at the right moment, never two in a scene.
      - When he wants to wound or to win, he switches to the lords' polite words, coldly.
  - **Figures of speech** (work, body, luck): the mill, the cane, the cattle, the body that works or breaks, cards and dice. Samples (original): "A wound closes from the bottom, not the top." "Cane doesn't care who cuts it." "Luck is a horse nobody owns."
  - **Signature words and phrases**: Luzia's sayings, rationed (once in a chapter at most); "Luck will open the way" (his version of her "The orixás will open the way"; see the inheritance line in the Bible) in his youth and at the lottery, never again after ch. 16; numbers said aloud. Yoruba only sacred: "Ògún yè!" under his breath at Tuiuti, her song "Atotô" over the sick.
  - **Forms of address**
      - As a boy and youth: *sinhô*, *sinhá*, *nhonhô* or *sinhozinho* (to Tonico in front of others), *vosmecê* to whites; to Tonico alone, his name; Velho Kalunga as *mestre*; Luzia as *mãe*.
      - In the war: *sargento*, *capitão*; comrades by nickname; he is "Zuavo".
      - Rich: wants to be called *senhor* and is; calls the Pires "Coronel" and "Dona"; calls his sons by name, never "moleque"; Carmen by no name in the text (see the Bible's naming rule).
  - **Manners and tics**: a pause before answering; looks at teeth, hands and hooves before he speaks of a man or an animal; says healing steps aloud while he works; hums when he cannot speak.
  - **Never says**: his own name (the reader never hears it); Acosta Ñu, in any chapter; that he loves anyone; why he beat Afonso at São João; "moleque" to anyone; an apology to a white man.
  - **Under pressure**: shorter, then silent; counts under his breath; a Yoruba cry in mortal danger; anger is one cold line followed by action, never a tirade. Lying: more words than usual, and the lords' polite forms.

## Language layer: PT-BR

  - Recôncavo popular speech of the enslaved and freed, carried in syntax and word choice, never in spelling: *a gente* for "we", short coordinated clauses, simple tenses, popular agreement in his youth (*os boi*, *nós fica*) that thins after the war, when he reads.
  - Period forms of address kept as they were: *sinhô*, *sinhá*, *nhonhô*, *vosmecê*, *senhor*; *moleque* as the insult it was.
  - The lords' register, when he uses it, is correct and slightly stiff: *Pois não, Coronel. Vossa mercê há de me desculpar.*
  - Samples (original): "A gente conta o gado duas vez e ainda falta." (youth/early) · "Quarenta e três cabeças. Ontem eram quarenta e quatro." (rich) · "Ferida fecha de baixo pra cima."
  - To verify: which popular Bahian forms of the 1850s–80s are documented (avoid later regionalisms such as *oxente* until checked).

## Language layer: EN

  - Plain, short English; no dialect spelling. His background shows in rhythm and word choice: simple clauses joined with "and", concrete nouns, few abstract words, the occasional dropped subject ("Counted them twice. Still one short.").
  - Portuguese forms of address stay untranslated (*sinhô*, *sinhá*, *nhonhô*, *senhor*, *moleque*), made clear by context, as agreed in the Bilingual Lexicon.
  - The lords' register in English: correct, formal, a little old-fashioned ("If the Colonel will permit me.").
  - Samples (original): "A wound closes from the bottom." · "Forty-three head. Yesterday it was forty-four." · "Luck will open the way, mãe."

## Neighbour voices

  - **Bastião**: also from the estate and the roda, but loud, a storyteller, jokes as weapons; Benedito answers him in few words and laughs.
  - **Tonico**: the lords' speech that Benedito knows from inside; Tonico says more and decides less.
  - **Luzia**: her sayings and songs live in his mouth, but rationed; she sings and talks freely, he does not.
  - **Carmen**: a learner of Portuguese who grows posh and quotes the Pires; he is fluent and plain. Their arguments: her long quoted sentences against his short concrete lines.
  - **The Pires**: elaborate, Latin, French; he answers with cold polite forms or one plain line.
