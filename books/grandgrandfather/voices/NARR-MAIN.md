# NARR-MAIN: Main narrator

  - **Type**: Narrator
  - **Version**: 0.1 (draft) | **Status**: draft | **Created**: 2026-10-10
  - **Based on**: blend of Itamar Vieira Junior, Gabriel García Márquez and Quentin Tarantino, chosen by Rafael on 2026-10-10

## Who and when

The voice that tells the 1800s story itself, in scenes: close third person, following Luzia in chapter 1, then him, then the woman from Spain in her chapters. Used for all chapters of the main story. Not used for the prologue, epilogue or author passages (AUTHOR-MAIN), letters (letter voices) or documents.

## Tags

  - Narrative person: 3rd, close (one person per scene)
  - Addressed to: no one
  - Form: plain narration, scene-led
  - Literacy: raw and vernacular. Plain, everyday, physical words; crude when the scene is crude. Not "educated". Characters' own literacy shows only in their dialogue, through their voices.

## Literacy profile

The narrator speaks plainly, close to how the people of the story would speak, never like a scholar. Short words, short sentences, fragments. No academic or modern words. Period and regional words where they belong (senzala, engenho, jornal, orixá). Same level in both languages.

## Reference authors (core)

  - Itamar Vieira Junior, *Torto Arado*: 45% (close, sensory, rooted in the land and the body)
  - Gabriel García Márquez, *One Hundred Years of Solitude*: 30% (chance and fate, deadpan irony, the rare glance across years)
  - Quentin Tarantino (films and screenplays): 25% (holds the camera where others cut away; loaded, menacing dialogue with dark jokes; slow build, sudden release; no moralizing). His cathartic release is used only at the catharsis points listed below.

## Voice rules

1.  **Short and plain.** Simple sentences, fragments, everyday words. Do: "The warehouse stank. Tar. Salt fish. Piss in the corners." Don't: "The warehouse was redolent of a variety of maritime odours."
2.  **One person's skin.** We know only what the point-of-view character sees, hears, smells and guesses. Do: "She did not understand the words. She understood the hand." Don't: explain what the buyer intended.
3.  **The place is in every scene.** Dirt, smell, heat, bodies, weather, the sound of the mill. Do: "Two hundred bodies' worth of sweat soaked into the planks." Don't: a scene that could happen anywhere.
4.  **History through objects and actions, never explained.** Do: the dealer writes nothing down. Don't: "Since the law of 1831 banned the trade..."
5.  **Dialogue short and loaded.** Menace and dark jokes, no speeches. Do: "Good teeth." "All of them. Count them." "I'm counting." Don't: a character explaining the slave trade.
6.  **Chance runs things; the glance ahead is rare.** At most one look across years per scene, to show fate or irony, never to spoil the plot. Do: "It would not end for a long time." Don't: "She did not know then that she would die in Vassouras."
7.  **No verdicts.** Cruelty and kindness come with their reasons; the narrator judges no one. Do: the dealer's hurry, shown. Don't: "the cruel dealer".
8.  **Never name him or the woman from Spain** (shared rule).

## Conveying feeling

How this voice makes the reader feel what each scene's Reader feeling (in Scenes) asks for.

1.  **Body first.** Feeling arrives through smell, taste, dirt and touch, never named. Do: "His thumb tasted of everything he was." Don't: "She felt disgusted."
2.  **Hold the camera; the door stays closed.** At the worst moment, do not cut away and do not comment: short plain sentences, one beat longer than is comfortable; one short line marks the turn. Sexual violence is never shown in the room. It may reach the reader from outside: what others hear through the walls, what they see before and after, what the morning shows; the camera stays with the listeners. This outside view is never used when the victim is a child: then the book cuts away completely. Do: "From the senzala they could hear her through the boards, and then the other noises, and nobody slept." Don't: anything inside the room.
3.  **Dark humour as pressure.** A deadpan line or absurd detail next to the horror, never mocking the victim. Do: the haggling over her like over a horse. Don't: a joke at her pain.
4.  **Catharsis is rationed.** The Tarantino release (the worst turns on someone who earned it, and the camera stays to watch) happens only at the catharsis points marked in Chapters. Everywhere else violence lands with no release. Even at those points, the one who wins keeps his contradictions and pays a cost.

Catharsis points (Chapters, Oct 10, 2026; to revisit): ch. 3, he beats Ezequiel; Interlude, Tonico banishes Ezequiel with the urine letter; ch. 6, Tuiuti (the zouaves hold in the mud) and Curuzu (Marcolino plants the flag from his back); ch. 10, he stops Damião; ch. 20, he bursts into Albino's house.

## Language layer: EN

  - **References**: Vieira Junior, *Crooked Plow*, tr. Johnny Lorenz 40%; García Márquez, *One Hundred Years of Solitude*, tr. Gregory Rabassa 30%; Tarantino's screenplays (EN original) 30%.
  - **Offsets**: none.
  - **Vocabulary**: short Anglo-Saxon words; crude words when the scene is crude (piss, stink); no modern slang, no academic words. Kept in Portuguese and made clear by context: senzala, engenho, jornal, ganhadeira, orixá, capitão do mato, Sinhô/Sinhá. Rum or cachaça: to decide in 00 Bilingual Lexicon.
  - **Phrase-building**: fragments; "and" chains for momentum ("and it burned, and nobody screamed"); few semicolons; verbs of the body.
  - **Address and dialogue**: double quotation marks; forms of address kept in Portuguese where they carry rank (Sinhô, Sinhá).
  - **Literacy markers**: the narration stays plain; characters' schooling shows only in their own lines.
  - **Language rules**:
    1.  Prefer the short word. Do: "stank". Don't: "emitted an odour".
    2.  A Portuguese word stays Portuguese when English has no true equivalent, and the next sentence makes it clear. Do: "Every evening she paid the jornal: the coins the widow was owed for the day." Don't: a footnote or "which means".
  - **Reference paragraph (EN)**:

Vinegar at dawn. They threw it on the line by the bucket, like washing down a floor. It got into the cuts the irons had made, and it burned, and nobody screamed, because screaming had stopped working on the ship. The warehouse stank. Tar. Salt fish. Piss in the corners. Two hundred bodies' worth of sweat soaked into the planks. The old man came in at noon and the smell got worse. Wet tobacco. Last night's rum, still coming out through his skin. Rotten teeth. He stopped in front of Luzia and took her jaw in his hand like it was his. His thumb went in. Over the top teeth. Over the bottom. It tasted of everything he was. "Good teeth," he said. "All of them," said the dealer. "Count them." "I'm counting." He was done counting. The hand did not leave. It slid to the side of her neck and stayed there, warm, damp, heavy, while he looked at her like something he had already decided to buy. She did not understand the words. She understood the hand. The light came through the crack between two planks as thin as a blade. Dust turned in it, never landing. She put herself there.
