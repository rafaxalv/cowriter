### 3\. The Roda

  - When / where: 1858–1865, age 12–19 · the estate, Cachoeira · his youth and growth, capoeira, the rivalry with Ezequiel grown up and sour, up to the fight at the festa; then the war (merged with The Bargain, Oct 27, 2026)
  - Readiness: 76/100 · beats 4 · arc 4 · links 5 · facts 4 · decisions 4 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **The shape of the chapter.** After his father dies in his hands (ch. 2) and his mother is sold, he wants one thing: freedom, somehow. He becomes a fighter: he grows strong, picks fights he could lose, and throws himself into the roda (decided Oct 27, 2026). He finds his people: the capoeira boys, misfits who have to hide their skills and train in secret, and the terreiro, a faith to follow. This is the part of his life when he is closest to his African community: the roda, the terreiro, the songs Luzia left him. Ezequiel is the bully, with a few friends of his own, and whatever he does goes unpunished; when it does, Benedito blames Tonico for doing nothing, like always. Underdogs against the bully. Over the years his skill grows: the roda is where his force turns into play. With each of Kalunga's lessons his anger slowly decreases, but it is always there; it comes back after the war (ch. 10) (decided Nov 1, 2026). The chapter ends with the first real door to freedom he has ever seen: the war.
      - **1858. Everything changes at once** (decided Oct 30, 2026). His mother, Luzia, is sold south (ch. 2). The friendship with Tonico (Antônio de Menezes, the master's son, his age, his milk brother) goes cold, and the protection goes with it. Tonico is rarely seen now: he works with his father. No one asks for Benedito to be released any more, and the work becomes heavy: full days in the cane and the boiling house, like everyone else.
      - **c. 1858. Ezequiel's reward** (continues "After that night", ch. 2). Ezequiel: pardo, born enslaved on the estate c. 1834, twelve years older than Benedito; the master's unacknowledged son and so Tonico's half-brother, which only Ezequiel knows (he learned it at 12–13 from the goiabeira, ch. 2); as a boy he trained briefly with Velho Kalunga, who sent him away for his pride and violence (ch. 2); then the master's informer and the overseer's helper. He gets rewards for it – in the end his freedom: c. 1858, at about 24, the landlord frees him and makes him capitão do mato – but these are all ways the landlord manipulates him into doing what he wants. Never the word "son". This path takes him into becoming a violent and proud capitão do mato.
      - **c. 1858–59. The first whipping** (soon after he becomes capitão do mato; Ezequiel's point of view). Once he becomes capitão do mato there is his most cathartic moment. He catches a negro stealing. The negro refuses to say anything. He keeps screaming for him to confess. He remains silent. He slaps him and screams. Nothing. Silence. He threatens him with the whip. Nothing. He takes him to the tree and ties him, and gives his last warning. He is tense, a bit excited, but mostly angry: it is the first time he has to show his authority, to finally show he is special and not the same. He whips the negro on the hand, and the negro finally makes a noise. The noise hits his brain back in time, to when the landlord slapped his mother in that room while he watched from the goiabeira. His whole body gets goosebumps. He hits again: a moan, stronger goosebumps. He doesn't understand and doesn't care. The more he whips, the more excited he gets, like never before. He whips harder every time. He remembers that room. But he is the landlord now. He whips. He only stops when someone holds him. He is wet in sweat. He didn't even notice how many times he whipped. He doesn't know how much time passed. He gets out of the trance. He feels his pants tight around the crotch. His hands hold the whip with extreme strength. His whole body is then released. Someone holds him and says: "Stop, he won't be able to work if you continue." (Someone: unnamed – "Not important. The focus is Ezequiel.") He looks at the negro tied to the tree. He whipped his arms the whole time. His hand was almost detached. The negro had an amputation later on. Ezequiel was now the most brutal capitão do mato. Everyone feared him, especially because he seemed to enjoy the process. Written explicitly, following Rafael's words. Pays off in the Interlude: the man with the amputated hand never gets over his fear of Ezequiel; years later Ezequiel uses it to find Kalunga's hideout (revised Nov 2, 2026). Benedito, about 13, does not watch: he only hears of it, first from the senzala, then as Kalunga's joke (decided Nov 1, 2026).
      - **c. 1858–59. The hunt begins** (decided Oct 30, 2026). As capitão do mato, Ezequiel starts to hunt Velho Kalunga, the man who sent him away as a boy (ch. 2). Kalunga is always smarter and stronger: he slips away every time, and sometimes makes a fool of him. But Kalunga is old now, not as strong as when he sent Ezequiel away, and every escape is harder: the secret rodas become more dangerous.
      - **c. 1858. Bastião reaches out** (decided Oct 30, 2026). Bastião: enslaved on the estate, two or three years older than Benedito; quick, funny, a born storyteller; already one of Velho Kalunga's students. As boys they knew each other but were not close, and Bastião teased him as the master's son's pet, "the milk brother" (ch. 2). Now he sees the anger in Benedito when his mother is sold, and understands he can help; he is the one who reaches out first, still teasing ("the master's pet has lost his master"), but now as a friend. Through Bastião, Benedito enters a world he barely knew while he was at Tonico's side: the senzala's own community, its secrets, the roda, the terreiro, Velho Kalunga. As he grows, his bonds with the other enslaved grow with him.
      - **c. 1859. Bastião takes him to Velho Kalunga** (decided Oct 30, 2026). Velho Kalunga: an old capoeirista, a Black man who escaped a farm long ago (nobody knows which, or when); he lives free in the woods and the margins and goes from place to place helping others train in secret. The boys tell legends about him, half as jokes: that he killed a capitão do mato with one kick, that the dogs never find his trail, that he was a soldier, a prince in Africa, a ghost. He neither confirms nor denies, and mostly laughs. He is a funny master, not a solemn one: he jokes all the time, teases his students, makes the hard things look easy. He teaches everything while telling jokes: every sentence is a joke and a lesson (decided Nov 1, 2026). Sometimes spicy and ironic, jokes about inequality, about slaves and owners, about the unfairness of life; sometimes deeply philosophical; sometimes as silly as a joke about a fart, or about Ezequiel's hard dick. His lessons come in small pieces, one training session at a time (below), and Benedito learns slowly. One night Bastião brings Benedito to a clearing in the woods to train with them. Kalunga's clan has one rule: only those who have proven themselves worthy belong, and no one outside the clan may ever come to the hideout. Benedito has to earn his place. A culture shock: the boy who spent his childhood playing with the white boy knows none of the roda's traditions, its songs, its gestures, its manners; he stands outside the circle like a stranger. Bastião teases him for it, the master's pet come to play with the senzala, and the others laugh; there is real tension between them at first. Batuques and samba de roda too: they happen on Sundays and holy days in the senzala yard, with the master's or the overseer's leave; the capoeira does not ask leave. They train at night, hidden: a gathering of young men fighting after dark looks like a plan to run or rise, and Ezequiel has every reason, and every right, to punish it.
      - **c. 1859. Kalunga's joke** (decided Oct 30, 2026). At a roda after the first whipping, between jokes, with Benedito and Bastião there, Velho Kalunga says Ezequiel's dick was harder than the tree the negro was tied to. Written explicitly, following Rafael's words. Everyone laughs, but it is not only a joke: Kalunga taught Ezequiel, sent him away, and knows what he saw in that boy. Laughing at the whip is his kind of resistance: no one can fight the capitão do mato in daylight, but they can make him ridiculous.
      - **c. 1859–60. The joke spreads** (decided Oct 30, 2026). From the roda to the senzala, the other estates, the market and the rodas of Cachoeira: nobody knows who said it first, and everybody tells it. It becomes an improvised verse in the capoeira songs (mocking verses were part of the roda). Ezequiel hears it. He cannot whip a joke; he guesses where it came from, and the hunt for Kalunga becomes personal.
      - **How the lessons are told** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Kalunga's five lessons below are not five scenes. They come mixed into two or three roda nights, inside his jokes, never named or numbered on the page; the festa fight shows what Benedito has absorbed. The lesson notes below are for the writer.
      - **c. 1859–60. The doubt.** Benedito has heard the legends and expects a fighter. What he finds is an old man who jokes all the time and dances: he does not look like a master, or a fighter at all, and that is why Benedito does not believe in him at first. Kalunga teaches almost nothing but the ginga, the rhythm and the songs: to Benedito it feels like dancing lessons, too much ginga, too few kicks and punches. Among other things he would like to land the rabo de arraia (the spinning heel kick with the hands on the ground; see research/capoeira/moves.md); now and then he tries it and misses, and nobody makes a ritual of it. Kalunga, between jokes, says only: "Your body can't sing yet." **First lesson** (decided Nov 1, 2026): the ginga is not only the rhythm. It is getting the body to move the way it is supposed to, naturally, without too much thinking: on instinct, in the right moment, with the right balance. Much like dancing, yes.
      - **c. 1860. The turn.** As his rhythm improves, his moves improve with it. He starts to respect the master, then to enjoy the music and the dance of capoeira. His familiarity with music, singing at work since childhood, makes it easier: he already sings in rhythm, and once his body can sing too he becomes a much more dangerous fighter. **Second lesson** (decided Nov 1, 2026): knowing how to move is only part of it; in the real fight you have to control the flow of the fight itself. You cannot control the opponent, but you can trick him. Feint a move and see how he reacts: if his reaction opens an opportunity, use it; if he does not react, perhaps throw the punch for real and catch him by surprise.
      - **c. 1860–61. The acting game** (decided Nov 1, 2026). **Third lesson**: the opponent cannot know what you will do next. Better: he should think he knows. It is an acting game. Pretend you are hurt when you are not; pretend you will dive when you will punch. Play on your opponent's body, so that he loses his tempo, so that he loses his ginga.
      - **c. 1859–62. Three hearts change, slowly** (decided Oct 30, 2026). None of the three expects much of the others. Bastião sees a pampered boy from the big house; Kalunga, a boy who wants to hit things; Benedito, a joker who dances and a friend who mocks him. It changes by small steps, over the years, never in one scene: Bastião's teasing turns into a private joke between them, and the day Ezequiel singles Benedito out, Bastião stands beside him. Kalunga, who sent one angry boy away, recognises the anger in this one too, but also the voice, the songs his mother left him, and decides to keep him. Benedito, who mocked the dancing, starts to sing in the roda, and finds that Kalunga's jokes were lessons. By the end of the chapter the three are a kind of family: the first he has had since Luzia.
      - **c. 1860–61. He joins the Kalunga clan** (decided Oct 30, 2026). He has earned his place: Velho Kalunga accepts him into the clan, in his own way, with a joke rather than a ceremony. Bastião, who has called him "the milk brother" since they were boys: "Does this make us the coffee brothers?" (*"Então agora a gente é irmão de café?"*). Everyone laughs, Kalunga too. Coffee for the senzala, milk for the big house: the old tease becomes their private joke. (In Portuguese it also plays on *café com leite*, the mixed-race colour; pays off in ch. 12.) **The clan's creed** (decided Nov 1, 2026): the art is not about killing people; it is about protecting our freedom. We do not want to impose our ways onto others, as the lords did. We protect each other, and we are happy we have the chance to do it; and we do it with a smile on our faces. (Note for the writer, not for the text: at Liberdade he betrays this creed, a senhor whose wishes are orders, ch. 16.)
      - **c. 1860–62. The terreiro** (decided Oct 27, 2026). One night after a roda the capoeira boys invite him to a Candomblé ceremony in Cachoeira, at Mãe Otília's terreiro. Mãe Otília: the priestess of the terreiro; she comes to know him here, and that is why she gives him the guia at the end of the chapter. Rosária: an enslaved house servant from the estate and an apprentice of the Boa Morte sisterhood (its sisters are older freed Black women; she helps and sings); clever and religious. She is there, singing, and her voice catches him. He knows the songs: they are his mother's (he still does not know what they mean). He starts to sing too, and the two become friends, only friends until the festa (below); he will leave anyway.
      - **c. 1861–62. The mind game** (decided Nov 1, 2026). **Fourth lesson**: finally, you play on your opponent's mind. Play on his ego if he has too much of it. Play on his fears. Play on the ones he cares about: it is not an honourable thing to do, but it can become necessary for survival. Play games with his mind, with his pride. Laugh, joke, make it look easy even if it is not.
      - **c. 1862–63. What ginga is** (decided Nov 1, 2026). **The last lesson**: the ginga is an art. It is the dance; it is the magician playing a trick on the opponent; it is the difference between what one thinks will happen and what actually happens. It is much more than throwing kicks: a connection of body, mind and soul. When you are in ginga you do not think; the ginga just happens. And most importantly, enjoy the process: "The strongest kicks are delivered with a smile."
      - **c. 1860–63. The rivalry grows up and turns sour** (decided Oct 27, 2026). He is no longer the boy who pees from a roof and runs. Ezequiel is free now and holds the whip, and he uses his freedom to provoke Benedito: he flaunts it, reminds him that he is still property, that he will die a slave, that freedom is something Benedito will never have. Benedito answers with silence, and with the roda. The hate is mutual and quiet.
      - **c. 1862–65. Tonico and Ezequiel** (decided Oct 27, 2026). As Tonico grows older he takes on more of the estate's business, and sometimes he has to give Ezequiel orders. Ezequiel refuses to listen to him, or obeys slowly and with contempt: he will not take orders from the boy who has everything that should also be his, his own half-brother, though only Ezequiel knows it. The master does not always back his son. Pays off in the Interlude: the first thing Tonico does as master is fire him.
      - **c. 1862–64. Thoughts of running away** (decided Oct 27, 2026). He makes plans to run, to a quilombo like the ones people whisper about, but he is afraid: many of those who run are caught and killed, and the man who hunts them is Ezequiel, whose whip he has seen. And Ezequiel, because of the rivalry, is always watching him: Benedito gets few chances to slip away, and he is sure Ezequiel would seize any chance to punish him. Running is not really an option; the plans stay plans. The first real way out will be the war. (Bastião does run, at the end of the chapter.)
      - **August 1864. The festa in Cachoeira: he beats Ezequiel** (decided Oct 27, 2026; the festa and the fight merged Oct 31, 2026). At the August festa of the Boa Morte sisterhood in Cachoeira, where Rosária sings as an apprentice of the sisterhood, Ezequiel and his friends, off duty and drunk, harass her in the crowd. The capoeira boys step in: a street fight in the festa, quick and dirty, and capoeira wins it. In the middle of it someone in the crowd shouts the joke at Ezequiel (decided Oct 30, 2026). Benedito defends Rosária the way he would have defended his mother, if he had had the chance (decided Nov 1, 2026): in Ezequiel's hands on her he sees Luzia being taken, and he gets angry and explosive. Then the fight narrows to the two of them: two of Kalunga's students, the one he sent away and the one he kept. Ezequiel is winning. In the middle of it Kalunga's lessons come back to him, one by one, without thinking: he feints, he pretends to be hurt, the shouted joke works on Ezequiel's pride, and he plays on what Ezequiel cares about most, the father who never calls him son: Benedito taunts him with it, the dishonourable lesson, and it hits harder than any kick (it also shows that Benedito has guessed the truth) (decided Nov 1, 2026). He smiles. The rabo de arraia comes out almost by accident, with no flourish, and Ezequiel is down before either of them understands what happened. Standing over him he says: Not black enough to fight, not white enough to be a master. \[Catharsis point.\] He feels he has finally overcome his rival. In the moment he really hates Ezequiel and is only angry; only later does he understand the complexity of Ezequiel's situation, and that his and Tonico's many provocations never helped (decided Nov 2, 2026). (Notes for the writer, not for the text: the boy taught to keep his head down wins by lowering it; the rabo de arraia is the last lesson, the ginga that just happens; the explosive anger and Kalunga's smile fight inside him in the same fight.) This time there is no melting into the crowd: the whole festa has seen an enslaved boy put the capitão do mato on the ground, and Ezequiel wants revenge. Afterwards, away from the noise, Benedito and Rosária talk, and the romance starts.
      - **1865. The news of the war, and the bargain.** News comes that the army frees enslaved men who serve. Pressed to contribute to the war, the Coronel decides to offer some of his enslaved men, freed on condition of enlisting, as planters did for honours and favour with the Crown. He puts himself forward at once: it is the first real door to freedom he has ever seen, the way out that running, under Ezequiel's eyes, never was. It also comes just in time: since the festa Ezequiel has been waiting for his revenge, and the war is his escape (decided Oct 31, 2026). A bet on luck, the way his mother taught him; the slight chance of freedom is better than certainty of perpetual slavery. Tonico is never a candidate: the master's son was never going to be sent while there were enslaved men to send (decided Oct 27, 2026). Ezequiel is spared too: he is no longer enslaved, so he is not among the men offered, and the master protects his capitão do mato. The freedom Benedito has to risk his life for, Ezequiel already has, and it keeps him safe at home: one more turn of the rivalry (decided Oct 27, 2026). He hums Olúrónbí as he goes, not knowing it is a song about paying with a person. With Luzia gone, Orí ni baba, Orí ni ìyá is all he has (see research/music/luzia-songs.md).
      - **1865. Everyone leaves.** Rosária is sold. Bastião refuses to go to someone else's war and runs to a quilombo. Velho Kalunga stays in the woods near the estate (decided Nov 1, 2026); Ezequiel finds the hideout and kills him c. 1868 (Interlude, decided Nov 2, 2026).
      - **1865. The guia.** Mãe Otília gives him the guia for Ogum: "Ògún yè!", greeting to the iron god who opens the roads. Chapter ends: he leaves.
  - Facts:
      
      - [DOCUMENTED] In 1865–66 enslaved men were not drafted: owners donated enslaved men by freeing them on condition of enlisting, under pressure to contribute (630 donated from Rio city alone); enslaved substitutes for free men were rare. (Sources: S1)
      - [STRONGLY SUPPORTED as tradition] The Irmandade da Boa Morte was founded in Salvador (Barroquinha) c. 1820 by Black women linked to Candomblé, and refounded in Cachoeira c. 1850; its festa is in August. A festa in Cachoeira in 1864 is plausible. (Sources: S2)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Capoeira became a national crime only with the Penal Code of 1890 (arts. 402–404); before that, specific repression was mainly in Rio (public whippings, 1821–24; 200 lashes and forced labour for enslaved capoeiras, 1824). In Bahia it was policed as batuque, disorder or vagrancy; Manoel Querino describes neighbourhood capoeira groups in nineteenth-century Salvador. (Sources: S2)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Masters were divided on batuques and festas: some saw a safety valve, others feared them, since revolts were often set for Christmas, holy days and Sundays. The festive calendar: Christmas and Kings, Holy Week, Santo Antônio, São João (bonfires), Corpus Christi, the Black brotherhoods' festas and funerals, the Bonfim in Salvador (January), the Dois de Julho (from 1824), Candomblé ceremonies, samba de roda. (Sources: S2)
