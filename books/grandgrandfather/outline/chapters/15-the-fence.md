### 15\. The Fence

  - When / where: c. 1884–87 · Jacadigo (the negative things start; split from São João (decided Nov 2, 2026); reorganized Nov 2, 2026)
  - Readiness: 73/100 · beats 4 · arc 4 · links 5 · facts 4 · decisions 3 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **How the chapter runs** (reorganized Nov 2, 2026). The fence and the Pires → the old hands begin to go, Firmino dies → the slow turn in both of them → her pride, the Pires drawing her in, the teas → the white boys → his distrust → the other fence, which closes the chapter. The fence frames the chapter (decided Nov 2, 2026): it opens with the wire between the farms and ends with the fence growing between him and Carmen. Two meanings, one laid out at the start and one at the end. The negative things start here; the family breaks in ch. 16.
      - **1. c. 1884–86. The oxen that disappear.** Liberdade oxen turn up mixed into the Pires herd, and nobody can prove anything. On a steamer a Uruguayan merchant tells him Uruguay is all fenced with wire. He buys the rolls; the cows lean on the fence, one gets tangled in it, and the peões mock "the corral the size of the world". The Pires are furious. (Dr. Teles's measured boundaries, ch. 14, are what make the fence possible.) In 1887 only "one or two" ranchers in Mato Grosso have fences, and Liberdade is one of them. The first meaning of the fence (decided Nov 2, 2026): it separates the farms and keeps what is his; told plainly, so the reader carries it to the end of the chapter (9).
      - **2. The old hands begin to go** (moved from ch. 14, decided Nov 2, 2026). Over these years the loyal veterans start to die, slowly, one by one, and some of the men who carried the war inside them leave or fall apart. Hiring new people becomes a challenge he has to deal with: the new men are not his comrades, and the edge that kept Liberdade competitive without owning anyone starts to wear thin (ch. 14; complete in ch. 17).
      - **3. Firmino dies** (decided Nov 1, 2026). Firmino dies of an old war wound from Paraguay that never healed, slowly, in the 1880s; his loss is one of the losses that leave the farm without its old hands (see ch. 17).
      - **4. The slow turn.** As the years pass, things change slowly in both of them: smaller incidents at first, then they evolve. She develops the pride; he starts being paranoid. The warmth cools; her gratitude wears slowly into resentment, with no single breaking point: she could not say when it changed (decided Nov 1, 2026). Being sold had taught her his life, but once she is rich and courted by the elite, her old European racism comes back (decided Nov 1, 2026). Bastião, carelessly, mentions Rosária once: Carmen never forgets it (decided Nov 1, 2026). His headaches (ch. 7) come more often over the years, slowly getting worse (decided Nov 1, 2026). Proposed events (to confirm): her first silk dress ordered from Corumbá; then a piano or European furniture nobody uses; she stops going down to the corrals; she starts correcting his Portuguese and the sons' manners. He counts the cattle twice; he locks the storehouse and keeps the key on him; he hears thieves at night and fires into the sky; later he accuses his sons.
      - **5. Her pride and appearances.** Once rich, she becomes materialistic, very proud of how rich she appears: clothes, furniture, the house, how the family is seen in Corumbá. This is part of the divide between her and him. She wants her sons to look like nobility. They are partly Black, which bothers her: she is also a bit racist. The sons refuse to work the land, and she does not force them; she does not like to see them doing heavy work, because it makes them look like slaves, and that disgusts her. Another rift with him.
      - **6. The Pires and the wife.** The Pires find a chance to remain close to the family at Liberdade by attracting her. They can see she wants to be included, so they lure her in. Through her they learn about the farm, and slowly they manipulate her into conflicts with her husband: they suggest she is not being treated as well as she should be, they make her feel she is not living the life she should; they keep showing her how they live and how much she is still missing. This feeds the rift between the couple. He does not really like the Pires; he can see their bad intentions clearly. They keep a strange relationship, mostly because of the ties of his wife and sons. The Pires are fake people, but very good at keeping the appearance of nobility, education and respect. They can manipulate his wife and sons, but not him. Very often he is put in the position of having to accept the Pires or lose his relationship with his wife and sons. He becomes increasingly paranoid, often with reason, but over time that gets blurry.
      - **6a. The teas** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Carmen often spends time having tea with the Pires, sometimes at their house, sometimes in Benedito's house. He does not like her spending so much time at theirs, because he does not go there himself. He ends up forbidding her to go; she invites them into Benedito's house instead, which is not comfortable for him either. But this way, at least, he can watch.
      - **6b. The salon talk** (groundwork with Rafael, Nov 2, 2026). Over the teas the Pires talk, at length and with pleasure, about what it means to be rich. Their theme: money is not enough; any lucky man can have money, but true wealth is culture, education, manners, a name. A posh, self-satisfied philosophizing, with an air of superiority and plenty of hypocrisy: they are rich themselves, slave owners, and they say all this while their cattle graze land that is not theirs (ch. 14). The barb under every sentence, never said to his face, is the lottery: fortune can be won with a ticket; a name cannot. The quotes they use are in 6c and in the research note (research/daily-life/citacoes-salao-pires.md). Background (decided Nov 2, 2026): the Pires are an old family rich in land and slaves but short of cash and living on credit, while Benedito has cash; culture is the one currency he cannot buy, and that is why they spend it so freely (it also means the money they later lend the sons, ch. 18, is itself borrowed) \[PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION\].
      - **6c. The quotes** (groundwork with Rafael, Nov 2, 2026; quotes proposed, see the research note for exact text and sources). The Pires quote the thinkers of their education, in the original language, and translate only when they want her to notice she needed it. Proposed set: Gracián, *Oráculo manual* (1647), §87, "Nace bárbaro el hombre; redímese de bestia cultivándose" (man is born a barbarian and redeems himself from the beast by cultivation), offered to her as the wisdom of her own Spain, which she has never read; Seneca, *Letters* 2, "Non qui parum habet, sed qui plus cupit, pauper est" (the poor man is not the one who has little but the one who wants more), said by men who want the Barão's lands; Horace, *Epistles* 1.1, "vilius argentum est auro, virtutibus aurum" (silver is worth less than gold, gold less than virtue) (the next lines, which they never quote, describe them: "citizens, money first, virtue after the coins"); Chesterfield, *Letters to his Son* (16 Oct. 1747), politeness and good breeding are "absolutely necessary to adorn any, or all other good qualities or talents", read in a French edition; Buffon, *Discours sur le style* (1753), "Le style est l'homme même" (the style is the man himself); the Marquês de Maricá, the Brazilian maxim-writer of their fathers' time, "A riqueza doura a sabedoria e os talentos, mas não os constitui" (wealth gilds wisdom and talent but does not make them); and the line of the Brazilian manuals of manners, "a educação é mais necessária do que a ilustração" (manners are more necessary than learning, *Revista Popular*, 1859). Use a few, not all; each should come back later in Carmen's mouth (6e).
      - **6d. Carmen feels ignorant** (groundwork with Rafael, Nov 2, 2026). She can read and do sums (the priest taught her, ch. 11), but she is not well read like them, and they make her feel it: a name she should know, a Latin line nobody translates, a smile between husband and wife when she answers wrong. She pretends to know: she nods, repeats the names, laughs at the right moment, gets a pronunciation wrong and sees them notice. Then she tries to catch up: she borrows books from the Pires and orders others from Corumbá, and reads at night by the lamp, slowly, a French she cannot follow and a Spanish she can. (Among his things she finds the Spanish *Don Quixote* he took from a sacked house in Asunción (ch. 8) and never managed to read to the end, and in it Sancho's grandmother: "there are only two lineages in the world, the having and the not having" (II.20). It is the truest line she reads, and the one the Pires would never quote. She does not use it on him. She says it only once, to Benedito, late, in a rare moment of peace, and for once they agree (decided Nov 2, 2026) (where: suggested ch. 17, near the night he tells her his true name).)
      - **6e. She uses the quotes on Benedito** (groundwork with Rafael, Nov 2, 2026). At her own table, often in front of the sons, she uses the same lines on her husband that the Pires used on her, and makes him feel ignorant in turn: Gracián's barbarian, said to a man who was born a slave; Seneca, when he counts the cattle twice or locks the storehouse (4, 8): "the poor man is the one who wants more"; Buffon, when she corrects his Portuguese (4). He learned to read in the war, from old newspapers (ch. 4); he has no Latin and no French, and no answer that she would accept: a proverb of Luzia's or a joke from Kalunga's roda sounds, to her now, like the senzala. Mostly he says nothing. The sons hear it, and learn from it which of their parents is the educated one (ch. 16). The humiliation passes down the line exactly as it was handed to her. (The full arguments, and what the ledgers show him, are in ch. 16, 6.)
      - **6f. Provocation, not a plan** (note for the writer, not for the text; groundwork with Rafael, Nov 2, 2026). The Pires' philosophizing is not a well-built plan to undermine Liberdade. It is provocation: it costs them nothing, they enjoy it, and they poke to see what happens. Whatever it brings them (what they learn about the farm through her, the rift between the couple) comes as a bonus. Keep it light and believable: vanity and malice, not a conspiracy. For the reader, the cruelty is that nobody planned it.
      - **7. The white boys.** The Pires sons and Otávio (son of a Corumbá merchant who trades with the farm; minor character) grow up with his sons, always around at Liberdade. He is reminded of his own childhood with Tonico. But he notices bad intentions towards his sons: they call them moleque when they think no one is listening; in their games his sons are always the horse, the servant, the one who fetches; they ask too many questions (how many head of cattle, where the money is kept); small things go missing after their visits. He warns his sons, but this creates more rifts. His wife is always in favour of the white boys: for her they are a way to get closer to their parents, to be among the noble. He second-guesses himself: Tonico turned out to be a loyal friend after all. Finally he allows the white boys to be always around, and his sons to be with them. The negative signs never disappear; whenever he mentions them, he is accused of being distrustful and paranoid. For the reader: he is paranoid, but not always wrong.
      - **8. His distrust grows** (it starts with the lottery, ch. 12; developed here). Step by step: first he hides his wealth and keeps the money close; then the white neighbours, who hate a rich Black man, and Black people, who envy him, become in his eyes the same threat. He sees thieves everywhere and fires his gun into the sky at night to scare them off. Finally the suspicion turns inward, onto his own house: he accuses his sons of stealing from him (grandma does the same in the prologue). By the end of the chapter he trusts no one but his wife with the accounts, and even that only half.
      - **9. The other fence** (decided Nov 2, 2026). The last note of the chapter. The fence that separates the farms now grows between Benedito and Carmen. In bed she puts a pillow between them, a sign for him not to come close. He does not cross it. The second meaning of the fence, set against the first (1): the wire he put up to keep what is his, and the one she puts up to keep herself from him. Not explained on the page; the image is enough. This closes the chapter.
