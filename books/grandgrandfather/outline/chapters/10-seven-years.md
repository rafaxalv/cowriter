### 10\. Seven years of freedom, poverty and bad luck

  - When / where: 1870–1877, age 24–31 · the discharge, the river to Corumbá; Corumbá (The River and The Paper merged Oct 31, 2026; The Ticket merged in, Nov 1, 2026)
  - Readiness: 71/100 · beats 4 · arc 4 · links 4 · facts 3 · decisions 4 · voice 2 (Nov 4, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **The Paper (1870–1876).** Discharged without back pay. The boats go downriver to Bahia; he doesn't take one.
      - **Roque Júnior goes home** (decided Oct 30, 2026). They part at the discharge: Roque Júnior goes back to São Paulo, to his mother and the brothers he has never met. Benedito never hears of him again.
      - Benedito arrives in booming Corumbá.
      - **The jail (c. 1870)** (decided Nov 1, 2026). He sleeps every day in a different place; no one wants a negro around. One night he loses his documents. The police check him and he ends up in jail. Without his carta de alforria he can be taken for a runaway: a clerk mentions that, if no one claims him and nothing proves he is free, he will be advertised and sold at auction. The first night is almost a relief: at least for that night he does not have to think about where he is going to sleep. Then every day the registry is not found, the fear grows. After about a week, Euclides Bastos, a white comrade from the war, recognises him and vouches for him; his word is enough, because he is white. Then the police find his registry. Euclides means well and never sees why his word counted and Benedito's did not.
      - **The vagrancy threat (c. 1871)** (decided Nov 1, 2026). Between jobs, a policeman warns him that a man with no work is a vagrant; the memory of the cell does the rest. He takes the first steady job going: porter for Seu Albino's tavern, carrying barrels and goods from the port. To him Albino is just a stingy, crude boss; he knows nothing of the trafficking until the woman from Spain tells him (ch. 11).
      - The land plot promised by the 1865 decree: Euclides Bastos's claim goes through without a thought; his stays "under review". No one says why.
      - **The vote that isn't his (parish election, 1872)** (decided Nov 1, 2026): one paragraph, not a scene. A local boss, a regular at Albino's, herds him and the other porters to the polls to vote for a man who will never answer to them. His first act as a citizen is not his.
      - **Firmino in Corumbá** (decided Nov 2, 2026): he too stays on the frontier after the war. They see each other from time to time and help each other, sometimes working together; only mentioned, never expanded.
      - Working as a porter, ranch hand or arsenal labourer beside Paraguayan immigrants, the men he fought. Freedom is harder than he dreamed: now he must pay rent and buy food, with no guarantee of work or money. As a slave he always had a roof and a ration; free, he can go hungry. He never wishes to go back, but freedom is thinner than the war promised (his experience, never the narrator's verdict).
      - **The anger comes back** (decided Nov 1, 2026). The anger that Kalunga's lessons had slowly softened (ch. 3) comes back after the war, stronger: the war, the unpaid discharge, the jail, the plot "under review", a freedom thinner than promised. The headaches from the blow at Humaitá (ch. 7) start here, now and then, and they make the anger worse (decided Nov 1, 2026).
      - Whenever a list of the freed or a newspaper from the south reaches Corumbá, he searches it for Luzia's name (he stops only when her letters come, ch. 13).
      - **The tickets, a compulsion** (decided Nov 2, 2026). From his first days in Corumbá he buys a lottery ticket for every draw. Never a scene, only one-sentence beats scattered through the chapter: "He left, and on his way bought a lottery ticket. Maybe this time." He buys a ticket instead of food. He walks out of the jail and buys one. It looks silly, a poor man throwing his money away, and it borders on compulsion. Seven years go by and nothing. Bad luck. He becomes friends with Seu Bento, the ticket seller, this way.
      - **The Ticket (c. 1877).** Seu Bento, the ticket seller, an elderly free Black street vendor: he sells him tickets "because you have a winner's face", becomes his most honest friend and keeps telling him to save.
      - He has carried for Albino's tavern since 1871 (above), so he knows the place years before she arrives (decided Nov 1, 2026).
      - **The first meeting (c. February 1877).** At Albino's place he meets the woman from Spain, whom everyone takes for Albino's niece. She is afraid of him at first; he is a poorly dressed Black porter in a room where she has no control. He notices her youth, whiteness and literacy, and envies the doors he imagines those things open to her, while most are closed to him. He does not yet know she is trafficked or being sold.
      - **The conversations before the offer (c. February–July 1877).** During Benedito's occasional work for Albino, he and Carmen manage two longer conversations and three or four brief attempts before Albino finds a reason to interrupt and send him away. In the first longer talk, she is guarded and afraid; in the second, they find some common ground in reading and fragments of their pasts. Carmen shares little and hides the trafficking, the sale and the brothel threat. The brief attempts are summarized, showing Albino watching and cutting them off. The conversations give Benedito a glimpse of a life he thinks she can choose; learning the truth will change that envy in her story chapter (ch. 13).
  - Facts:
      
      - [PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION] A freedman caught without papers could be held as a suspected runaway; captured runaways were advertised and, if unclaimed, sold at auction. (to verify for Mato Grosso in the 1870s)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Vagrancy (Criminal Code of 1830): a man without steady work could be warned to find an occupation and jailed if he did not. Poor Black men were the usual targets. (see research/freed-slave-rights-1865-1888.md)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Brazil-born freedmen were citizens and could vote in parish elections if they met the income requirement, but could not be electors (who chose deputies). (see research/freed-slave-rights-1865-1888.md)
