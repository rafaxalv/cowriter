### 14\. Liberdade

  - When / where: spring 1878, then looking back to early 1878, and on to c. 1884 · Jacadigo, Bolivian border (a rural area about 20–30 km south and southwest of Corumbá: the iron-red Jacadigo hills behind, the plain and the Lagoa do Jacadigo in front, Bolivia over the hills; see project note places-jacadigo) (Jacadigo and the good years of The House merged, Nov 1, 2026: the purchase of the land and the first good years; reorganized Nov 2, 2026)
  - Readiness: 78/100 · beats 4 · arc 3 · links 5 · facts 5 · decisions 5 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **How the chapter runs** (reorganized Nov 2, 2026). The opening scene (spring 1878) → back in time: buying the land, the Pires, naming the farm (early 1878) → the people of Liberdade → the good years and what he changes (1878–c. 1883) → the neighbours grow wary → the sons are born. The whole chapter is the happy ending the reader believes in; the slow turn starts in ch. 15.
      - **1. Opening scene: the horses** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Very short. Spring, a few months after he bought the farm (c. 1878). Benedito checks the teeth of some horses he is buying. They seem fine; he pays the seller. Things are good. Then the chapter moves back in time to the purchase of the land with Dr. Teles and to the Pires neighbours (below), and proceeds from there. (Note for the writer, not for the text: the teeth echo Luzia's sale, ch. 1, and the recruiter checking his own teeth, ch. 4; now he is the one checking. Never pointed out.)
      - **2. Buying the land** (told looking back, after the opening scene; early 1878; expanded, decided Nov 2, 2026). Southern Mato Grosso is still emptied by the war: the Paraguayan occupation (1864–68) broke up the young ranches, owners fled or died, and their cattle went wild. Land is cheap, but titles are a mess: most holdings are old sesmarias or posses marked "by eye", never measured, and since the Land Law of 1850 new land can only be bought. So he needs a seller with a real paper. **The seller**: the heirs of the Barão de Vila Maria, who held sesmarias at the foot of the Urucum hills, next to Jacadigo, and died in 1876 on his way back from Rio; his heirs sell part of his war-broken lands \[PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION: the Barão, his sesmarias and his death are documented; the sale to Benedito is fictional\]. **Dr. Teles** is honest, and it shows in the work: he traces the chain of owners, insists the boundaries are measured and written down (almost nobody in the region does it), and draws up a clean deed at the notary's; it is paid as the prize money arrives. Even so, on the form, next to his name, the clerk writes *preto*. Teles's advice: "Write everything down. Here the boundaries live in people's memories, and memories belong to whoever is strongest." (Sets up the fence, ch. 15, and the clean deeds that do not save the land, ch. 18.) **What he finds**: the iron-red hills behind, the lagoon in front, wild unbranded cattle nobody can claim, and the ruins of a house burned in the war.
      - **3. The Pires neighbours** (expanded with Rafael, decided Nov 2, 2026). An old ranching family of the region, there before the war; they fled during the invasion, came back and rebuilt with enslaved labour, holding their land by old custom, boundaries "by eye". **They wanted the Barão's lands**: they knew the Barão would never come back, and were planning to take them over unofficially, just by slowly occupying the land no one was defending (their cattle mixed into the wild herd, their men grazing it as if it were theirs). Benedito arrives as an impediment to their plan. **Then they offer to buy**: a courtesy visit, friendly and concerned, with hidden interests. The lands are not good, they say; the Barão was not a man of his word, and his heirs will cause problems; alone, Benedito will never manage to take care of all that. Benedito notes it, but at first shrugs and does not mind: he just refuses, and does not expect problems. **Why they do not use force**: Benedito, Firmino and Bastião form a strong pack, two veterans of the war and a capoeirista of Kalunga's roda, and it ends any thought in the Pires' heads of taking the land by force. They smile, and wait (the slow rivalry of ch. 15 starts here). The patriarch is a colonel of the Guarda Nacional, "Coronel Pires" (decided Nov 2, 2026): an honorary militia rank, as frontier ranching families often held, with prestige and armed men at his call; it is part of why nobody can ever prove the cattle thefts (ch. 15, 17). He is not juiz de paz.
      - **4. He names the farm Liberdade.** After slavery, the war, the poor years in Corumbá and the lottery, it reads like the happy ending of an epic: a beautiful farm whose name says everything he fought for. Write it so that the reader believes it too.
      - **5. The people of Liberdade.** He employs free men who were once enslaved, promising a fair wage and respect, all paid wages. Among them are other men who fought the war: some were friends and are loyal to him; others carry a lot of trauma and are mostly helped. Two are closest (next two items).
      - **5a. Firmino** (met in the war, ch. 4–9, and in Corumbá, ch. 10). One of the veterans, freed by the war like him, and his most loyal man at Liberdade: a great worker, the first in the corrals and the last to leave, who learns every improvement and teaches it to the others. His right hand, and one of the few people he trusts. (His slow death: ch. 15.)
      - **5b. Bastião** (decided Oct 30, 2026). He came with Luzia's letters (ch. 12–13) and stays: with Firmino, one of the most trusted men at Liberdade. Firmino the farm's best hand, Bastião the friend from before everything.
      - **6. The good years.** They have some good years at Liberdade. The lands are beautiful; the couple is fairly happy; she is still young and innocent. There is real warmth between them in these first years (decided Nov 1, 2026). This sets the tone of a happy ending. Liberdade is a cattle farm (the region lived on cattle: live cattle driven to other provinces, hides, and from c. 1870 charque, salted dried beef; see sources below), and it is productive: he is good with the land and knows what to do. He is still strong, and in the first years he throws himself into the work (decided Oct 30, 2026): he wakes before everyone and works all day, and no job is beneath him. He works beside his workers, men who were enslaved like him; this earns him their respect, and doing every job himself he learns exactly how the farm works and where it can be better. He knows their language, and being this close, with such good communication, he finds ways to make the work smarter, easier and more profitable.
      - **7. The first years: what he changes** (decided Oct 30, 2026). Small, light, funny stories, in the tone of ch. 2: the boy who peed from the roof comes back in the man. They set the reader at ease with him at Liberdade before the slow turn. In each one he finds, almost by accident, something nobody in the region was doing (see Facts). In order (next six items).
      - **7.1 c. 1878. The cattle steal the salt.** A few oxen break into the store at night and eat the salt, bought dear and carried from far away. Carmen is furious. In the weeks after, those oxen are fatter than the rest and will not leave the store door; one of them follows Bastião everywhere ("at last someone likes you"). He sees it: cattle that know the salt grow fat and come when called. The salt troughs (cochos).
      - **7.2 c. 1878–79. How many head do we have?** Carmen asks, and nobody knows; the peões give three different numbers. Benedito, Bastião and Firmino try to count, and the count changes every day: oxen leave, oxen come back, oxen that are not theirs. An old tame cow solves it: she becomes the sinuelo and leads the herd into the corral. Then the first branding (ferra). A short shadow: he hesitates with the hot iron in his hand, and does not say why. His work in the field meets Carmen's ledgers in the house.
      - **7.3 c. 1879, the first dry season. The caiman in the last pool.** The lagoon shrinks, the cattle sink in the mud, and a caiman lives in the last pool: Bastião against the caiman, pure comedy. The peões laugh when he has them dig holes in the dry ground, until water comes up. The cacimbas: water where nobody looked.
      - **7.4 c. 1879–80. The hides that rot.** The hides are left in the field, and the merchant in Corumbá pays half for them. He stakes the hides out in the yard; the yard becomes a field of stretched hides, to Carmen's despair, and the dogs steal one. Hides dried well and without holes: "Liberdade hides" get a name in the Corumbá warehouses.
      - **7.5 c. 1880–81. The horses that drag their legs.** The mal de cadeiras. The peões talk of the evil eye (quebranto). He tries Luzia's herbs, and nothing works: the horse spits out the tea. But he notices that the horses kept on high ground, away from the swamp of the horseflies and the capybaras, do not fall ill. He isolates the sick ones and buys mules: a private war between Bastião and one stubborn mule. The healer of men learns to care for animals, and learns that not everything can be cured.
      - **7.6 c. 1881–83. "I want to speak to the owner."** The Descalvados agent comes on the steamer, buying cattle. He takes Benedito for the foreman and asks for the owner. "I am the owner." He goes up the river and sees the steam factory, the first machine he has seen since the war, and the agent pays for good cattle without asking anyone's colour. A big foreign buyer who only looks at the ox.
      - **8. The neighbours grow wary.** The other landlords mock him at first, but grow wary of him as a competitor. He can produce, he has competitive prices, and he has fewer problems managing his workforce. He never owns a person, and it costs him (decided Nov 2, 2026): his neighbours have slaves and pay no wages. He stays competitive only because he works himself to the bone, and Bastião, Firmino and the trusted veterans are just as committed. The Pires family is one of these competitors. (It cannot last: the old hands start to go in ch. 15, and by ch. 17 they are all gone.)
      - **9. The sons are born**, four of them (working names, decided Nov 2, 2026): Afonso, the heir; Inácio, after his father, the follower; Manuel, after her absent father, the dreamer; and the youngest, José, after her brother Xosé, lost in Cuba (the son's name simply José (decided Nov 2, 2026)). Lucília becomes godmother. The first son, raised by his mother and Lucília, is the heir and wants to be accepted by the white elite. She tells the children she came "to marry a rich man", the version grandma heard; she can never write home the truth, and she searches for Ruchla for years.
  - Facts:
      
      - Mato Grosso ranchers in 1875–78 gave their cattle no water, salt or pasture; the cattle lived half wild (João Severiano da Fonseca, in Wilcox 2009). \[DOCUMENTED\]
      - Salt tames bagual cattle: mixed with tame cattle, they eat from the troughs and lose their fear (Süssekind 2016). Pantanal pastures lack minerals; salt raises births and weight (Embrapa 2008). \[DOCUMENTED\]
      - Ear marks, branding (ferra) and the sinuelo, the tame animal that leads the herd (Süssekind 2016). \[DOCUMENTED\]
      - In the dry season the baías, corixos and salinas dry up (Embrapa 2007). Cacimbas at Liberdade: \[PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION\].
      - Mal de cadeiras (Trypanosoma evansi), carried by horseflies; it devastated Mato Grosso's horses from the 1860s; no remedy worked; isolation was the measure taken (Vieira 2023). \[DOCUMENTED\] Mules as his answer: \[PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION\].
      - Descalvados, on the Paraguay River near Cáceres: a charqueada from the 1870s; bought by Jaime Cibils in 1880 and run with steam engines, making meat extract, broths, hides, tallow and soap for export (Rossetto 2023; Corrêa & Corrêa 2010). \[DOCUMENTED\] That it bought Liberdade's cattle: \[FICTIONAL\].
