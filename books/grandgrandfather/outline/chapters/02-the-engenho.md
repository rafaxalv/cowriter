### 2\. The Engenho

  - When / where: 1846–1858, age 0–12 · sugar estate in the Recôncavo · Tone: funny. There is suffering in this childhood, but also a lot of laughter and adventure (the pranks, the stick fights, the river). It turns dark with Inácio's death. He is a mischievous, quick, cheeky boy, not only a victim. The hard moments (Inácio's death, Luzia's sale) land harder because we have laughed with him first.
  - Readiness: 71/100 · beats 4 · arc 4 · links 4 · facts 4 · decisions 3 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **c. 1845–46. Kalunga and the boy Ezequiel** (decided Oct 30, 2026). Ezequiel, 11–12, finds his way to Velho Kalunga's secret rodas and learns from him. But he is proud, and violent with the other boys: he hurts them, he wants to win, not to play. Kalunga, then a strong man in middle age, sees quickly that he does not care for their cause, and sends him away. Ezequiel never forgets it. (Before the goiabeira night and long before the road to capitão do mato.)
      - **1846. The Coronel turns away.** While Luzia is pregnant, and then busy with her baby, the Coronel stops calling for her. He calls again for Ezequiel's mother, the woman he called before Luzia; she is older now. Luzia no longer has to hide visits to the big house. After this, the chapter does not follow the Coronel's nights.
      - **1846–47. Ezequiel notices.** At 12–13 he is old enough to see it: he is not as white as Tonico, nor as black as Benedito, and he is the only one of the three without a clear father.
      - **Ezequiel's secret** (c. 1846–47; Ezequiel, the capitão do mato, as a boy; his point of view, an important character set-up; decided Oct 23, 2026). He was never told who his father was. Always being met with silence from his mom every time he asked. After a while she ordered him to never ask, being very angry when he did. She would never say. When he was 12/13 and became a bit more aware, he started to notice the night walks his mom always did from time to time. Up until then he never even bothered much about where she was going, but on one specific night he got curious. His mom left without saying anything; she thought he was probably asleep. He wasn't. He decided to follow her, hiding behind bushes in the dark. He was good at it – could move quickly without a sound – a skill that would come in handy in his future as capitão do mato. He proceeds to follow her and realises she is going into the landlord's house. She enters, disappears. He notices lamps are turned on in one of the rooms. Quickly runs and climbs a goiabeira tree next to its window. From there he can see through the window panes the landlord touching his mom. First her breasts, then her ass. He slaps her and she falls. The man orders her to undress and brings her to the bed. Ezequiel watches for a while. He hears his mom, he hears the violence the landlord seemed to enjoy. She never cries. She remains almost emotionless. That seemed to infuriate the landlord, who would slap and beat her in order to hear sounds out of her mouth. Ezequiel can't watch for long. He runs. He cries, he is angry. He can't understand. But he knows he needs to be silent: other negroes had been violently beaten for challenging the landlord. He himself had been punished a few times. Fear was real. The anger had to be repressed. The image of his mom being assaulted and raped had to be repressed. He calms down. Gets home. Lays down. And it finally clicks. He knows who his father is. (This is the moment he knows; ch. 1 shows what everyone sees in his skin. Written explicitly, following Rafael's words: "This moment is cathartic – make it explicit – follow my words.") (The night walks are his mother's renewed visits to the Coronel, above.)
      - **After that night** (c. 1847 onwards). The time passes by. He starts noticing how the landlord's son has privileges, how his life was easier. He resents that. He should have that as well – he is also the son of the landlord. He tries to show he is better. He strives at everything. Shows off in front of the landlord as much as possible, trying to differentiate himself from the other negroes, because perhaps he would be treated better if he could show that to his unspoken father. The landlord makes use of that. He stimulates that, often saying "I always knew you were special" or "different". He profited from this. The boy started to tell on his fellow slaves: tell who maybe cheated work a bit, who skipped a shift, who got something extra to eat. Petty things. All to show his father he was special, he was worthy of being his son. (Tonico is his half-brother, which gives the pee prank its edge; Ezequiel is about 20 at the prank, c. 1854. Mirror: the gossip says the protagonist is the landlord's son and it is false; Ezequiel really is, and nobody says it.) His reward comes in ch. 3. *This ends the early childhood.*
      - **c. 1849–52. Luzia and her son:** her singing, admired by everyone on the estate (even the master's family stops to listen). She teaches him her Yoruba songs, which he learns without knowing what they mean; the family sings while they work, every day, most of the day, and that is why he sings so well (decided Oct 30, 2026); at night she sings him Olúrónbí as a lullaby (see research/music/luzia-songs.md); her voice will be his strongest memory of her, and he will sing her songs in the trenches and near the end of his life; she teaches him to keep his head down and to trust luck; she tries to keep what she earns, but it always goes to someone in need, and repeats one promise to him: the orixás will open the way, and one day they will both be free. The freedom never comes. The master now keeps her near the estate, but on market days she still works ao ganho in Cachoeira, selling sweets and remedies, and he carries her tray. Sundays and holy days are the enslaved's own: Inácio and Luzia work their roça, a small plot of cassava, beans and a few chickens; Saturday is market day in the towns of the Recôncavo.
      - **c. 1850–52. "The brothers."** The master and Dona Mariana jokingly call Benedito and Tonico "brothers". Ezequiel, Tonico's real brother, hears it, and it feeds his jealousy: the brother's place is his, not Benedito's.
      - **c. 1852.** Dona Mariana, the master's wife, teaches him prayers and letters "as a game", then stops when he learns too fast. A small kindness he resents and treasures.
      - **c. 1852–55. Before the estrangement: he and Tonico.** As small boys they are true friends. They fish in the river, race to the mill, trade a slingshot for a song, play capoeira and soldiers, and fight with sticks for swords, like real fighters; he teaches Tonico to swim, and Tonico once takes a beating from his father to cover for him. The friendship has a privilege: Tonico can always ask his father, or the overseers, to release Benedito from a task, so he escapes work often. He still works as a child, but less than the other enslaved children, and some of them resent it (decided Oct 30, 2026). Real, innocent mutual respect between a white boy and a Black boy who do not yet know the world forbids it. Write these scenes warm and unforced, so that their loss hurts later. Every clash with Ezequiel binds the two boys closer.
      - **c. 1853–55.** At the mill with his father: during the harvest he spends days beside Inácio in the mill and boiling house. He learns the whole chain of sugar: cutting cane, the crushing rollers, the boiling kettles, the purging house, the sugar loaves packed in crates for Salvador. He learns the harvest calendar, who gives orders (master, overseer, sugar master), how many hands each step needs and what a crate is worth. Without knowing it, he is learning to run a farm, the seed of Jacadigo.
      - **c. 1853–55. Following Luzia.** She is the estate's healer and midwife. She knows Yoruba plants, sets bones, delivers every child born in the senzala, and nursed the sick through the cholera of 1855 (she could not save Inácio). Even the master's family calls for her. He follows her everywhere and learns: which plants stop bleeding or fever, how to clean and bind a wound, to boil water, to keep the sick drinking. He will carry this knowledge into the war.
      - **c. 1852–55. Bastião** (decided Oct 30, 2026). Bastião, two or three years older, an enslaved boy on the estate: quick, funny, a born storyteller. They know each other but are not close. Benedito spends his days with Tonico, and to Bastião and the senzala children he is half the master's son's pet. Bastião teases him about it, but as a joke more than a grudge: he knows Benedito is only a small boy. His favourite name for him is "the milk brother" (*irmão de leite*), mocking the bond with Tonico (decided Oct 30, 2026; pays off in ch. 3).
      - **c. 1854. The pee prank.** Their favourite prank, pure mischief: they hide somewhere high and pee on whoever passes below, then roll about laughing. The grown-ups shout and shake their fists, but nobody can chase the master's son. Their best target is Ezequiel, the master's informer and the overseer's helper (not yet capitão do mato), the master's unrecognised son, hard on the enslaved and harder still on Tonico and the boy. One day they pee on him from above. Surprised and furious, he shouts up at them. Tonico shouts back: "Who are you going to complain to? Your dad?" Both boys howl with laughter. It hurts him more than the pee: he knows exactly who his father is, Tonico's own father, and he can never say it. The insult feeds his resentment, and his envy runs both ways. He envies Tonico, who has the father's name, the house and the privileges that should also be his. And he envies Benedito, who has less than anyone, but has a father: Inácio, who carries him on his shoulders at the mill and calls him son. (Irony only Ezequiel and the reader see: Tonico mocks his own brother about their shared father.) But that day some of it also lands in one of the troughs where the cane juice runs from the mill to the boiling house. The batch is spoiled, and the mill has to stop for hours while everything is cleaned. Tonico runs away, leaving the boy behind. The master punishes him severely, and the boy thinks: "Why does he have to be so angry?" (his own sons will think the same words about him).
      - **c. 1854–55. The sand prank.** Another day, with other children, they throw sand at people passing by. The angry passers-by go to a white woman who works as a servant on the estate (role to confirm: Tonico's nanny?) and ask: "Is this your boy?" She sees Tonico and says, proudly, that he is. Then they complain: she should bring up her children better, look what they have just done. She has to clean up after them (to confirm: wash the sandy passers-by, or bathe the boys?). Tonico gets away; when the story reaches the master, it is the boy who is punished.
      - **1855. His father's death.** Inácio (working name), an enslaved boiling-house worker and Luzia's companion, the man he calls father, falls sick in the cholera epidemic of 1855, when the boy is 9 (about 10,000 dead in Salvador alone; the enslaved and freed died out of proportion). The Coronel keeps calling Luzia to the big house to look after his own family, so it is the boy who mostly takes care of his father; he comes to feel that the Coronel did not let his mother care for him properly. He listens carefully to her instructions and repeats every step aloud as he does it (boil the water, keep him drinking, clean him), singing her songs at the same time, "Atotô" to Omolu among them: a ritual, and a way to make sure he has done everything right (decided Oct 27, 2026). She could not have saved him in any way. Inácio dies in the boy's hands. Luzia comes after he is dead, cries, and sings the lament over him, "We look for our father, and cannot find him", the saddest song the boy has ever heard. Afterwards she tells him: "Orí ni baba, Orí ni ìyá": your head is your father, your head is your mother (see research/music/luzia-songs.md). He never forgets. His first wound. (Option: tell the cholera only as a memory, in a few lines, so it does not stretch the chapter.) The song and the ritual follow him for the rest of his life (the war, ch. 6; Acosta Ñu, ch. 9; his own death, ch. 17).
      - **c. 1856. The mark in his eye:** once, when Tonico is helping him clear land, their play gets rough and Tonico hits him in the face with a piece of wood. An accident, not malice; a mark stays in the eye itself for life.
      - **1858. The master's debts.** When his debts come due, he sells Luzia, despite everything she knows. The estate loses its healer to pay a creditor. He begs Tonico to persuade his father. Tonico tries, or says he did; he is a boy and it changes nothing. He can't forgive either of them: he hates the master, and the friendship with Tonico goes cold. Estranged: not enemies, not friends.
      - Chapter ends: Luzia is sold south to pay the master's debts. He stays. His second wound.
  - Facts:
      
      - [DOCUMENTED] The 1858 sale was legal: no law yet stopped a master from selling a mother away from her 12-year-old son. Only in 1869 (Decreto 1.695) did Brazil ban separating husband and wife, or children under 15 from their parents, in any sale (and ban public slave auctions); the Free Womb Law of 1871 lowered the age to 12. (Sources: S3)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The Eusébio de Queirós Law (1850) ends the Atlantic trade: no new Africans arrive, prices rise, and the coffee planters of the South buy enslaved people from the declining sugar Northeast. Bahia only taxed slaves leaving the province from 1862, after her sale. (Sources: S4)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Salvador's city law of 1857 forces African ganhadores to register, pay a fee and wear a numbered metal plate; ganhadeiras face a yearly fee. The porters strike for 13 days (1–13 June 1857), Brazil's first general strike, and win partial changes. (Sources: S5)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The cholera of 1855–56 in Salvador: nearly 10,000 dead (8–18% of the population); the enslaved and especially the freed died out of proportion (freed people were 3% of the population but 15% of cholera deaths). (Sources: S1, S2)
      - [DOCUMENTED] In the Recôncavo (1780–1860) the enslaved generally had Sundays and holy days (sometimes Saturdays) and many worked their own roças (cassava, maize, beans, bananas, dendê, poultry, goats, pigs); a few sold surpluses, and some saved for their freedom; town markets were on Saturdays. (Sources: S6)
