### 11\. Her Road

  - When / where: c. February–May 1877 · the back of Seu Albino's tavern, Corumbá; her story, told to him: a fishing village near Vigo (1858–1876); the steamer Vigo → Buenos Aires (c. mid-1876); Buenos Aires (c.   - Readiness: 71/100 · beats 4 · arc 4 · links 4 · facts 2 · decisions 5 · voice 2 (Nov 4, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **How the chapter works: she tells him her story** (decided Nov 2, 2026). After their first meeting (ch. 10) they meet many times, in stolen moments at the back of the house; Lucília keeps watch. At first Albino does not mind; but he sees the two growing fond of each other, fears Benedito might "spoil" her virginity, and starts chasing him off whenever he finds them talking (decided Nov 2, 2026). They do not exactly hide, but they make small efforts to talk in peace; the rising risk runs under every meeting. He asks: where are you from, who taught you to read, what happened to your family, how did you come here; she answers part by part, in dialogue, and the story flows. Told from her side: what it costs her to tell it, what she softens, and her fear of him wearing off. Each part below is something she tells him. Her story must be finished before the win: the day after it, Albino hides her (ch. 12).
      - **Galicia (1858–1876).** Rosa, the lacemaker (palilleira), hard and devout, sings Galician songs; a "widow of the living": Manuel, the father, a fisherman, went to Buenos Aires and stopped writing. Her daughter half-believes he is rich somewhere and will rescue her; he never appears. Rosa lets her daughter go to save her from hunger, and regrets it for the rest of her life.
      - **Her dream and her counting** (decided Nov 1, 2026). She wants a respectable marriage: to choose her own husband and be a *señora* with a house, not a lacemaker's daughter waiting for a father who never writes. Rosa is paid by the piece; every night mother and daughter count bobbins, metres and coins. This is where "she trusts only what she can count" begins.
      - Xosé, the older brother, is conscripted to Cuba because they cannot pay the exemption; one letter, about fighting rebels and slaves, then yellow fever.
      - Don Faustino, the village priest, teaches her to read. He introduces the gancho Alvarado, out of naivety or for a fee (left open). Alvarado, well dressed and charming, carries letters "from girls who married well in Brazil" and promises a job as a housemaid in Buenos Aires.
      - **The crossing (c. 1876, steamer Vigo → Buenos Aires).** Ruchla (Raquel in Latin America), a Jewish girl from Austrian Galicia who boards via Lisbon: the other girl from "Galicia", one Catholic and one Jewish. Her closest friend.
      - **First doubt** (decided Nov 1, 2026). Alvarado's letters "from girls who married well in Brazil" all look alike: the same paper, the same phrases.
      - Doña Engracia, an older Galician woman going to join her son, teaches her to hide money in her hem and to say "no" in Portuguese. She gets off in Buenos Aires and is never seen again.
      - Capitán Iriarte looks the other way for a fee; one night he shows a kindness that cannot be repaid.
      - **Buenos Aires (c. 1876–77).** Madame Angélique's house: a French madam, elegant, businesslike, almost maternal. Being sold is the first time she is treated as goods; later she will recognise his life in hers.
      - Madame Angélique keeps her apart and untouched: a virgin sells for far more. Ruchla is sold; every day she sees what is waiting for her.
      - **How she understands, in stages** (decided Nov 1, 2026): the doubt on the ship (the letters); at the port she sees Madame Angélique pay Alvarado; when Ruchla is sold, she knows what she is and what waits for her.
      - **Madame Angélique's warning** (decided Nov 1, 2026): the worst buyers, she says, are the Black and mixed men of the river ports. The fear goes with her to Corumbá. Near the end she tells him this too, admitting she was afraid of him at first: a sign she has come to trust him (decided Nov 2, 2026).
      - She is sold upriver.
      - **Upriver (c. 1877, Asunción → Corumbá).** The river journey through the land of the war.
      - Seu Albino's tavern. He keeps her untouched in the back of the house, working with Lucília, and guards her closely: her price depends on her staying a virgin. In public she is his niece; nobody in Corumbá knows she was trafficked. He does not pimp her, which would hurt his image as a respectable white man with some influence; he wants one big, quick, private sale to a rich man. He always threatens her: if she does not behave, he will put her in the brothel; it really works on her (decided Nov 1, 2026). Lucília becomes her first friend. She meets him there, before his lottery win (first meeting, ch. 10), and tells him her story over many meetings (this chapter).
      - **Between her parts, they talk** (moved from ch. 10, Nov 2, 2026). They bond over not being free: he tells her that even freed, he does not feel free; she understands, and says she would rather be sold to a rich man than end up in a brothel (she means as a maid or a servant). A free woman's life would not be easy either; all her options look bad.
      - They agree that even with freedom things are not easy. He tells his story and how people seem to do their best to make his life as a free man harder than as a slave.
      - **What they are to each other** (decided Nov 1, 2026). He likes her. He likes talking to her. She is beautiful, and somehow they understand each other's feelings. They talk about not being free, but it means very different things to each of them: to him, not being owned, dignity, land of his own; to her, choosing her husband, respectability, the life she was promised in Galicia. She likes him, but he is far from the man she dreamed of marrying: brave, polite in his way, but poor. She never sees him as a candidate, but as a very nice companion, one who does not feel aggressive or like he would abuse her. He is clearly strong and has many skills. He sings to her sometimes and she likes it, but she never really understands what the songs say, not the way Rosária did. She admires him but never connects. He feels it: he can see her appreciation, but he can feel the difference from Rosária.
      - **Between meetings, the tickets** (decided Nov 2, 2026). He keeps buying tickets. Once he gives her one as a gift; she finds it silly, and says it, the only time the line is spoken in the book: "Men believe in luck; women count." (Said once, by her; elsewhere only shown (decided Nov 2, 2026).)
      - **The last meeting (April–May 1877), the end of the chapter** (moved Nov 4, 2026). She finishes her story: being sold upriver, arriving at Albino's. He leaves, and on his way buys a ticket with his last coins, before the June draw. Maybe this time. A bet on luck, the faith he got from Luzia. Hook: the draw.
