### 1\. Good Teeth

  - When / where: c. 1832–1846 · Salvador, then Coronel Menezes's sugar estate in the Recôncavo
  - Readiness: 65/100 · beats 4 · arc 3 · links 3 · facts 3 · decisions 4 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **Prelude: Luzia, c. 1832–33, Salvador. The sale (opening scene).** Buyers check her teeth, guess her age, chalk prices and haggle as if over a horse. Luzia, about 14, is just off the ship. A dirty old buyer with stinking breath touches her in a way no buyer needs to. Because she is beautiful she is taken apart from the group, cleaned and dressed. Told from inside the silence of the one being sold; never explicit. She lands just after the 1831 ban: by law she is free, and she is enslaved anyway (see Facts).
      - **1830s. The ganhadeira, briefly.** She sells in the streets, pays the jornal and gives the rest away. She trusts the orixás and luck; when she sings, people stop to listen. Selling, she sings Olúrónbí, the market women's song of the woman who promised the iroko tree her child and had to pay (see research/music/luzia-songs.md).
      - **1835. After the Malê Revolt.** In a square she sees a man whipped. She learns to keep her head down.
      - **c. 1843. Sold to the estate.** Her Salvador owner dies, and she is sold to Coronel Menezes's engenho in the Recôncavo as a healer and midwife. The street's half-freedom ends.
      - **The estate: the sugar chain**, seen through Luzia's work: she treats the cane-cutters' gashes and the boiling house's burns. The cane, the crushing mill, the boiling kettles, the purging house; the bell before dawn; the harvest (safra), August to April or May, with the mill grinding 18–20 hours a day in double shifts, cane cut by task (some 4,200 canes a day), the rest of the work from sunrise to sunset; most accidents happen at night, from exhaustion (Inácio's burn, below). They sing while they work, in the cane, at the mill, in the boiling house: one of their main distractions, every day, most of the day.
      - **The estate: the colour ladder.** Africans and crioulos, pretos and pardos, house and field: who gets lighter work, better rations, a pair of shoes, a word from the big house. Everyone knows the ladder; nobody says it.
      - **Ezequiel's place on it.** Born c. 1834 to a house servant the master took. His lighter skin is what everyone sees and nobody names. His mother never speaks of it (in ch. 2 she forbids him to ask). About 9 when Luzia arrives. For Luzia, his mother is a warning: a child with no father.
      - **The Coronel's nights.** The master calls Luzia to his room for the first time, and it goes on. She is not the only one; the senzala tries to guess which women go to the big house. A house maid knows: Luzia's remedies once saved her son, and she spreads false rumours to cover her. Never shown directly.
      - **c. 1844–45. Inácio.** He is burned in the boiling house when a kettle falls; the estate leaves him to die. She nurses him for weeks, and love grows out of the healing.
      - **The hidden love**, mostly in dialogue. Inácio suspects but is afraid to ask; Luzia stays silent to protect him. She chose Inácio; the master chose her.
      - **1845–46. The question.** She is pregnant and cannot tell who the father is: preto or pardo? If pardo, he will be like Ezequiel, with no father, and Inácio may leave her. She can tell no one. One day Ezequiel's mother sees her belly. She says nothing; she gives Luzia a look, as if to say *I know what you are going through*, and pulls Ezequiel close. The silence is louder than any gossip.
      - **Three births: 1846, Benedito, preto.** Born in the middle of the harvest, with the mill running day and night; nobody stops for him. The maid delivers him. He is all black: Inácio's son. The fear and the gossip break. The estate book counts him as property; an ink drop falls where his name should be.
      - **Three births: weeks later, Tonico, branco.** The long-awaited heir: the master needs a legitimate son for the estate and its debts. A lavish baptism in the chapel with planter godparents; the enslaved serve the feast. Ezequiel, 12, is among those serving: he carries plates at the feast of the son he can never be, and hates him from that day. Luzia is ordered to wet-nurse Tonico: the two boys become milk brothers.
      - **Closing image.** Three boys on one estate: a white master father, a Black enslaved father, a father nobody will name. Option for the last line: the Yoruba proverb "Birth does not differ from birth; as the free man was born so was the slave." (see research/music/luzia-songs.md) Chapter ends.
  - Facts:
      
      - [DOCUMENTED] Lei Feijó (7 Nov 1831) banned the trade and declared every African landed after it free; never enforced ("para inglês ver"): about 750,000 smuggled in, about 11,000 seized. (Sources: S1, S2)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The Malê Revolt (Salvador, 1835); Bahia's Law nº 9 (1835) put Africans under suspicion, taxes and deportation. (to verify)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The daily jornal of ganhadores and ganhadeiras; African savings circles (juntas). (to verify)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Masters' sexual exploitation of enslaved women went unpunished; a child followed the mother's condition, born enslaved whoever the father was. (to verify)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The Recôncavo sugar chain and harvest calendar; the colour hierarchy on estates (pretos, pardos, Africans, crioulos). (to verify)
      - [DOCUMENTED] The harvest (safra) ran from about August for eight or nine months; on large engenhos the mill ground 18–20 hours a day in double shifts, cane-cutting was task work (350 bundles of 12 canes), other work ran from sunrise to sunset, and most accidents happened at night. Workloads rose with the sugar boom after 1770–90 and again in the 1820s–30s. (Sources: S3)
      - [DOCUMENTED] Batuques (African drumming and dance) were banned or tolerated by governor: banned by the Conde da Ponte (1807), tolerated by the Conde dos Arcos (1810–18), repressed again after the Malê Revolt (1835), when the import of drums was banned. (Sources: S3)
      - [PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION] The enslaved sang at work: Salvador's African porters sang in chorus as they carried loads (Wetherell, 1840s–50s; Avé-Lallemant, 1859; Querino); in Minas Gerais the vissungos were Bantu-language work songs of the mines (recorded from 1928). Direct evidence for the Recôncavo cane fields is thinner. (Research file: `research/daily-life/rotina-lazer-festas-reconcavo.md`)
