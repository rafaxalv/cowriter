### 16\. São João

  - When / where: c. 1884–88 · Jacadigo (the family breaks; split from The Fence (decided Nov 2, 2026); reorganized into small events, Nov 2, 2026)
  - Readiness: 82/100 · beats 5 · arc 5 · links 5 · facts 3 · decisions 4 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **How the chapter runs** (reorganized Nov 2, 2026). Too much was packed into one story, so the chapter is now told through small events, each one a single conflict, building to the São João night. Four movements: the sons drift from the farm (1–3) → the culture he brings, hushed and mocked (4–5) → the arguments Carmen wins with the Pires' quotes, and what the ledgers show him (6) → he becomes "senhor" and nobody is free (7–8) → the São João night, which closes the chapter (9). The boys are small here (Afonso c. 5–8, José a toddler at the start): keep the events child-sized.
      - **1. c. 1884–86. What he wants for them, and what she wants** (decided Nov 1, 2026). He wants to teach them the ways of the farm, as his father Inácio once taught him the mill (ch. 2), and to sing along with them, as his mother did with him. But the boys are not very interested. Their mother wants them to learn to read, to have manners, to dress well; she wants to walk them through the streets of Corumbá with good posture, speaking politely to the elite. They easily prefer that, and grow further and further away from their father's routine. They do not understand why he cannot just rest and be like the other landowners; they think he works too much and smells of cow shit. His wife is good at arguing for learning to read, and he accepts it (he learned to read himself, from Dom Obá's newspapers); but he thinks the poshness goes too far. In his view the boys spend too much time learning to read, having coffees with other elite children, doing well-behaved activities like horse riding and some silly sports (which activities: see research/daily-life/infancia-elite-1880.md).
      - **2. His resentment** (decided Nov 1, 2026). maybe their spoiled ways are the white part of their blood; they are not *negro* enough to work, and not white enough to be an acceptable part of the elite they want to join. He thinks of Ezequiel for a moment ("Not black enough to fight, not white enough to be a master", ch. 3) and shrugs off the thought. His own childhood, even as a slave, still felt good to him: he worked alongside his father, he played pranks with Tonico. Children should be out exploring the world, dirty and careless from time to time, he thinks. He resents his sons: he feels they are spoiled, weak and unaware of how hard the world can be. In a way they resemble the worst of Tonico. These boys would never fight for him, he thinks; Tonico at least came around later on (the letter, sending Bastião, ch. 12–13), proving to be a true friend. His sons, he feels, are not even his friends. (Note for the writer, not for the text: he gave his sons Tonico's childhood, the master's son's, not his own, and resents what he made; see also "The sons grow up spoiled" and "The white boys", below.)
      - **3. The small events: the sons and the farm** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Short scenes, a page or two each, in any order the draft needs (next five items).
      - **3.1 The boots.** Enslaved people went barefoot; shoes were the mark of the free, and he remembers the first pair he owned. He buys the boys strong work boots in Corumbá, proud of the gift. They want patent shoes like the Pires boys'. The boots stay new in a corner of the house; he looks at them sometimes. (Real aspect: in slavery-era Brazil the enslaved were kept barefoot, and freed people bought shoes as a first sign of freedom \[STRONGLY SUPPORTED; to cite\].)
      - **3.2 The English saddle.** The boys learn to ride Corumbá style, on an English saddle, upright, in riding clothes. In the corral they cannot throw a lasso or turn a calf; the peões laugh, and Benedito laughs too, without meaning harm. Afonso never forgives the laugh.
      - **3.3 The gift.** For a birthday he carves a small ox, or plaits a lasso himself, the way his father made things for him. The boy thanks him politely, then runs to show everyone the pocket watch the Pires gave him.
      - **3.4 The sons' horse game.** His sons play with the peões' children, and make them be the horse, the servant, the one who fetches: exactly what the Pires boys do to them (ch. 15, 7). He sees it from the corral and does not know whom to blame: the Pires boys, his wife, his sons, or himself.
      - **3.5 A prank that isn't funny.** He has always hoped for a prank like his own from the roof (ch. 2), something wild and innocent. When it comes, it is cruel: a son plays a trick on an old Black hand, one of the veterans (water, a name, a laugh), the kind of joke the master's boys played. He sees the master in his son. He punishes the boy harder than the prank deserves, and does not explain why.
      - **4. The culture he brings.** His wife and sons reject his African culture: they are not interested in capoeira or the songs he sings. His ways and traumas make it harder to be close to them, and harder for them to accept his culture: small aspects of racism inside the family.
      - **4.1 A song hushed at the table** (decided Nov 2, 2026). He sings one of Luzia's songs at the table, without thinking, the way she sang to him. Carmen hushes him: "Not in front of the Pires." The guests smile into their cups. He stops in the middle of the line, and never sings it in the house again.
      - **4.2 Capoeira seen by the Pires boys** (decided Nov 2, 2026). He and Bastião play capoeira in the yard on a Sunday, laughing, as in Kalunga's roda. The Pires boys watch and mock the style: it looks like a dance; real men should fight, not dance, they say. Afonso, ashamed in front of his friends, says it is what slaves do. (Note for the writer: the reader knows from ch. 3 that the dance is the fight; Benedito beat Ezequiel with it. He could show them, and does not.)
      - **4.3 The photograph in Corumbá** (decided Nov 2, 2026). A family portrait in a Corumbá studio, the proof of a good family. The photographer powders the boys' faces lighter for the plate, and Carmen thanks him. Benedito stands at the back, unpowdered; in the picture he is the darkest thing in it. (Real aspect: photography studios in Brazilian towns in the 1880s, and whitening in portraits \[to verify for Corumbá\]. The photograph does not survive into grandma's time; it lives only in this chapter (decided Nov 2, 2026).)
      - **5. Spoiled, and still Black.** The sons grow up spoiled and disconnected from reality. Still they suffer racism: they are still Black, even if they are rich. They use the money to compensate for it. They are proud, and angry that they cannot be respected because of their race. This pride and anger will matter when they lose everything (ch. 18).
      - **6. The arguments she wins** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Carmen now uses the Pires' quotes (ch. 15, 6c–6e) at home, and wins every argument easily: he has no Latin, no French, no book to answer with. The dialogues are planned below; the lines are sketches, to be rewritten in the chapter's voice. Each argument is followed by a plain event on the farm where his knowledge, the kind nobody quotes, is what keeps them rich.
      - **6.1 The boys and the roundup (Gracián).** He: "The boys come with me to the roundup tomorrow. Afonso is old enough to sit a horse in the corral." She: "Afonso has his lesson." He: "He can do his letters at night. The cattle don't wait." She, in her own Spanish, slowly: "*Nace bárbaro el hombre; redímese de bestia cultivándose.* Man is born a barbarian, and redeems himself from the beast by cultivation. Do you want your sons to stay beasts?" He: "I learned to read in a war." She: "And it shows." He has no answer; the word *bestia* stays with him (he was sold with his teeth checked, like a horse, ch. 1, 4). The boys stay home. That same week the screwworm (bicheira) gets into the calves' navels after the branding; he works three days in the corral cleaning wounds by hand with Bastião, and saves nearly all of them. Nobody at the house asks how the calves are.
      - **6.2 The locks and the counting (Seneca).** Night. He counts the cattle money a second time and locks the storehouse, the key on a string round his neck. She, from the doorway: "*Non qui parum habet, sed qui plus cupit, pauper est.* The poor man is not the one who has little, but the one who wants more. You are the poorest man in Mato Grosso." He: "The poor man is the one who has nothing to eat. I have been him." She: "Everyone has been something. Now you are rich, and you hide the money like a slave hiding bread." He says nothing, and locks the door anyway. (For the reader: things do go missing after the white boys' visits, ch. 15, 7; he is paranoid, but not always wrong.)
      - **6.3 The ledgers** (decided Nov 2, 2026). Carmen keeps the accounts (ch. 14, 7.2; she takes them over completely in 7, below). One night he reads her neat columns, slowly, with his finger. On one side, everything she has won the arguments for: the tutor, the books from Corumbá, the French lessons, the silk, the photograph, the riding clothes. On the other, what pays for all of it: steers sold to the Descalvados agent, Liberdade hides, the calves he saved from the bicheira. Nothing on the first side has ever earned a coin; the cattle pay for everything, including the culture that despises them. She sees him reading and understands that he understands. Neither of them says anything. (He loses every argument out loud; the ledger is the only place where he wins, and nobody else reads it.)
      - **7. Becoming "senhor".** Demanding, his wishes are orders: he turns out harsh and strict, and the marriage sours, with ups and downs. The critical argument: he says, "You said you would rather be sold to a rich man. I am a rich man. I saved you." He tells her he also wanted a white wife, believing their sons would be treated better and stopped at fewer doors; she understands her whiteness was part of why he chose her. She answers: "I meant as a maid or a servant, not a sex slave. You didn't save me, you own me. You made me your slave; you never gave me the choice." It breaks his illusion of being her saviour and puts him in the place of the master. A deep inner conflict he does not know how to deal with. She takes over the household money and the farm accounts.
      - **8. Nobody is free at Liberdade.** Under the name, each of them is trapped in a different way: he by his fear of losing everything and his distrust; his wife, bought and married without a choice; the sons by their father's anger and rules. The narrator never points out the irony; the reader sees it.
      - **9. The São João night ends the chapter** (decided Nov 1, 2026): everything above builds up to it; it is the moment the family breaks.
      - A June night on the farm. His first son, Afonso, the heir (decided Nov 1, 2026), about 7 or 8, comes home from the São João bonfire with a moustache and beard blackened on his face in charcoal from the fogueira, a game the children play on each other (decided Nov 2, 2026), laughing, smelling of bonfire smoke. The father sees the charcoal beard on a small boy's face in the firelight and is back at Acosta Ñu. He beats the child hard, without a word, then scrubs his face until the skin is raw. The boy thinks: "Why does he have to be so angry?", the words his father once thought about the master. He never explains; the family never learns why, so it looks like pure cruelty. Only the reader knows.
      - His wife pulls the child away and, for the first time, stands against him in front of the children. After years of building the farms together she no longer recognises him; it feels like a betrayal of their partnership. The distance that opens that night is the deepest in their marriage and never fully closes.
      - The son grows up afraid of his father and never understands (pays off in ch. 18). From that night Afonso never lets a beard grow: he always shaves clean, earlier and more carefully than any boy his age, and people remark on it (decided Nov 2, 2026).
      - He never goes to a São João again: every June he stays in the dark while the bonfires burn on the neighbouring farms.
