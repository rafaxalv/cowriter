### 18\. The Table

  - When / where: 1890s · Corumbá
  - Readiness: 69/100 · beats 3 · arc 4 · links 4 · facts 3 · decisions 5 · voice 2 (Nov 2, 2026)
  - Stories:
      
      - **How the chapter runs** (reorganized (decided Nov 2, 2026)). The inheritance and the Pires' credit (1) → the widow and her ledgers (2) → why the sons gamble (3) and with whom (4) → the last card game, told from inside Afonso, which ends the chapter and the story (5). (Xosé's garimpo story removed (decided Nov 2, 2026).) Nothing comes after the last game in this chapter; the frame returns in the Epilogue.
      - **1. The inheritance.** The sons inherit; the Pires lend on credit. Dr. Teles's deeds were clean, and the land is lost anyway: registries, credit and inheritance law work as they were built to.
      - **2. The widow** (decided Nov 1, 2026). With him gone, she has to take over his tasks with the sons, and she quickly sees she has raised boys who cannot really run the farm, and do not want to. She sees the danger and fights with her ledgers, but the sons do not listen; half blind to the Pires, whom she has trusted for years, she loses. Her hidden hem money (arc F5, the only one who saved) is a small private comfort when everything else is gone; it is not what saves them: the family survives without the fortune (decided Nov 2, 2026). Real aspect: under the Ordenações Filipinas (in force until 1916) the default marriage was total community of property, so the widow was *meeira* (owner of half) and head of the household; in practice male relatives and the juiz de órfãos often limited her (Findlay, ANPUH) \[DOCUMENTED; details to verify\].
      - **3. Why they start.** the sons never learned to run the land, and Liberdade loses money year after year. Every time there is trouble at the farm, they gamble to raise cash. Their father bet on luck and won, didn't he? It is their easy way out.
      - **4. Who they play with.** the white boys they grew up with (ch. 15), now men, the Pires sons and Otávio, who pretend to be their friends and want only their wealth. The cards may be marked, or the game rigged in small ways (ch. 18, 5: it is never shown for certain (decided Nov 2, 2026)). The whites take it slowly, sometimes letting them win, eating away at what they have. Shortly before the end, the Pires let Afonso beat them twice in a row (decided Nov 2, 2026): the bait for the last game. Pride and the wish to be like the white elite keep the sons at the table. They resent their father, but they do not want to be poor; they lose everything out of pride and the desire to be like the white elite.
      - **5. The last game: the final scene** (told from inside Afonso, the heir; decided Nov 2, 2026; reorganized (decided Nov 2, 2026)). The important scene of the book's ending, and the chapter ends with it. His father is dead. For the first time in his life Afonso has let his beard grow, the son who always shaved clean since the São João night (ch. 16); he can finally wear it, and he pampers it proudly in the mirror before he goes, and at the table, a release. He is proud and sure he will win: he has just beaten the Pires twice (they let him win, 4, and he does not know it). Tonight he will bet more, and win more. At the table the Pires sons and Otávio, the friends of his childhood. They push him to go all in, and he is sure he holds a great hand: "The cards will open the way." One of them hums Olúrónbí at the table without knowing what it is. **Rigged, or not?** (decided Nov 2, 2026): the cheating is never shown for certain. Maybe it is the slightly bent corners of some cards; maybe the blinks the Pires share across the table; the reader, like Afonso, never knows. But when they win, the Pires are not surprised. He loses everything: the farm, the name his father built, the fortune of the lottery. **It ends here**, on Afonso at the table, the beard on his face (the charcoal beard of São João, and the boy of Acosta Ñu behind it, never pointed out).
