## 0. The elements to play with: luck, freedom, violence, money

Four forces run through every part of the book. Every scene should touch at least one of them. They are not lessons; they are the conditions the characters live under, and the book shows how each person bends, uses or is broken by them.

### Luck

  - **Luck is everywhere.** Where you are born, to which family, which name you are given; whether the bullet or the cholera finds you; whether you win the lottery or lose the bet.
  - **Luck sees no race.** That is why it appeals to him. The lottery wheel does not check his colour, and neither does the cholera. In a world where everything else is decided by colour and paper, chance is the one fair judge.
  - **He knows how it works.** Some luck brings more luck, but nothing is guaranteed. He respects the odds; he never takes them for granted.
  - **The war is his first bet.** If he survives, he is free. A chance of dying is better than the certainty of a whole life enslaved.
  - **He passes it on.** He puts his faith in luck into his sons, and they grow up loving the gamble. Luck is the family's inheritance, from his ticket to the sons' card tables to grandma's lottery (see the luck theme in the Character Bible).

### Freedom

  - **He bets his life for it** and accepts the odds of dying in the war.
  - **Freedom is not the happy ending.** Once free, life is not substantially better, and in some ways harder. He must work to survive, pay rent, buy food, with no guarantee of money ever. As a slave he always had a roof and a ration; as a free man he can go hungry.
  - **So the book questions freedom** without ever regretting it: he never wishes to go back. Freedom without money, papers or respect is a thinner thing than he dreamed. (Careful: "they were better off enslaved" was the planters' own argument against abolition. The hardship is his lived experience, never the narrator's verdict.)
  - **Freedom has a mirror.** The woman from Spain is born free and loses it; he gains it and then takes hers away by buying her.

### Violence

  - **Violence is a language,** the first one he learned.
  - **It is everywhere:** in slavery, in the war, in racism, in families torn apart by sale, in trafficking, and finally in his own house (the São João night).
  - **It is passed down** like luck: what was done to him, he does to others, often without seeing it. In the war it runs both ways: Black soldiers do to white men what was done to them (Joca whipping a Paraguayan officer to death, Damião in the village), and white soldiers and officers do it to Black men (Albuquerque, Benites). On the battlefield the field is level, because a bullet does not check colour; in the camp it is not. These stories also feed racist messaging, and he pays for it: he is wrongly accused of the rape and killing he tried to prevent, simply because he belongs to the Black troop. For now these remain accusations. He is innocent of what he is accused of, and guilty of what nobody knows about.

### Money

  - **"Money is freedom"** is Luzia's lesson, the first thing he believes. Every coin she hides is for his freedom.
  - **The book tests it:** money buys his land, a seat at the table, a woman. It does not buy his mother back, his neighbours' acceptance, his sons' understanding or his name.
  - **Luck brings the money; violence and the law take it away.** The four elements feed each other. *(Draft: to develop together.)*

## 1. The central family story

The book is based on a family story passed down through the user's maternal side.

At its centre are the user's great-great-grandparents, although their exact identities are currently uncertain:

### The man

  - He was reportedly born enslaved in Brazil.
  - He later fought in the War of the Triple Alliance / Paraguayan War (1864–1870).
  - According to family tradition, his military service resulted in his freedom.
  - At some point afterwards, he supposedly won a lottery.
  - With that money, he allegedly acquired several farms.
  - He subsequently became a relatively wealthy landowner.
  - He had several sons.
  - After his death, however, his children apparently lost the family's wealth and property.
  - His exact name is currently unknown.

### The woman

  - She was reportedly Spanish.
  - Family tradition says she had been trafficked from Spain to Brazil.
  - It is unclear whether she was actually trafficked, deceived into migration, brought through an exploitative arrangement, or whether the story has been altered over generations.
  - He bought her from her traffickers and married her. In his mind he saved her; in hers she was saved and given no choice. The marriage is complicated, with ups and downs.
  - Nevertheless, they had several children together.
  - Her name is also currently unknown.
  - Her documents and precise circumstances of arrival in Brazil are unknown.

So the extraordinary part isn't simply that your ancestor went from slavery to wealth.

It is that two people with almost completely forgotten identities supposedly became the founders of an entire family branch.

## 2. The most important narrative idea: two forgotten people

This became one of the strongest themes in the discussion.

You don't want to invent names for these people simply because a novel traditionally requires characters to have names.

Instead:

They have no names because you genuinely don't know their names.

And that absence itself becomes part of the story.

The man could be referred to throughout the fictional sections as:

**The Man With No Name**

or simply:

**the man**

Likewise, the woman could initially be:

**the woman from Spain**

or another deliberately descriptive but non-name designation.

This creates a mystery without pretending to know something that you don't.

## 3. "History forgot him twice"

This became one of the strongest possible motifs for the book.

The basic idea is:

### First forgetting

When he was enslaved, his original identity may have been replaced, altered, or obscured.

Enslaved people could have African names that were not preserved in later Brazilian records. They could subsequently receive Christian/Portuguese names through baptism, ownership, or other processes.

So the first disappearance of his identity occurs within the historical system of slavery.

### Second forgetting

Even the name he subsequently used is now unknown to his descendants.

So centuries—or generations—later, the family knows the extraordinary story but not the man's name.

That creates a very powerful paradox:

Everyone remembers what he supposedly accomplished, but nobody remembers who he was.

The same idea can be developed with the Spanish woman, although the mechanism is different.

Her identity wasn't necessarily erased through slavery.

Her name may have disappeared because of:

  - migration,
  - lack of surviving records,
  - exploitation,
  - undocumented movement,
  - family silence,
  - changing names,
  - or simply the ordinary fragility of historical records.

This gives the book a symmetry:

Two people. Two different journeys. Two different forms of disappearance. One family descended from both.

## 4. The book should not pretend to be a conventional biography

A major conclusion from the conversation was that you don't have enough documentary evidence to write a traditional biography of these individuals.

That is not necessarily a weakness.

Instead, the book can explicitly embrace the uncertainty.

There are effectively three levels of truth:

### A. Things you can document

For example:

  - Brazilian slavery existed.
  - The Paraguayan War happened.
  - Enslaved men participated in the war.
  - Military service could be connected with promises or grants of freedom in particular circumstances.
  - Spanish immigration to Brazil occurred.
  - Human trafficking and exploitation existed.
  - Lotteries existed.
  - Land ownership and agricultural wealth could be accumulated.
  - Certain historical events affected the region.

### B. Family traditions

For example:

  - "He was a slave."
  - "He fought in the war."
  - "He became free because of it."
  - "He won the lottery."
  - "He bought several farms."
  - "She was trafficked from Spain."
  - "She never really liked him."
  - "The children eventually lost everything."

These are potentially true, partially true, exaggerated, or distorted.

### C. Fictional reconstruction

Scenes can be created to make the historical world tangible.

For example:

  - A slave market.
  - An inspection.
  - A military camp.
  - A journey.
  - A farm.
  - A conversation between two characters.
  - A Spanish woman arriving in Brazil.
  - A family dinner.
  - A lottery ticket being purchased.

But the reader should understand that these scenes are reconstructions inspired by documented historical circumstances, rather than claims that you know these exact events occurred to your ancestors. With the blended style (section 5), this is said once, in an author's note, never by interrupting the story.

## 5. Narrative style: history blended into the story (decided)

**Decision:** no factual step-outs. The book is immersive historical fiction from the first page to the last. The history is never explained to the reader. The characters live it, and the reader catches glimpses of the reality without the story becoming too factual. The goal is to keep readers hooked.

### How the history gets in

  - **Through the senses:** prices, smells, objects, clothes and food, all inside the scene.
  - **Through characters:** each one carries a real fact (see the character bible). A soldier complaining about unpaid wages *is* the history of unpaid veterans.
  - **Through documents inside the story:** a bill of sale, an army roll, a letter of freedom (*carta de alforria*), a newspaper scrap, a lottery ticket, a letter from Spain. Short, real-looking, never explained.
  - **Through dialogue and rumour:** what people believed, including the things they got wrong.
  - **Through dramatic irony:** the reader senses what the characters cannot know.

### Rules

  - Never pause the plot to explain. A fact gets in only if a character needs it, sees it or suffers it.
  - Keep facts slightly out of focus: real enough to feel true, never a textbook.
  - Every chapter ends on a hook.
  - The line between truth and invention is drawn **once**, in an author's note, never inside the story.

### Example

In the market scene, the reader learns how slave markets worked without being told. A buyer guesses the man's age from his teeth, a price is chalked on a board, a clerk notes the tax due, and two men haggle as if over a horse. The young man says nothing. Everything the old "history" section would have explained is there in the scene.

## 5b. Tone: chance, contradictions, no judgement (decided)

Life in this book turns on chance. A sale, a cholera season, a recruiter's mood, a lottery draw, a card at the table: so many things can happen that change a life completely, and the characters mostly don't choose them.

  - **No morals pushed at the reader.** The book never tells anyone what to think of a character or a choice.
  - **Good and bad sides, and contradictions.** He saves a woman and enslaves her; he loves his sons and beats one; he hates masters and becomes one. She is a victim of trafficking and arrives with her own racism.
  - **No heroes and no villains, Black or white.** No one is perfect and no one is completely evil. Racism also runs among Black people (Africans and *crioulos*, *pretos* and *pardos*).
  - **Insight instead of judgement.** The reader sees the reasons, fears and hopes behind each decision, and is left to weigh it.
  - **Luck works both ways.** The same chance that makes the family rich undoes it (see the luck theme in the Character Bible).

## 6. The opening scene

You proposed starting the entire book with a fictional scene in the slave trade.

The idea was that the reader would initially believe they're simply entering a historical story.

Two slave buyers—or perhaps a buyer and seller—are examining an enslaved person.

One notices his teeth.

Something along the lines of:

"Look at this one. Good teeth."

The other examines him.

"Strong too."

They discuss his value.

The scene would contain small, historically plausible details about the environment, people, clothing, smells, sounds, commerce, etc.

The important point is not to glorify the slave owners.

The scene should instead expose the horrifying normality of treating a human being as merchandise.

The dialogue should be deliberately mundane.

That's what makes it disturbing.

They aren't necessarily portrayed as cartoonishly evil.

They are participating in a system in which discussing a person's monetary value is considered normal.

## 7. Why the teeth idea is interesting

You brought up a fascinating historical/scientific connection.

Slave traders could inspect physical characteristics—including teeth—as part of assessing an enslaved person's apparent health, age, and capacity for labour.

You then wanted to connect this with modern sports science.

The interesting observation is:

Centuries later, researchers are still finding associations between oral health and physical performance/athletic health.

The book could therefore make an unexpected transition:

The men examining him were not scientists. They did not understand modern physiology. They were certainly not measuring athletic performance.

And yet, in one crude sense, they were looking at something that modern researchers would eventually investigate scientifically: the relationship between oral health and the functioning of the human body.

The important caveat is that this should not become:

"The slave traders were right."

That's too simplistic and risks validating their reasoning.

Instead:

They were observing physical characteristics for a brutal commercial purpose. Modern science later studied some relationships between oral health, systemic health, and athletic performance using entirely different methods.

That distinction is important.

**With the blended style:** the modern sports-science link can't be stated in the text. It can be left as an echo instead. In the market they check his teeth to put a price on him. Years later, an army recruiter checks his teeth again, to see whether he can bite open paper cartridges. The same gesture, two masters, and no explanation. The modern science can go in the author's note. *(Teeth inspection of recruits: to verify for the Brazilian army.)*

## 8. The controversial aspect of the opening

The slave-market scene is potentially one of the book's most controversial elements.

There is a risk that fictionalising the slave trade could accidentally:

  - romanticise it,
  - make it entertaining in the wrong way,
  - over-dramatise historical reality,
  - or give invented dialogue the appearance of documented fact.

Without step-outs, the scene has to carry its own moral weight:

  - Tell it close to the enslaved man: what he sees, smells and refuses to show. Never through the buyers' eyes.
  - Keep the dialogue mundane. The horror is in how normal it is.
  - Draw the line between truth and invention once, in the author's note or as the book's epigraph:

**What follows is imagined. The system is not.**

## 9. Questions the story raises, answered inside the story

The old "research mode" becomes a plot engine. A scene raises a question in the reader's mind, and a later scene, character or document answers it. The reader is never handed a lecture.

| **A scene raises…** | **…and the story answers it through** |
| :-: | :-: |
| Someone examines an enslaved man's teeth. | The army recruiter's inspection later, and a bill of sale that lists him like livestock. |
| Someone talks about military recruitment. | The master and the recruiter negotiating the substitute deal over coffee, with him in the doorway. |
| The man receives his freedom. | The carta de alforria in his hands: its exact wording, and what it doesn't give him (no pay, no land, no name). |
| He buys land. | The notary scene: the deed, the insults hidden in it, and the price compared in his head with what his mother was sold for. |

This keeps the history feeling like part of the plot, not a textbook chapter.

## 11. Historical context we identified

The book could potentially cover a very broad period, roughly from the early/mid-19th century into the early Brazilian Republic.

Important contextual subjects include:

### Slavery in Brazil

  - Brazil was one of the largest destinations of enslaved Africans in the Atlantic world.
  - Slavery shaped the country's economy and social hierarchy.
  - Enslaved people worked in agriculture, domestic service, mining, cattle-related work, urban occupations and many other sectors.
  - Enslaved communities maintained family, cultural and religious traditions despite enormous disruption.

### Imperial Brazil

The world of:

  - Dom Pedro II
  - expanding agriculture
  - railways
  - telegraph
  - immigration
  - newspapers
  - photography
  - growing cities

This creates an interesting contradiction:

Brazil could be technologically modern while still maintaining slavery.

### Paraguayan War

The war from 1864 to 1870 could be one of the book's major turning points.

Questions to investigate:

  - How were enslaved men recruited?
  - What promises of freedom existed?
  - Were some purchased specifically for military service?
  - What happened to veterans?
  - What conditions did soldiers experience?
  - How many died from disease?
  - What happened when they returned?

### Abolition

The book could eventually reach:

  - the Lei do Ventre Livre (1871)
  - the Lei dos Sexagenários (1885)
  - the Lei Áurea (1888)

And then the abolition of slavery.

### The Republic

In 1889, Brazil became a republic.

That could provide another historical transformation during the lifetime of your ancestors or their children.

## 12. The Spanish woman is potentially an entire second mystery

Her story should not simply be treated as an appendix to his.

She could have her own narrative arc.

The questions are fascinating:

  - Where in Spain was she from?
  - Why did she leave?
  - Was she voluntarily emigrating?
  - Was she deceived?
  - Was she trafficked?
  - Who paid for her journey?
  - How did she travel?
  - Where did she arrive?
  - Did she speak Portuguese?
  - Could she read?
  - Did she have relatives in Brazil?
  - Did she retain contact with Spain?
  - Why did she marry him?
  - Was marriage her choice?
  - What did she think about him?
  - Why did she apparently dislike him?

And, critically:

What if the family story about her being trafficked turns out to be wrong?

That would not weaken the book.

It could make it stronger.

The investigation itself becomes the story.

## 13. Two parallel disappearances

This could become the central philosophical structure of the book.

### The man

His identity is obscured through slavery.

### The woman

Her identity disappears through migration, possible trafficking, lack of documentation, family memory, or another mechanism.

### Their descendants

Their children inherit their blood, property, stories and consequences—but eventually lose their names.

This gives you a powerful central question:

How can the people who created a family's future disappear from that family's memory?

And perhaps an even stronger one:

**What survives when the name doesn't?**

## 14. The family rise and fall

The book shouldn't stop when the man becomes wealthy.

That's actually only the middle.

The dramatic structure is:

**Nothing → slavery → freedom → fortune → family → wealth → death → collapse → disappearance**

The rise makes the eventual loss more dramatic.

The lottery is particularly useful because it creates an almost unbelievable reversal:

A man who was once legally considered property becomes the owner of land.

And then:

His descendants eventually lose everything.

That gives the story a kind of historical irony.

## 15. The children are important

The children represent the transition between worlds.

Their father experienced slavery.

They potentially grew up as landowners.

They may have experienced:

  - education,
  - social mobility,
  - wealth,
  - prejudice,
  - changing Brazilian society,
  - abolition,
  - the transition from Empire to Republic.

Then they lose the inheritance.

That creates an important question:

Did the children understand what their father had survived?

Perhaps they did.

Perhaps they didn't.

Perhaps family wealth eventually made slavery feel like a distant, embarrassing secret.

This is something worth investigating.

## 18. A possible recurring structure

Each chapter is built to keep the reader turning pages:

1.  **HOOK.** Open in motion, mid-action or mid-conversation.
2.  **SCENE.** A 5–15 page immersive scene.
3.  **TEXTURE.** The real world surfaces through a detail, a document or a character's line, never through explanation.
4.  **TURN.** Something changes: a sale, a death, a win, a betrayal.
5.  **CLIFFHANGER.** An unanswered question pulls the reader into the next chapter.

Once she enters the story, chapters alternate between his line and hers, cutting away at the high points.

## 20. The book's deepest theme

At first glance, the book is about:

slavery → war → freedom → lottery → wealth → family → loss.

But underneath that, it's about:

**memory → identity → erasure → inheritance.**

The central irony could be:

A man who apparently fought his way from slavery to freedom, acquired land and created a family could ultimately lose the one thing that should have been easiest to preserve: his name.

And alongside him:

A woman who crossed an ocean and became the mother of several children could disappear from history almost completely.

So the book isn't ultimately about finding two names.

It's about what it means when the names are gone but the consequences of their lives remain.

## 21. One thing we should be very careful about

There are several elements that are compelling precisely because they are uncertain:

  - "He was a slave."
  - "The war made him free."
  - "He won the lottery."
  - "He bought several farms."
  - "She was trafficked from Spain."
  - "She hated him."
  - "The family lost everything."

For the book, we should never quietly turn these into established facts.

A useful internal system would be:

| **Label** | **Meaning** |
| :-: | :-: |
| DOCUMENTED | We have evidence. |
| STRONGLY SUPPORTED | Multiple independent sources support it. |
| FAMILY TRADITION | Relatives reported it. |
| PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION | Historically consistent but unverified. |
| FICTIONAL | Deliberately invented for the narrative. |

That gives you enormous freedom to write dramatically without sacrificing intellectual honesty. These labels are for our working notes; the reader never sees them.

## 22. The most exciting possibility

The research itself could eventually change the story.

Imagine starting with:

My grandmother said he was a slave who fought in the war and won the lottery.

Then you investigate.

Perhaps you discover:

  - he really existed;
  - he really fought;
  - his freedom story is slightly different;
  - the lottery story is true but the amount is exaggerated;
  - the farms really existed;
  - the Spanish woman wasn't trafficked but arrived under another unusual circumstance;
  - or perhaps almost none of it can be verified.

Any of those outcomes can work.

Because the actual book becomes:

**What happens when a family tries to recover a history that was almost completely lost?**

That is much larger than a genealogy.

## The one idea I would preserve above everything else

I would make the missing names the book's recurring motif.

Not just:

"We don't know his name."

But:

He had a name once. Somebody knew it. Somebody called him by it. Somebody loved him, hated him, bought him, sold him, fought beside him, married him, or buried him. And now, somehow, we don't know what it was.

And the same question eventually applies to the Spanish woman.

That gives the book an emotional thread that can run from the first page to the last.
