# Alternatives: the Purchase (ch. 13)

Working notes from a conversation with Rafael (Nov 3, 2026). **Nothing here is decided**: Chapters remains the plan of record. Part 1 enriches the current arc and keeps everything it sets up; Part 2 holds different arcs that would rewrite parts of the story. Choose before ch. 13 is revised (chapter-revision).

## Why revisit it

Rafael's concern: the current Purchase reads too soft. He meets her, she tells him her story, and he fights Albino to save her. The reader roots for him, and the central question ("When chance hands you power, do you break the chain you inherited, or pass it on?") gets a kind answer. The aim is a Purchase that is more outrageous and more interesting, without turning him into a cartoon.

**Historical ground** \[DOCUMENTED; sources to add\]: *branqueamento*, "whitening" the population and the family, was a real ideal in late nineteenth-century Brazil, preached openly by the elites.

## How a critic would weigh it

Four tests, applied to the current version (he meets her, falls for her, saves her) and to a harder one (he buys her, also to whiten his family):

1. **Does it test the central question?** The current version mostly answers it kindly: he breaks the chain. The harder one makes the answer uncomfortable: he passes the chain on while telling himself he isn't. → harder version.
2. **Is the reader complicit?** In the current version we root for him. In the harder one we understand him and are still disturbed; the reader has to weigh it. → harder version.
3. **Does it pay off what came before?** Her chapter (ch. 11), the meetings at Albino's and Luzia's letters (ch. 13) all build toward a choice made out of feeling. → current version, unless the set-ups are kept (Part 1) or rebuilt (Part 2).
4. **Does it risk cliché?** The current version risks a sentimental rescue; a fully cold buyer risks a villain. **Mixed motives avoid both.**

Conclusion: the enrichment in Part 1 passes all four tests, because it keeps every set-up and adds the harder edge.

## Part 1. Enrichment to the current arc (keeps everything set up)

1. **Mixed motives** (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026). He saves her, and he also sets out, on purpose, to whiten his family: marrying a white woman so his children will be whiter. It is him **taking action**: he stops relying on luck and shapes his family's future himself.
    - **Pillar 2, luck**: Machiavelli's *virtù*. The Purchase stays a decision, not a draw (as the Concept says), but the decision is darker.
    - **The dark irony** (never pointed out): the way he takes control is by erasing himself from his descendants. Whiter children, and no name (the missing names).
    - **Mirror of Luzia**: she made her own luck by saving to buy her freedom; he makes his by buying whiteness. Same lesson, poisoned.
2. **He says it aloud, sincerely** (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026). Not with evil intent: from his side it is hope, a better future for his children. It is still racist. The outrage comes from the reader, never from the narrator (no verdicts).
    - Sample line (original, to refine): "My sons won't be stopped at any door."
3. **Bastião as the conscience.** Benedito tells him the plan, and Bastião hears what is underneath it: that a black face is the thing stopping them. He says the uncomfortable thing aloud, so the book doesn't have to. (A character judges; the book doesn't.)
    - Other roles considered for Bastião: **the broker** (the offer comes through him, which puts him into "everyone wants to be the master" too); **the silent witness** (he says nothing, stops coming to the house, and the friendship cools). The conscience is the strongest.
4. **The society voices it first.** The Pires, Albino or the town preach whitening before he does, so he is absorbing what the world taught him, not inventing it (pillar 3; handle-with-care: "everyone wants to be the master" never falls mainly on the formerly enslaved).
5. **The payoffs.**
    - Carmen later hears the same reasoning and realises why she was chosen; "You didn't save me, you own me" (ch. 16) cuts deeper.
    - Already in the plan, and strengthened: the Character Bible's "she was white (whitening, his view)" (ch. 13); the photograph where the photographer powders the boys' faces lighter (ch. 16).

**What Part 1 changes**: ch. 13 (the motive, his line to Bastião, the scene where he says it); one or two earlier moments where the society voices whitening (ch. 10 or 12, to place); a later moment where Carmen understands (ch. 15 or 16, to place); the Bible bullet on why he married her; the Concept's luck pillar, one line (the decision is also a calculation). Ch. 11 and the letters stay as they are.

## Part 2. Alternative arcs (would change the story)

1. **The offer comes to him.** The lottery makes him visible; someone (Albino, or a dealer) offers her like a good horse, and he just pays. No fight; the coldness is the outrage. Can carry Part 1's mixed motives.
    - Changes: **ch. 11** (Her Road) is rebuilt, since he does not know her before the offer and her story can no longer come from the meetings at Albino's (she might tell it after the Purchase, in the house, to a man who owns her); **ch. 10** (the first meeting and her price go); **ch. 13** (no fight with Albino; Luzia's letters can no longer be what turns the Purchase into a decision; Bastião's role changes); **ch. 15–16** (the marriage starts from nothing at all); the **Concept** (the luck pillar's "the Purchase is a decision, not a draw" must be rewritten).
2. **She is part of a deal.** The Pires or a creditor throws her in to close a land sale or a debt: she is bought like the cattle.
    - Changes: **ch. 13** and **ch. 14** (the Purchase moves into the land deal with the Pires or the Barão's lands); **ch. 11** and **ch. 10** as above; the Pires' role grows (no new characters without Rafael's approval).
3. **She bargains.** She sees him as her way out and sells herself on her own terms: complicit, not only a victim.
    - Changes: **ch. 11** (her arc gains agency), **ch. 13** (the handover: she is not surprised, she chose), **ch. 15–16** (her pride and the Pires' world read differently); the Bible's Carmen entry; "You didn't save me, you own me" may no longer fit.
