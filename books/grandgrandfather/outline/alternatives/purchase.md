# Alternatives: the Purchase (ch. 13)

Working notes from a conversation with Rafael (Nov 3, 2026). **Nothing here is decided**: Chapters remains the plan of record. Part 1 enriches the current arc and keeps everything it sets up; Part 2 holds different arcs that would rewrite parts of the story; Part 3 adds urgency, a race against the clock, and keeps the current set-ups. Choose before ch. 13 is revised (chapter-revision).

## Why revisit it

Rafael's concern: the current Purchase reads too soft. He meets her, she tells him her story, and he fights Albino to save her. The reader roots for him, and the central question ("When chance hands you power, do you break the chain you inherited, or pass it on?") gets a kind answer. The aim is a Purchase that is more outrageous and more interesting, without turning him into a cartoon.

**Historical ground** \[DOCUMENTED; sources to add\]: *branqueamento*, "whitening" the population and the family, was a real ideal in late nineteenth-century Brazil, preached openly by the elites.

## How a critic would weigh it

Four tests, applied to the current version (he meets her, falls for her, saves her) and to a harder one (he buys her, also to whiten his family):

1. **Does it test the central question?** The current version mostly answers it kindly: he breaks the chain. The harder one makes the answer uncomfortable: he passes the chain on while telling himself he isn't. → harder version.
2. **Is the reader complicit?** In the current version we root for him. In the harder one we understand him and are still disturbed; the reader has to weigh it. → harder version.
3. **Does it pay off what came before?** Her chapter (ch. 11), the meetings at Albino's and Luzia's letters (ch. 13) all build toward a choice made out of feeling. → current version, unless the set-ups are kept (Part 1) or rebuilt (Part 2).
4. **Does it risk cliché?** The current version risks a sentimental rescue; a fully cold buyer risks a villain. **Mixed motives avoid both.**

Conclusion: the enrichment in Part 1 passes all four tests, because it keeps every set-up and adds the harder edge.

## Part 1. Enrichment to the current arc (keeps everything set up)

1. **Mixed motives** (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026). He saves her, and he also sets out, on purpose, to whiten his family: marrying a white woman so his children will be whiter. It is him **taking action**: he stops relying on luck and shapes his family's future himself.
    - **Pillar 2, luck**: Machiavelli's *virtù*. The Purchase stays a decision, not a draw (as the Concept says), but the decision is darker.
    - **The dark irony** (never pointed out): the way he takes control is by erasing himself from his descendants. Whiter children, and no name (the missing names).
    - **Mirror of Luzia**: she made her own luck by saving to buy her freedom; he makes his by buying whiteness. Same lesson, poisoned.
2. **He says it aloud, sincerely** (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026). Not with evil intent: from his side it is hope, a better future for his children. It is still racist. The outrage comes from the reader, never from the narrator (no verdicts).
    - Sample line (original, to refine): "My sons won't be stopped at any door."
3. **Bastião as the conscience.** Benedito tells him the plan, and Bastião hears what is underneath it: that a black face is the thing stopping them. He says the uncomfortable thing aloud, so the book doesn't have to. (A character judges; the book doesn't.)
    - Other roles considered for Bastião: **the broker** (the offer comes through him, which puts him into "everyone wants to be the master" too); **the silent witness** (he says nothing, stops coming to the house, and the friendship cools). The conscience is the strongest.
4. **The society voices it first.** The Pires, Albino or the town preach whitening before he does, so he is absorbing what the world taught him, not inventing it (pillar 3; handle-with-care: "everyone wants to be the master" never falls mainly on the formerly enslaved).
5. **The payoffs.**
    - Carmen later hears the same reasoning and realises why she was chosen; "You didn't save me, you own me" (ch. 16) cuts deeper.
    - Already in the plan, and strengthened: the Character Bible's "she was white (whitening, his view)" (ch. 13); the photograph where the photographer powders the boys' faces lighter (ch. 16).

**What Part 1 changes**: ch. 13 (the motive, his line to Bastião, the scene where he says it); one or two earlier moments where the society voices whitening (ch. 10 or 12, to place); a later moment where Carmen understands (ch. 15 or 16, to place); the Bible bullet on why he married her; the Concept's luck pillar, one line (the decision is also a calculation). Ch. 11 and the letters stay as they are.

## Part 2. Alternative arcs (would change the story)

1. **The offer comes to him.** The lottery makes him visible; someone (Albino, or a dealer) offers her like a good horse, and he just pays. No fight; the coldness is the outrage. Can carry Part 1's mixed motives.
    - Changes: **ch. 11** (Her Road) is rebuilt, since he does not know her before the offer and her story can no longer come from the meetings at Albino's (she might tell it after the Purchase, in the house, to a man who owns her); **ch. 10** (the first meeting and her price go); **ch. 13** (no fight with Albino; Luzia's letters can no longer be what turns the Purchase into a decision; Bastião's role changes); **ch. 15–16** (the marriage starts from nothing at all); the **Concept** (the luck pillar's "the Purchase is a decision, not a draw" must be rewritten).
2. **She is part of a deal.** The Pires or a creditor throws her in to close a land sale or a debt: she is bought like the cattle.
    - Changes: **ch. 13** and **ch. 14** (the Purchase moves into the land deal with the Pires or the Barão's lands); **ch. 11** and **ch. 10** as above; the Pires' role grows (no new characters without Rafael's approval).
3. **She bargains.** She sees him as her way out and sells herself on her own terms: complicit, not only a victim.
    - Changes: **ch. 11** (her arc gains agency), **ch. 13** (the handover: she is not surprised, she chose), **ch. 15–16** (her pride and the Pires' world read differently); the Bible's Carmen entry; "You didn't save me, you own me" may no longer fit.

## Part 3. Urgency: the race against the clock (Rafael's favourite, Nov 3, 2026)

**The problem it solves** (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026): the Purchase is bland; nothing makes the reader lock in to find out what happens next. The only obstacles are Albino's prejudice and a rising price, and money solves both: nobody else wants her, and nothing happens if Benedito is late. Part 3 adds a **ticking clock** and a **real threat**, and keeps what the plan already sets up (the meetings, her story, the 4–5 months Albino hides her, Bastião's arrival in late November, the letters, the slow prize money). It combines with Part 1: the whitening motive is what tips his final choice.

### The engine: three things already in the plan

1. **Rich on paper, not in hand.** The prize is claimed through the agent's correspondent in Rio and takes months (ch. 12). The whole town knows he is rich, and he cannot pay. Whatever happens to her before the money arrives, money cannot stop.
2. **Albino's crime.** Selling a free woman is a crime (Criminal Code of 1830, art. 179), so Albino can only sell her quietly, and fears Benedito might steal her (ch. 13). After the win, hiding her for months is a growing risk for him.
3. **The steamer as a clock.** Corumbá lives by the river steamers: up from Montevideo and Asunción, on to Cuiabá (7–12 days each way), and back down. A boat that goes upriver comes back down in two to four weeks, on a known day.

### The story, step by step

1. **The win (Day 0, July 1877), as now** (ch. 12). **Benedito's first offer, the next morning (Day 1)**: her own price, **one conto**, the number she told him (ch. 10–11), on his word and a note against the prize, since he has no cash. Albino refuses: not to a Black man, and not on paper. From that day he hides her (ch. 13). **What the reader does not see yet**: that same week Albino does two things. He writes downriver to Madame Angélique in Buenos Aires, who sold her to him, offering her back for quick cash, so he can be rid of her and of the risk. And he asks his friend the lottery agent, a regular at his tavern, to sit on the claim: the letter to the Rio correspondent is never sent.
2. **The waiting (Aug–Nov), as now, with Benedito pushing.** He comes back to Albino again and again, and each time the price is higher: **two contos** (a few weeks later), then **"five, in cash, on the table"**: a price Albino names only because he knows Benedito cannot pay it (Albino's own price to Alvarado, below, is far lower). He tries to see her and cannot; Lucília is kept away. He presses the agent, who has excuses: the mail, Rio, papers. In between, he waits on luck, as he always has (and still buys tickets).
3. **The steamer from downriver (c. late Nov 1877).** Bastião arrives on it, with the letters (ch. 12): the reunion, the letters, Luzia's death (Interlude; read at the start of ch. 13). **On the same boat is Alvarado**, the gancho who brought her from Vigo and was paid by Madame Angélique at the port in Buenos Aires (ch. 11): Angélique has sent him to collect "the virgin from Vigo", to take her down to Buenos Aires when the steamer comes back down from Cuiabá. On the way, drunk, Alvarado brags about it on deck; Bastião, a runaway who has learned to listen to white men who think Black men don't, hears it without knowing what it means.
4. **The click.** That night, after the letters, grief, and Benedito tells Bastião the story of the girl at Albino's. Bastião remembers the man on the boat. "What was his name?" "Alvarado." Benedito knows the name: she told it to him, at Albino's, in her story (ch. 11). **The clock starts**: two to four weeks, until the steamer comes back down. His mother is dead; he will not wait on luck again (ch. 13, as now: he decides).
5. **The race for the money.** He tries to raise cash before the boat returns, and every door closes:
    - The agent offers to buy his claim at a ruinous discount, cash "in a few weeks".
    - Nobody lends to a Black man, however rich on paper.
    - Firmino offers him his savings, every coin (a small, true beat: his loyalty, ch. 14–15). It is not enough.
    - **The surprise: Seu Bento, who carries the agent's mail to the steamer, realises the claim letter never left.** Benedito understands that his money has been held hostage all along, and by whom.
6. **Benedito's last offer, before her move.** With the clock running and no cash, he goes to Albino one more time and offers **everything he will have**: half the prize, ten contos, on paper. Albino almost wavers (it is a fortune), and refuses: on paper is worth nothing if the agent never pays (and Albino knows why he won't), and a sale to Benedito would put Albino's name on a deal in the open; Alvarado will take her out of Brazil with no trace. Benedito leaves knowing that only force is left.
7. **Her move** (her agency; option C folded in). Lucília, forbidden to talk to her, risks it and tells her everything: the lottery, Benedito rich, and who the visitor in the house is. She sees Alvarado in Albino's house and recognises him: the man of the letters, the ship and the port. Terrified, she does the one thing that ruins his business: **she tells Alvarado she is no longer a virgin**, that the Black man had her, long ago. A lie, and a weapon: her price collapses (in ch. 11 her whole value is her virginity). Alvarado and Albino quarrel over money. **It backfires**: Albino, furious, carries out his old threat. If she is worth only a brothel price, Alvarado will take her for a brothel, and he will move her to the port the night before the steamer leaves, out of sight of the town. (No sexual violence shown; the threat is what matters. Handle with care.)
8. **The warning.** Lucília gets word to Benedito: which night, which path to the port.
9. **The night by the river.** Benedito and Bastião wait on the path. Albino's and Alvarado's men, knives against capoeira (Kalunga's roda, ch. 3). They take her. **The cost**: Bastião is cut, and Benedito sews the wound with Luzia's hands (the healer, ch. 2, 6). One of Alvarado's men goes into the dark river, and nobody knows whether he lived. Like Damião in the burning village (ch. 9), chance decides, and Benedito never learns which.
10. **The morning: the choice.** She is hidden at Seu Bento's. **In law she is a free woman.** He could give her money and a passage downriver, and let her go. But Albino has his own weapon: a Black man has abducted his white "niece", and, the girl herself says, dishonoured her. Benedito remembers the jail in Corumbá (ch. 10). Benedito has his: the held-back claim, a free woman sold (art. 179), and Alvarado in town as a witness. **Mutual blackmail, settled as a deal**: two contos, "double what you ever dreamed of getting for her": one for her, and one for silence; paid when the prize arrives; Albino signs a paper, as her uncle and guardian, consenting to her marriage (she is about 19, and under 21 a guardian's consent was needed \[to verify\]); and Albino has the agent send the claim at last (as now, "Albino uses his influence to ease it", but now we know why). Alvarado goes back down on the steamer with his deposit returned and a little more, paid by Albino for his silence; on the quay he leaves Benedito one sentence, "Ask her about Buenos Aires", a small poison that never quite goes away.
11. **Why he pays instead of setting her free.** He tells Bastião, sincerely, as hope: married to a white woman, his sons will not be stopped at any door (Part 1). Bastião hears what is underneath it. **He never asks her.** He rescued her by force, and then bought the paper that makes her his: the central question at its sharpest, the chance to break the chain in his hands for one night, and passed on.
12. **The handover, changed.** She already knows he is rich (Lucília), so she is not surprised that "a rich man" has her. Her question stays: "So what am I going to be? A maid, a helper, or what?" "You will be my wife." She says nothing: she is afraid to challenge it, and now she knows the alternative was a brothel downriver.

### Who wants silence, and why (Rafael, Nov 3, 2026)

Nobody involved wants the story to spread, each for a different reason, and that shapes everything: why nobody goes to the police, why the fight happens at night out of town, why the end is a quiet deal, and why the town never learns the truth.

- **Carmen**: she does not want the town to know she was trafficked, sold, kept for a buyer, nor the lie she told about her virginity, which would be her ruin in Corumbá. Her lie was meant only for Alvarado, inside the house.
- **Albino**: a respectable white man with some influence; any link to trafficking (and art. 179) would ruin him. That is why he sells her far away, why Benedito's open offers frighten him, and why he can threaten Benedito with the abduction charge but never use it in public.
- **Alvarado**: does not care: a foreigner with nothing to lose in Corumbá, leaving on the next boat. His silence is the one for sale: he can threaten Albino ("your niece came from Angélique's house") and Benedito ("your bride came from a Buenos Aires brothel"). Albino pays him off; his last sentence on the quay is the threat he keeps.
- **Benedito**: wants to marry her without gossip: a respectable white wife is the whole point of the whitening (Part 1), and a story of brothels and blood on the river path would destroy it. So he never goes to the police, fights only in the dark, and pays for a paper instead of exposing Albino.
- **Bastião**: a runaway travelling on Tonico's pass; any police attention could end his freedom. He fights masked by the night, and leaves the town's eyes as soon as he can.
- **Lucília**: risks her work and her place by warning them.
- **The result**: everyone keeps quiet, so the town gets only the surface, "the lucky Black man took Albino's niece into his house before the wedding" (ch. 13, as now). The real story is told by nobody, ever (pillar 1, what is withheld). Mutual silence also holds the deal together: each side has something on the other.

### Smart surprises (what the reader does not see coming)

- Bastião, the friend from before everything, arrives carrying the threat with him, on the same boat, without knowing it.
- The name the reader already knows from ch. 11 comes back: Alvarado.
- The slow money was never slow by chance: Albino held it hostage. Seu Bento, the honest friend, finds out by carrying the mail.
- Carmen's lie: the move that should save her makes things worse, and then gives Albino his weapon against Benedito.
- Albino turns down ten contos: the reader learns that for him, silence and keeping her from a Black man are worth more than money.
- The fight is not the end: the rescue becomes a purchase, and the free woman needs a paper to be safe.

### Plot-hole checks

- **Coincidence (Bastião and Alvarado on one boat):** not chance. Both answer letters sent in the weeks after the win: Albino's to Buenos Aires, and the news in the papers that reached Tonico (ch. 12, Interlude). Both take the next steamer up. The book's chance is visible, but here it is cause and effect.
- **Why didn't Albino sell her earlier, to a white buyer in Corumbá?** He wanted one big, quiet sale (ch. 11), and few men in Corumbá could pay it without talk; after the win, the risk made a quick sale far away better.
- **Why didn't Benedito steal her or go to the police during the months?** She is hidden and guarded; a Black man's word counts for little and he has no proof (ch. 13); and he is waiting on luck, the habit he only breaks when the letters come.
- **Who knows what:** Benedito knows Alvarado's name from her story (ch. 11); Bastião knows the boast, not its meaning; Seu Bento knows the mail; Lucília knows the house. Each piece comes from someone who could know it.
- **Why can't he just set her free?** She is free in law already; what he lacks is protection against Albino's charge. The paper buys that; he chooses to make it a marriage.
- **Albino's motive** (current open options: profit, fear of theft, not letting a negro have her): all three now drive his actions, plus fear of exposure.
- **Why Albino refuses ten contos but accepts two:** the ten were on paper, from a man whose money Albino himself was holding back, and a sale in the open; after the fight, with Alvarado's man in the river and the girl in Benedito's hands, Albino's choice is two contos and silence, or nothing and the risk of exposure.
- **Why nobody calls the police:** every party has something to hide (Who wants silence).
- **Alvarado** leaves on the steamer, and is not seen again. The man in the river stays unknown (as Damião).
- **Her lie** stays in the town's mouth: the scandal of the "lucky Black man's" bride grows (ch. 13), and it can follow them into the Pires' salon (ch. 15).

- **Interlude**: Bastião's voyage up the river can show the drunk man on deck, without naming him.
- **Ch. 13**: a new middle: the click, the race, her move, the night by the river, the deal; the showdown at Albino's becomes the deal after the fight; the handover changes slightly; the Purchase stays a decision.
