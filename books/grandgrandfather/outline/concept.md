## The book in one paragraph

A family story told by the author's grandmother: her grandfather was born enslaved, went to the Paraguayan War, came back free, won the lottery, bought farms and "bought a Spanish girl" whom he married; after his death the sons lost everything gambling. The book is immersive historical fiction built around those few remembered facts, with the real world of nineteenth-century Brazil around them. Much is invented; the history is not.

## The story in six movements

1.  **Bahia (1846–1865).** Born enslaved on a sugar estate, son of Luzia, a healer and singer who trusts luck and the orixás, in the same season as the master's heir and in the shadow of the master's unnamed pardo son: three boys, three colours. A childhood with laughter as well as cruelty, a friendship with the master's son, his father dying in his hands and the sale of his mother; a youth of capoeira and a sour rivalry he finally wins. He bets on the war as his door to freedom.
2.  **War (1865–1870).** The Paraguayan War: comrades, cholera, atrocities on all sides, and a man who stops keeping his head down.
3.  **Free, not equal (1870–c. 1877).** Freedom in Corumbá is poorer and harder than he dreamed. He keeps buying lottery tickets.
4.  **Her road (c. 1858–1877).** A girl from Galicia is deceived and trafficked through Buenos Aires to Corumbá, kept untouched to be sold. The two lines alternate and meet.
5.  **Fortune (c. 1877–1889).** The lottery win; his mother's last letter; he buys the woman and marries her, saving her and owning her at once. A cattle farm called Liberdade, good years, then pride, distrust and the neighbours' slow manipulation.
6.  **The fall (1890s).** His memory fails, his family and the neighbours doubt him, and after his death the sons lose everything at cards. The frame returns to the grandmother, who remembers the story but not his name.

## Key ideas

  - **The missing names.** Neither he nor the woman from Spain is ever named; he whispers his true name to her on his deathbed and the reader never hears it. Everyone remembers what he did, nobody remembers who he was. What survives when the name doesn't?
  - **Family and descent: who is a father, whose son?** The book is about family and descent, and slavery breaks both: mothers sold away from their sons, a master's son whom nobody names (Ezequiel), and a man used to father children for a fazenda, a father of too many to be father of any (Pata Seca, through the son Benedito meets in the war, ch. 4–7). Benedito searches for his mother; his own sons never know who he was; the author searches for a great-great-grandfather whose name is lost. The book itself is a descendant's search (decided Oct 30, 2026).
  - **Four forces in every scene: luck, freedom, violence, money.** Luck sees no race, so he trusts it; freedom is not the happy ending; violence is the first language he learned and he passes it on; money is freedom, and is not.
  - **Liberdade, where nobody is free.** The farm that looks like the happy ending traps everyone in it, each in a different way.
  - **Mirrors.** The gossip says he is the master's son and it is false; Ezequiel is, and nobody says it. The enslaved man becomes a master; the free woman loses her freedom; the boyhood friendship across the colour line returns as the white "friends" who ruin his sons.
  - **What passes down.** Faith in luck, violence, the fear of losing everything, music and healing (the one good inheritance) pass from Luzia to him, to his sons and on to the grandmother. Repeated lines show it ("Why does he have to be so angry?"); nobody explains it. Some chains break.
  - **Memory you cannot trust.** The grandmother's fog in the frame, his failing memory at the end: the reader is not always sure who is right.

## How it is told

  - **History blended in, never explained.** No factual step-outs; the real world enters through objects, documents, dialogue and rumour. The line between truth and invention is drawn once, in an author's note.
  - **Chance, contradictions, no judgement.** No heroes and no villains, Black or white; the reader sees the reasons behind each choice and weighs it.
  - **Voices.** The chapters are in close third person, following him and later her. The prologue and epilogue are in the author's own voice (AUTHOR-MAIN), about his grandmother.
  - **Chapter shape.** Hook, scene, texture, turn, cliffhanger. His chapters and hers alternate from Part III until they meet.
  - **Tone range.** From a funny, mischievous childhood to the war's horror; humour keeps him from being only a victim.

## Handle with care

  - Scenes of slavery are told from inside the person being sold or used, never through the buyers' eyes; sexual violence is never shown directly. Exceptions chosen by Rafael: Ezequiel's goiabeira night (ch. 2) and first whipping (ch. 3) are explicit; they are cathartic character set-ups.
  - The hardships of freedom are his lived experience, never the narrator's verdict (the "better off enslaved" argument belonged to the planters).
  - Family traditions are never quietly turned into facts. Working notes use truth labels (DOCUMENTED, STRONGLY SUPPORTED, FAMILY TRADITION, PLAUSIBLE RECONSTRUCTION, FICTIONAL); the reader never sees them.
