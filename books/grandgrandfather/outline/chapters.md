# Chapters

Oct 2, 2026 · Rafael

Working list of chapters and the stories planned for each. Working names, for planning only and never in the book text: Benedito (him) and Carmen (the woman from Spain). Built from the Character Bible arc. Style: immersive fiction, history blended into the scenes (no factual step-outs). Every chapter ends on a hook. Neither he nor the woman from Spain is ever named.

Structure note: Part IV (her story) does not run as a block. Her chapters alternate with his from Part III onwards, cutting away at high points, until the two lines meet in "The Purchase".

Open option: a short prologue and epilogue in the grandmother's voice, telling the story to a grandchild. Open option for Part II: tell much of the war through his letters to his mother (see chapter 5). Catharsis points (Oct 10, 2026; Tarantino-style release, rationed, see NARR-MAIN; to revisit): ch. 3 (he beats Ezequiel), Interlude (Tonico banishes Ezequiel), ch. 6 (Tuiuti; Curuzu), ch. 10 (he stops Damião), ch. 20 (Albino's house).

## PART I — BAHIA (1846–1865)

### 1\. Good Teeth

  - When / where: before and around his birth (1846) · Salvador and the Recôncavo
  - Stories:
      
      - Opening scene: buyers examine teeth, guess ages, chalk prices, haggle as over a horse. One buyer, a dirty old man with stinking breath, inspects her in a way no buyer needs to: his hands stay too long, his eyes go where a buyer's have no need to go. She is about 14 and does not fully understand it; she will understand it over time, each time she is sold to a man who looks at her that way, the last being Coronel Menezes. Tired from the voyage, she only wants it to be over. Because she is beautiful she is taken out of the group and valued differently; they clean and dress her apart from the others (decided Oct 10, 2026). Told from inside the silence of the one being sold (Luzia, arriving from Africa as a girl); never explicit.
      - Luzia's life: taken from the Bight of Benin as a girl; a ganhadeira in Salvador's streets; the Malê Revolt (1835) and the fear after it; the money she keeps above the jornal and then gives away as easily as it came: gifts, offerings to the orixás, help for anyone who asks. She trusts luck and the gods, but not naively: she believes the good she does comes back to her as luck. As a young woman she does not yet fully know what it is to be a slave: the street gives her a half-freedom. Two moments teach her: a man whipped in a square after the Malê Revolt (1835), and the night the master first calls her to his room. Even then she never manages to save; she keeps helping others, believing the luck will come back. As a girl in Yorubaland she learned healing and ritual singing together (leaves and remedies are 'woken' with chants). Her voice is extraordinary: when she sings in the street, selling, people stop to listen. Through her we see the street economy: the jornal she owes the master every day, the surplus she spends or gives away, and the African savings circles (juntas) that pooled money to buy freedom, which she never joins: what the orixás want will come.
      - How she meets Inácio (c. 1844–45): he is badly hurt in the boiling house (a burn from the kettles, or his arm caught in the crushing rollers). The estate leaves him to die; Luzia, already known for her remedies, nurses him back for weeks. Love grows out of the healing.
      - The heart of the chapter, told mostly in dialogue: a love that must hide from the master. Inácio's unspoken suspicion (he is afraid to ask: he has seen too many men whipped, some to death, and the fear is real) and helpless anger at the master's nights; Luzia's choice to stay silent to protect him, and later their son; quarrels and tenderness in whispers in the senzala. She chose Inácio; the master chose her. Both are true, and the chapter lets them sit side by side.
      - The master's nights: he forces himself on Luzia. She is not the only one: nobody in the senzala knows which women go to the big house; they whisper and try to guess. One of the house maids knows. She likes Luzia, whose remedies once saved her son, and spreads false rumours to cover her (the same maid later delivers Luzia's baby). Luzia bears it in silence and hides it from Inácio, who only suspects. Never shown directly; the reader understands through what is not said. Historical ground: sexual exploitation of enslaved women by masters was common and went unpunished, and a child followed the mother's condition: born enslaved, whoever the father was.
      - Glimpses of daily slave life: the senzala, the work bell before dawn, rations of manioc flour and dried meat, Sunday plots and markets, punishments everyone is made to watch.
      - Ezequiel (decided Oct 10, 2026): about 12 to 15 years before the protagonist, another enslaved woman on the estate (unnamed) gave birth to a lighter-skinned boy, Ezequiel: the master's son. Everyone can see it in his skin; nobody says it aloud. The master never recognises him. Ezequiel knows he is a bastard; he resents the master and can do nothing about it, so he comes to believe he is better than the other enslaved. This is the seed of the capitão do mato who hunts runaways and takes pleasure in it. For Luzia, his mother is a warning: a child with no father.
      - The tension of the chapter (decided Oct 10, 2026): Luzia is pregnant and does not know who the father is. She counts the moons, as she has for a hundred other women, but this time the count cannot decide. The master is white, Inácio is Black: she will know for sure when the child is born. She alone carries the question: will he be Black or pardo? If pardo, she becomes Ezequiel's mother: her child will have no father, and she fears Inácio will abandon her. She can tell no one. The birth releases it. He is born all black (preto): "Our main character will end all gossip once he is born all black" (decided Oct 23, 2026).
      - Tonico's birth (decided Oct 10, 2026): in the same season, a few weeks after the protagonist, Dona Mariana gives birth to Tonico, and the master finally has an official descendant. The estate celebrates. Ezequiel, a teenager, watches: Tonico is the son he cannot be, and he hates him from that day. This sets the rivalry between Ezequiel and the two boys. Three boys on one estate: Tonico has a white master father, the protagonist a Black enslaved father, Ezequiel no father, only rumours never recognised.
      - Chapter ends: his birth (1846). Luzia delivers many children in the senzala; this time another woman delivers hers: the house maid who covers for her. The child is dark: Inácio's. The fear breaks, for her and for the reader; Inácio's suspicions go with it, and, relieved, he stops thinking about it and is taken up with his son. The estate book counts the child as property; an ink drop falls where the name should be. Weeks later Luzia is ordered to nurse the master's newborn heir, Tonico: the two boys become milk brothers.
      - Decided Oct 7, 2026 (from the Book Draft scene map): a bridge from Salvador to the estate: c. 1843, after her Salvador owner dies, she is sold to Coronel Menezes's engenho as a healer and midwife. Inácio's accident is a burn in the boiling house (a kettle falls). The house maid who covers for Luzia (unnamed) delivers the baby. Revised Oct 10, 2026: Luzia counts the moons, as she has for a hundred other women, but the count cannot settle who the father is; only the birth does. The estate book lists him as a male child with a value; an ink drop falls where the name should be.
      - IMPORTANT, the law in Luzia's time: the law of 7 November 1831 (Lei Feijó) banned the slave trade and declared every African landed in Brazil after that date free. It was never enforced ("para inglês ver", "for the English to see"): about 750,000 Africans were smuggled in afterwards and only about 11,000 were seized. Proposal: Luzia lands in Salvador c. 1832–33, aged about 14, just after the ban. By law she is a free woman; she is enslaved anyway, and every sale of her (including the one in Chapter 2) is a sale of someone the law says is free. Nobody on the estate will ever say it. This fits her being a young woman during the Malê Revolt (1835). After the revolt, Bahia's Law nº 9 (1835) puts Africans under suspicion, taxes and deportation; she learns to keep her head down.
      - Sources: Lei de 7 de novembro de 1831, its non-enforcement and the 750,000 / 11,000 figures (historian Bruno Rodrigues de Lima, via DW / Poder360): <https://www.poder360.com.br/brasil/ha-190-anos-brasil-ganhava-primeira-lei-contra-escravidao-dw/> 
      - Further reading: Beatriz Mamigonian, Africanos livres: a abolição do tráfico de escravos no Brasil (Companhia das Letras, 2017).

### 2\. The Engenho

  - When / where: 1846–1858, age 0–12 · sugar estate in the Recôncavo · Tone: funny. There is suffering in this childhood, but also a lot of laughter and adventure (the pranks, the stick fights, the river). He is a mischievous, quick, cheeky boy, not only a victim. The hard moments (Inácio's death, Luzia's sale) land harder because we have laughed with him first.
  - Stories:
      
      - Luzia and her son: her singing, admired by everyone on the estate (even the master's family stops to listen). She teaches him her Yoruba songs; her voice will be his strongest memory of her, and he will sing her songs in the trenches and near the end of his life; she teaches him to keep his head down and to trust luck; she tries to keep what she earns, but it always goes to someone in need, and repeats one promise to him: the orixás will open the way, and one day they will both be free. The freedom never comes. The master now keeps her near the estate, but on market days she still works ao ganho in Cachoeira, selling sweets and remedies, and he carries her tray.
      - Luzia hides the master's nights from her son: a closed door, an excuse, a calm face in the morning. The reader understands through what the boy half-sees.
      - Dona Mariana teaches him letters "as a game", 
      - **Before the estrangement: he and Tonico.** As small boys they are true friends. They fish in the river, race to the mill, trade a slingshot for a song, play capoeira and soldiers, and fight with sticks for swords, like real fighters; he teaches Tonico to swim, and Tonico once takes a beating from his father to cover for him. Their favourite prank, pure mischief: they hide somewhere high and pee on whoever passes below, then roll about laughing. The grown-ups shout and shake their fists, but nobody can chase the master's son. Their best target is Ezequiel, the capitão do mato, the master's unrecognised son, hard on the enslaved and harder still on Tonico and the boy: they pee on him and mock him, knowing he can do nothing while Tonico is there. But that day some of it also lands in one of the troughs where the cane juice runs from the mill to the boiling house. The batch is spoiled, and the mill has to stop for hours while everything is cleaned. Tonico runs away, leaving the boy behind. The master punishes him severely, and the boy thinks: "Why does he have to be so angry?" (his own sons will think the same words about him). Another day, with other children, they throw sand at people passing by. The angry passers-by go to a white woman who works as a servant on the estate (role to confirm: Tonico's nanny?) and ask: "Is this your boy?" She sees Tonico and says, proudly, that he is. Then they complain: she should bring up her children better, look what they have just done. She has to clean up after them (to confirm: wash the sandy passers-by, or bathe the boys?). Tonico gets away; when the story reaches the master, it is the boy who is punished. The mark in his eye: once, when Tonico is helping him clear land, their play gets rough and Tonico hits him in the face with a piece of wood. An accident, not malice; a mark stays in the eye itself for life. Real, innocent mutual respect between a white boy and a Black boy who do not yet know the world forbids it. Write these scenes warm and unforced, so that their loss hurts later.
      - **Ezequiel's secret** (c. 1846–47; Ezequiel, the capitão do mato, as a boy; his point of view, an important character set-up; decided Oct 23, 2026). He was never told who his father was. Always being met with silence from his mom every time he asked. After a while she ordered him to never ask, being very angry when he did. She would never say. When he was 12/13 and became a bit more aware, he started to notice the night walks his mom always did from time to time. Up until then he never even bothered much about where she was going, but on one specific night he got curious. His mom left without saying anything; she thought he was probably asleep. He wasn't. He decided to follow her, hiding behind bushes in the dark. He was good at it – could move quickly without a sound – a skill that would come in handy in his future as capitão do mato. He proceeds to follow her and realises she is going into the landlord's house. She enters, disappears. He notices lamps are turned on in one of the rooms. Quickly runs and climbs a goiabeira tree next to its window. From there he can see through the window panes the landlord touching his mom. First her breasts, then her ass. He slaps her and she falls. The man orders her to undress and brings her to the bed. Ezequiel watches for a while. He hears his mom, he hears the violence the landlord seemed to enjoy. She never cries. She remains almost emotionless. That seemed to infuriate the landlord, who would slap and beat her in order to hear sounds out of her mouth. Ezequiel can't watch for long. He runs. He cries, he is angry. He can't understand. But he knows he needs to be silent: other negroes had been violently beaten for challenging the landlord. He himself had been punished a few times. Fear was real. The anger had to be repressed. The image of his mom being assaulted and raped had to be repressed. He calms down. Gets home. Lays down. And it finally clicks. He knows who his father is. (This is the moment he knows; ch. 1 shows what everyone sees in his skin. Written explicitly, following Rafael's words: "This moment is cathartic – make it explicit – follow my words.")
      - **After that night** (c. 1847–52). The time passes by. He starts noticing how the landlord's son has privileges, how his life was easier. He resents that. He should have that as well – he is also the son of the landlord. He tries to show he is better. He strives at everything. Shows off in front of the landlord as much as possible, trying to differentiate himself from the other negroes, because perhaps he would be treated better if he could show that to his unspoken father. The landlord makes use of that. He stimulates that, often saying "I always knew you were special" or "different". He profited from this. The boy started to tell on his fellow slaves: tell who maybe cheated work a bit, who skipped a shift, who got something extra to eat. Petty things. All to show his father he was special, he was worthy of being his son. He gets rewards for it – in the end his freedom: c. 1852, at about 18, the landlord frees him and makes him capitão do mato – but these are all ways the landlord manipulates him into doing what he wants. Never the word "son". This path takes him into becoming a violent and proud capitão do mato. (Tonico is his half-brother, which gives the pee prank its edge; Ezequiel is about 20 at the prank, c. 1854. Mirror: the gossip says the protagonist is the landlord's son and it is false; Ezequiel really is, and nobody says it.)
      - **The first whipping** (c. 1852, soon after he becomes capitão do mato; Ezequiel's point of view). Once he becomes capitão do mato there is his most cathartic moment. He catches a negro stealing. The negro refuses to say anything. He keeps screaming for him to confess. He remains silent. He slaps him and screams. Nothing. Silence. He threatens him with the whip. Nothing. He takes him to the tree and ties him, and gives his last warning. He is tense, a bit excited, but mostly angry: it is the first time he has to show his authority, to finally show he is special and not the same. He whips the negro on the hand, and the negro finally makes a noise. The noise hits his brain back in time, to when the landlord slapped his mother in that room while he watched from the goiabeira. His whole body gets goosebumps. He hits again: a moan, stronger goosebumps. He doesn't understand and doesn't care. The more he whips, the more excited he gets, like never before. He whips harder every time. He remembers that room. But he is the landlord now. He whips. He only stops when someone holds him. He is wet in sweat. He didn't even notice how many times he whipped. He doesn't know how much time passed. He gets out of the trance. He feels his pants tight around the crotch. His hands hold the whip with extreme strength. His whole body is then released. Someone holds him and says: "Stop, he won't be able to work if you continue." (Someone: unnamed – "Not important. The focus is Ezequiel.") He looks at the negro tied to the tree. He whipped his arms the whole time. His hand was almost detached. The negro had an amputation later on. Ezequiel was now the most brutal capitão do mato. Everyone feared him, especially because he seemed to enjoy the process. Some say his dick was harder than the tree the negro was tied to. Written explicitly, following Rafael's words. Pays off in the Interlude: the man with the amputated hand will appear to try to revenge himself when Tonico sends Ezequiel away. (Open, "let's not decide that now": whether the boy, about 6, is among those who watch.)
      - Tonico, the master's son, his "milk brother": they grow up playing together. When Luzia is to be sold, he begs Tonico to persuade his father. Tonico tries, or says he did; he is a boy and it changes nothing. He can't forgive either of them: he hates the master, and the friendship with Tonico goes cold. Estranged: not enemies, not friends.
      - His father: Inácio (working name), an enslaved boiling-house worker and Luzia's companion, the man he calls father. He dies in the cholera epidemic of 1855, when the boy is 9 (about 10,000 dead in Salvador alone; the enslaved and freed died out of proportion). Luzia saved him once; this time she cannot. At his death she sings over him the saddest song the boy has ever heard. His first wound. (Option: tell the cholera only as a memory, in a few lines, so it does not stretch the chapter.) The song follows him for the rest of his life (to place: the trenches, Acosta Ñu, his own death). Our main character ends all gossip once he is born all black: preto, Inácio's son beyond doubt. (Ezequiel, pardo, is the master's son that nobody names.)
      - At the mill with his father: during the harvest he spends days beside Inácio in the mill and boiling house. He learns the whole chain of sugar: cutting cane, the crushing rollers, the boiling kettles, the purging house, the sugar loaves packed in crates for Salvador. He learns the harvest calendar, who gives orders (master, overseer, sugar master), how many hands each step needs and what a crate is worth. Without knowing it, he is learning to run a farm, the seed of Jacadigo.
      - Luzia's skill: she is the estate's healer and midwife. She knows Yoruba plants, sets bones, delivers every child born in the senzala, and nursed the sick through the cholera of 1855 (she could not save Inácio). Even the master's family calls for her. He follows her everywhere and learns: which plants stop bleeding or fever, how to clean and bind a wound, to boil water, to keep the sick drinking. He will carry this knowledge into the war.
      - Luzia grows older and the master turns his attention to a younger enslaved woman. When his debts come due, he sells Luzia, not the young one, despite everything she knows. The estate loses its healer to pay a creditor.
      - Chapter ends: Luzia is sold south to pay the master's debts. He stays. His second wound.
      - IMPORTANT, was the sale legal? In 1858, yes, and that is the cruelty. No law yet stopped a master from selling a mother away from her 12-year-old son. Only in 1869 (Decreto 1.695) did Brazil ban separating husband and wife, or children under 15 from their parents, in any sale (and ban public slave auctions); the Free Womb Law of 1871 lowered the age to 12. Had the master waited eleven years, this sale would have been illegal. The deeper illegality is Luzia's own: if she landed after November 1831 (see Chapter 1), the law already said she was free, and the master is selling a free woman. Nobody checks.
      - The laws that shape these years: the Eusébio de Queirós Law (1850) really ends the Atlantic trade. No new Africans arrive, prices rise, and the coffee planters of the South start buying enslaved people from the declining sugar Northeast. That is why a debt-ridden Recôncavo master can sell Luzia south at a good price. (Bahia only taxed slaves leaving the province from 1862, after her sale.) In Salvador, a city law of 1857 forces African ganhadores to register, pay a fee and wear a numbered metal plate; ganhadeiras face a yearly fee. The porters strike for 13 days (1–13 June 1857), Brazil's first general strike, and win partial changes. Luzia hears of it on market days: a scene for her, and for the boy.
      - Sources (cholera of 1855–56 in Salvador): Barreto & Pimenta, "A saúde dos escravos na Bahia oitocentista através do Hospital da Misericórdia", Revista Territórios & Fronteiras 6(2), 2013. Nearly 10,000 dead in Salvador (8–18% of the population); the enslaved and especially the freed died out of proportion (freed people were 3% of the population but 15% of cholera deaths): <https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/4807280.pdf> 
      - Further reading: Onildo Reis David, O inimigo invisível: epidemia na Bahia no século XIX (EDUFBA / Sarah Letras, 1996), the original study of the epidemic in Bahia.
      - Sources (laws and the internal trade): Oliveira & Scheffer, "Lares partidos: o impacto do comércio interno de escravizados sobre as famílias cativas e negras, Salvador/BA e Campinas/SP, 1850–1881", Revista de História (São Paulo), 2019 (Decreto 1.695 of 1869 and the 1871 change from 15 to 12): <https://www.redalyc.org/journal/2850/285061378024/285061378024.pdf> 
      - Sources: Erivaldo Fagundes Neves, "Sampauleiros traficantes: comércio de escravos do Alto Sertão da Bahia para o Oeste Cafeeiro Paulista", Afro-Ásia 24, 2000 (Bahia's tax on slaves leaving the province from 1862; who was sold south; prices after 1850): <https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/770/77002404.pdf> 
      - Sources: the 1857 ganhadores strike (to deepen with João José Reis, Ganhadores: a greve negra de 1857 na Bahia, 2019): <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_of_the_Ganhadores>

### 6\. Curuzu

  - When / where: 1866, age 20 · Tuiuti, Curuzu
  - Stories:
      
      - Tuiuti (24 May 1866). \[Catharsis point: the zouaves hold in the mud with bayonets and capoeira until the line turns; the first time he fights back and it works. Cost: the same week, in camp, the field is not level.\]
      - Marcolino climbs his back to plant the flag at Curuzu. History remembers Marcolino, not the man underneath. \[Catharsis point: the wall taken, the Paraguayan flag torn down; the cost is that history forgets him.\] After Curuzu, Joca Cabinda captures a light-skinned Paraguayan officer, ties him to a tree and whips him the way a master whips a slave, while a few Black comrades watch. The protagonist was not there: he arrives too late, and the man is already dead. He is accused with the rest, simply because he belongs to the Black troop; Joca's friends stay silent. The same week, white officers have Black soldiers flogged for small faults. In battle the field is level; in the camp it is not.
      - Dom Obá teaches him to read with old newspapers. In the war he stops keeping his head down, as Luzia taught him: he begins to stand up against what other soldiers do (Joca, Damião, the abuse of Paraguayan women).

### 12\. The Paper (Seven years of freedom and poverty)

  - When / where: 1870–1876, age 24–30 · Corumbá
  - Stories:
      
      - A night in jail without his carta de alforria; a clerk mentions an auction.

As a slave he always had a roof and a ration; free, he can go hungry. He never wishes to go back, but freedom is thinner than the war promised (his experience, never the narrator's verdict).

### 13\. The Ticket

  - When / where: c. 1877, age \~31 · Corumbá
  - Stories:
      
      - Seu Bento, the ticket seller.
      - At Albino's he meets the woman from Spain, whom everyone takes for Albino's niece. Only when they talk does he learn the truth: she was taken and trafficked, she is not free, and she is being sold; she tells him her price, one conto de réis (1:000$000; plausible, to verify). Albino himself never names it. He likes her, but Albino, more and more protective of her, above all against a Black man, chases him off every time he tries to get near her. 
      - They bond over not being free: he tells her that even freed, he does not feel free; she understands, and says she would rather be sold to a rich man than end up in a brothel (she means as a maid or a servant). A free woman's life would not be easy either; all her options look bad. 
      - They agree that even with freedom things are not easy. He tells his story and how people seem to do their best to make his life as a free man harder than as a slave.
      - His last coins on a lottery ticket: a bet on luck, the faith he got from Luzia. Hook: the draw. 

### Interlude — Tonico's Journey (c. 1866–1869)

  - After he leaves for the war, Tonico feels the weight of it: another man went to die in his place.
  - The old master dies (c. 1867–68). Tonico, about 21, takes over the estate and its debts. The first thing he does is fire and banish Ezequiel: the legitimate son throws out the unrecognised one, his own half-brother. \[Catharsis point.\] Tonico hands him a dismissal letter that smells like urine. Without his title, the negro whose hand Ezequiel whipped off in his first punishment (see ch. 2) appears, to try to revenge himself (decided Oct 23, 2026; outcome to decide). Ezequiel flees and is never heard of again. Some say that, without his title, he became a target for many of his former victims, and that he was caught and got the end he deserved. Only rumours.
  - Selling sugar takes him south, to Rio and the coffee country. There he finds Luzia: older, on a coffee fazenda in Vassouras, in the Paraíba Valley, still the one everyone calls for the sick, still singing. Through his eyes we see the coffee world: hillsides of coffee bushes in rows, gangs of 20–25 enslaved workers driven under an overseer's eye, each tending thousands of bushes, a valley that produced about half the world's coffee and is already starting to wear out its soil.
  - Luzia writes a letter to her son herself, with a little help from Tonico, who is impressed: the woman who never kept a coin now counts every one. In it she tells her son how she stopped counting on luck and made her own destiny. Tonico promises to deliver it. They agree he has a better chance to find him then her - she is old, tired and without many resources. 
  - But he hears nothing of her son: letters from the front were rare and casualty lists unreliable. He keeps the letter.
  - Luzia's growth: after the sale she rebels against the gods (why would they do this?) and, for the first time in her life, saves, with the goal she has held since her son was a child: her freedom and the trip back to him. It takes years; she joins a junta (savings circle). Once she has enough, an official scams her and sells her a freedom worth nothing (placeholder: a short episode, details to come). She starts again and buys her freedom. She stops waiting for luck and drives her own. When Tonico finds her she is already free and saving for the journey north; she does not know her son went to war.
  - From Tonico she learns her son went to the war. She dies a free woman shortly after, before she can travel (c. 1869). Her Black brotherhood (Rosário dos Pretos) buries her in a marked grave, a place her son can visit.
  - Sources (coffee world, Vassouras, the Paraíba Valley): Redalyc article on the coffee economy of the Paraíba Valley (big owners in Vassouras and Bananal held over 70% of the enslaved; gangs of 20–25; about 5,000 bushes per worker by the 1870s): <https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/273/27315299004.pdf> 
  - Sources (how Luzia was sold south): Richard Graham, "Another Middle Passage", on the internal slave trade from the Northeast and Bahia to the coffee Southeast after 1850: <https://macmillan.yale.edu/glc/another-middle-passage-brazil>

## PART V — FORTUNE (c. 1877–1888)

### 18\. The Lottery

  - When / where: c. 1877 · Corumbá
  - Stories:
      
      - The win: 50 contos (which draw, to verify); what it means in years of a labourer's wages. Enough for land, cattle and a comfortable life. The win soon becomes a burden: everyone knows. People ask for loans and favours, try to cheat him, strangers claim to be cousins and old comrades. He loses his privacy and cannot tell friends from opportunists. His distrust and paranoia start here.
      - Collecting the prize: the ticket came from the Court lottery in Rio, sold in Corumbá through a Portuguese merchant who is the lottery agent (Seu Bento resells his tickets in the street, in fractions). The results come upriver weeks later, printed in Rio newspapers on the steamer. The agent pays small prizes from his till; a big one he must claim through his correspondent in Rio, for a commission, and it takes months. So the winner depends on one man. The agent looks at the ticket, then at him, and tells him he has misread it: the numbers don't match, and a preto like him can't read anyway. Perhaps he hints the ticket was stolen, or offers a small "consolation" for it. But he can read: Dom Obá taught him in the trenches with old newspapers. He reads the numbers aloud, slowly, off the printed list, in front of everyone. Seu Bento, who sold him the ticket and wrote it in his book, comes to vouch for him. Reading, the skill the war gave him, is what saves his fortune. Echo of the deeds Dr. Teles will write later: money doesn't stop the insults, it only changes their form.
      - How the town looks at "the lucky Black man".
      - The win makes the newspapers and the news travels. It sets off two things: Tonico sends Luzia's letter, and Albino raises the woman's price (ch. 20). Weeks later (c. 1877) a letter comes upriver from Bahia: Tonico writes, and folded inside is the letter Luzia wrote years before, in her own hand. He reads her words himself (Dom Obá taught him). He expected a mother proud of his luck; he finds a woman who turned against the gods and luck and made her own destiny. A shock. Its first effect: The Purchase (ch. 20). She is dead; she lies in a marked grave he can visit. He finally stops searching the lists for her name.
      - Sources (how lotteries worked): Beatriz Ana Loner, "A Loteria do Ipiranga e os trabalhadores: um sonho de liberdade no final do século XIX", História (São Paulo) 33(1), 2014. Tickets sold by lottery houses, travelling agents and cambistas, also in halves and quarters; winning numbers printed in newspapers; treasurers paid prizes, with delays and commissions for intermediaries; procuradores needed to claim large sums from the capital; fraud and duplicate tickets; enslaved and freed winners who bought their freedom: <https://www.redalyc.org/pdf/2210/221031471011.pdf> 
      - To verify: which lottery Corumbá bought in the 1870s (the Court lottery in Rio or a provincial one) and how a frontier winner was paid. The agent-and-correspondent version above is a plausible reconstruction.

### 19\. Jacadigo

      - Becoming "senhor": demanding, his wishes are orders.

### 20\. The Purchase

  - When / where: c. 1877–78 · Corumbá
  - Stories:
      
      - They met before the lottery (Chapters 13 and 17): she told him her story and her price, one conto de réis. Once he wins, the first thing he does, even before the money arrives, is go to Albino to buy her. Albino refuses to sell to a Black man. Then the newspapers make him "the lucky Black man", and Albino, knowing about the prize, raises the price every time he comes back (two contos, then five; to verify). When Luzia's letter arrives (ch. 18) and he learns she is dead, he feels alone in the world and snaps. This is not luck: he decides. He bursts into Albino's house with a simple threat: he will tell everyone what the "niece" really is and go to the police, and he is strong enough to make the visit unpleasant. \[Catharsis point.\] Selling a free woman is a crime (Criminal Code of 1830, art. 179, reducing a free person to slavery), but he has no proof, and a Black man's word counts for little. It does not matter: Albino does not want trouble or talk, and the man in front of him is ready to pay. Albino takes what he wanted in the first place: one conto. The prize is slow and complicated to collect; Albino, who now wants to be paid, uses his influence to ease it. He pays once the money arrives. He could not save his mother; he can save her. And married to a white woman, his children will not be as Black, and as cursed, as he is (his view, shaped by the whitening ideology of the time, never the narrator's): the colour of his family will not be left to luck either. For her it is a relief: she is spared the brothel. In his mind he is saving her, as he could not save his mother. The family legend of "buying a Spanish girl".
      - Marriage without a choice. She is saved and bought at once: grateful to be spared the brothel, hoping he will not be too bad. She calls him "senhor". He gives her no choice, and so, without seeing it, he enslaves her: the seed of most of their conflicts.

### 21\. The House

  - When / where: 1878–1886 · Jacadigo
  - Stories:
      
      - The good years. The chapter starts positive: they have some good years at Liberdade. The lands are beautiful; the couple is fairly happy; she is still young and innocent. He employs free men who were once enslaved, promising a fair wage and respect. This sets the tone of a happy ending.
      - The farm prospers. Liberdade is a cattle farm (the region lived on cattle: live cattle driven to other provinces, hides, and from c. 1870 charque, salted dried beef; see sources below). The farm is productive. He is good with the land and knows what to do. He is still strong, and from time to time he still works the land beside his workers, men who were enslaved like him, all paid wages; this makes him respected. He works closely with them and knows their language. Being this close, with such good communication, he finds ways to improve productivity and make the work smarter, easier and more profitable. He would employ some other slaves who fought the war. Some were friends and were loyal to him, some others carried a lot of trauma and were mostly helped. The loyal ones die over time and hiring new people will become a challenge he also has to deal.
      - Firmino (new character). One of the veterans, a comrade from the war, freed by it like him, and his most loyal man at Liberdade: a great worker, paid wages like the others, the first in the corrals and the last to leave, who learns every improvement and teaches it to the others. His right hand, and one of the few people he trusts. Later Firmino dies (when and how: to decide, before ch. 24); his loss is one of the losses that leave the farm without its old hands (see ch. 24).
      - The other landlords mock him at first, but grow wary of him as a competitor. He can produce, he has competitive prices, and he has fewer problems managing his workforce. His prices concern them most: they have slaves and pay no wages, yet they cannot produce significantly cheaper than Liberdade. The Pires family is one of these competitors.
      - Sources (cattle in the Pantanal and Corumbá, 1870–1914): Embrapa, "Pantanal: ciclos econômicos e berçário natural" (2022), the charque and hides cycle from c. 1870 and lean cattle sent to other provinces: https://www.infoteca.cnptia.embrapa.br/infoteca/bitstream/doc/1145409/1/Pantanalcicloseconomicos2022.pdf ; "Corumbá e seu papel como entreposto comercial de 1870 a 1914 na economia matogrossense": https://www.academia.edu/87261148/ ; Robert W. Wilcox, Cattle in the Backlands: Mato Grosso and the Evolution of Ranching in the Brazilian Tropics (2017), review: https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/15/article/692073/pdf
      - The slow turn. As the years pass, things change slowly in both of them: smaller incidents at first, then they evolve. She develops the pride; he starts being paranoid. Proposed events (to confirm): her first silk dress ordered from Corumbá; then a piano or European furniture nobody uses; she stops going down to the corrals; she starts correcting his Portuguese and the sons' manners. He counts the cattle twice; he locks the storehouse and keeps the key on him; he hears thieves at night and fires into the sky; later he accuses his sons.
      - The sons are born; Lucília becomes godmother.
      - He turns out harsh and strict, his wishes orders, and the marriage sours, with ups and downs. The critical argument: he says, "You said you would rather be sold to a rich man. I am a rich man. I saved you." She answers: "I meant as a maid or a servant, not a sex slave. You didn't save me, you own me. You made me your slave; you never gave me the choice." It breaks his illusion of being her saviour and puts him in the place of the master. A deep inner conflict he does not know how to deal with. She takes over the household money and the farm accounts.
      - His distrust grows (it starts with the lottery, ch. 18; developed here). Step by step: first he hides his wealth and keeps the money close; then the white neighbours, who hate a rich Black man, and Black people, who envy him, become in his eyes the same threat. He sees thieves everywhere and fires his gun into the sky at night to scare them off. Finally the suspicion turns inward, onto his own house: he accuses his sons of stealing from him (grandma does the same in the prologue). By the end of the chapter he trusts no one but his wife with the accounts, and even that only half.
      - Nobody is free at Liberdade. Under the name, each of them is trapped in a different way: he by his fear of losing everything and his distrust; his wife, bought and married without a choice; the sons by their father's anger and rules. The narrator never points out the irony; the reader sees it.
      - Her pride and appearances. Once rich, she becomes materialistic, very proud of how rich she appears: clothes, furniture, the house, how the family is seen in Corumbá. This is part of the divide between her and him. She wants her sons to look like nobility. They are partly Black, which bothers her: she is also a bit racist. The sons refuse to work the land, and she does not force them; she does not like to see them doing heavy work, because it makes them look like slaves, and that disgusts her. Another rift with him.
      - The sons grow up spoiled and disconnected from reality. Still they suffer racism: they are still Black, even if they are rich. They use the money to compensate for it. They are proud, and angry that they cannot be respected because of their race. This pride and anger will matter when they lose everything (ch. 25).
      - The white boys. The Pires sons and Otávio (son of a Corumbá merchant who trades with the farm; minor character) grow up with his sons, always around at Liberdade. He is reminded of his own childhood with Tonico. But he notices bad intentions towards his sons: they call them moleque when they think no one is listening; in their games his sons are always the horse, the servant, the one who fetches; they ask too many questions (how many head of cattle, where the money is kept); small things go missing after their visits. He warns his sons, but this creates more rifts. His wife is always in favour of the white boys: for her they are a way to get closer to their parents, to be among the noble. He second-guesses himself: Tonico turned out to be a loyal friend after all. Finally he allows the white boys to be always around, and his sons to be with them. The negative signs never disappear; whenever he mentions them, he is accused of being distrustful and paranoid. For the reader: he is paranoid, but not always wrong.
      - The Pires and the wife. The Pires find a chance to remain close to the family at Liberdade by attracting her. They can see she wants to be included, so they lure her in. Through her they learn about the farm, and slowly they manipulate her into conflicts with her husband: they suggest she is not being treated as well as she should be, they make her feel she is not living the life she should; they keep showing her how they live and how much she is still missing. This feeds the rift between the couple. He does not really like the Pires; he can see their bad intentions clearly. They keep a strange relationship, mostly because of the ties of his wife and sons. The Pires are fake people, but very good at keeping the appearance of nobility, education and respect. They can manipulate his wife and sons, but not him. Very often he is put in the position of having to accept the Pires or lose his relationship with his wife and sons. He becomes increasingly paranoid, often with reason, but over time that gets blurry.
      - Luzia's grave: he travels south to Vassouras, in the coffee country, or plans to, to stand at her grave (to decide).

### 23\. The Gates

  - When / where: 1888–1889 · Jacadigo
  - Stories:
      
      - The peak of his paranoia. Fugitives from nearby ranches arrive at his gates, and he takes them in. ## PART VI — THE FALL (1890s)

### 24\. The Name

  - When / where: 1890s · Jacadigo
  - Stories:
      
      - His last years. As he grows old his memory starts to fail, and the Pires and his own family sometimes use that to gaslight him. He starts doubting even himself. Never call it dementia or name any disease: even the reader should be confused. Is his memory wrong? Are the Pires lying? Is his wife doubting him for no reason? (Echo of grandma's fog in the prologue.)
      - All the loyal employees and ex war veterans are now dead. The new employees dont care as much, dont work hard as much. The practices that made the farm competitive get lost over time as the old employees die. The new ones are not interested in learning. Competition is stronger and the farm starts loosing money.
      - His death. He tells his wife his true name; the reader never hears it. She keeps it.

### 25\. The Table

  - When / where: 1890s · Corumbá
  - Stories:
      
      - The sons inherit; the Pires lend on credit.
      - Why they start: the sons never learned to run the land, and Liberdade loses money year after year. Every time there is trouble at the farm, they gamble to raise cash. Their father bet on luck and won, didn't he? It is their easy way out.
      - Who they play with: the white boys they grew up with (ch. 21), now men, the Pires sons and Otávio, who pretend to be their friends and want only their wealth. The cards are marked. The whites take it slowly, sometimes letting them win, eating away at what they have. Pride and the wish to be like the white elite keep the sons at the table. They resent their father, but they do not want to be poor; they lose everything out of pride and the desire to be like the white elite.
      - The last game: they are pushed to go all in, sure they hold a great hand. It seals it: they lose everything. The whites win.
      - Silvio, the second son, his father's favourite, becomes a sort of miner, a garimpeiro searching for precious stones (after the farms are lost, to confirm), also believing in luck. He is always scamming people, trying to find the easy way out of things and make easy money. He makes some great deals at times, but also loses a lot on gambling and fighting. (Where he digs: to research, the diamond garimpos of Mato Grosso.)
