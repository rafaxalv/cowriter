### 3. The Roda

**I**

In the year his mother was sold, everything changed at once, and he understood none of it.

Until then there had always been a voice from the big house. Tonico would appear at the edge of the cane, or call down from the veranda, and the overseer would shrug and wave him off: go, the master's son wants you. He had been, without ever deciding to be, a boy who escaped. Now no one called. The friendship had gone cold in a single afternoon, the afternoon of the cart, and with the friendship went the protection, and with the protection the whole of his childhood, which he discovered, too late, had been a kind of privilege. He worked the full day in the cane like everyone else, and his hands split and bled and closed again into something hard.

He told himself he hated Tonico; he said it every evening, almost tenderly, like a prayer. But it was not hatred. If it had been hatred it would have been simpler. It was shame: shame at having been the white boy's companion, at having been released from tasks the others did, at having believed it was friendship. When he passed the children who had worked while he fished, he lowered his eyes, and the lowering humiliated him more than their looks.

It was Bastião who spoke first. Bastião, two or three years older, who had called him "the milk brother" since they were small, and who called him that now, at the fire, in front of everyone.

"Well, milk brother. The master's pet has lost his master."

Some of them laughed. He got up, ready to fight, ready even to be beaten, because to be beaten would at least be something. But Bastião did not get up. He only looked at him, and there was no mockery in his eyes at all, and he said quietly, so that only he heard it:

"Come with me tonight. Bring nothing."

**II**

That same year Ezequiel was made free.

The master did it without ceremony. There was a paper, a few words in the office, and a whip handed over as if it were a tool, which it was. He had informed for years, petty things, and each time the master had said, "I always knew you were different." He had lived on that word. *Different.* He had waited, through all those years of telling, for the other word, and the other word never came. Freedom came instead, and the title of capitão do mato, and Ezequiel, standing in the office with the paper in his hand, felt something so like gratitude that he did not notice it was only another payment.

The first time he had to use the whip it was on a man who had stolen. The man would not confess. Ezequiel shouted at him. Silence. He struck him across the face. Silence. He tied him to the tree in the yard, and gave his last warning, and was surprised to find his own hands trembling, not with fear, he thought, but with anger. It was the first time he had to show what he was. Not one of them. Different.

He brought the whip down on the man's arm, and the man made a sound.

And suddenly Ezequiel was twelve years old and in the branches of a guava tree, outside a lit window, and the master's hand was striking his mother, and she was making no sound at all, and the master was striking her again *in order* to hear one. The skin of his whole body rose. He struck again. The man moaned. He did not understand what was happening to him and he did not want to understand. He remembered that room, but now it was he who stood in it; he was the one with the hand. He struck and struck, faster, harder, and the sweat ran into his eyes, and his whole body was tight, his trousers tight against him, until someone took hold of his arms from behind.

"Stop. He won't be able to work if you go on."

He came back as if from very far away. He did not know how many times he had struck, or how long it had lasted. He looked at the man on the tree. He had whipped the same arm the whole time; the hand hung from it almost loose. Later they cut it off.

From that day Ezequiel was the most feared capitão do mato in the district, and people said behind their hands that he enjoyed it. What frightened him was not the joke but the suspicion that it was true.

**III**

Bastião took him that night, and many nights after, through the cane and into the woods, to a clearing where young men from three estates trained by moonlight and spoke in whispers. The man they trained with was called Velho Kalunga.

On the way, Bastião told him the legends: that Kalunga had killed a capitão do mato with one kick, that dogs could not follow his trail, that he had been a prince in Africa, a ghost. He came to the clearing expecting a giant, and found a thin, grey-headed old man who laughed at everything and danced.

He was disappointed, and he was ashamed of being disappointed, and so he behaved badly. He stood outside the circle with his arms folded. He did not know the songs, or when to clap, or how to enter the roda: a whole world he had walked past every day and never seen. The others laughed at him. Bastião laughed loudest: the master's pet, come to play with the senzala. He hated Bastião for an evening, sincerely, and the next night he came back.

Kalunga taught nothing but the ginga. Back and forth, back and forth, the rhythm, the songs, more ginga. It seemed to him like dancing lessons. He wanted to kick; above all he wanted the rabo de arraia, the turning kick with the hands on the ground, and now and then he tried it, and fell, and nobody even laughed. Kalunga only said, between two jokes:

"Your body can't sing yet."

He took this for an insult. It was the only true thing anyone had said to him in a year.

The clan had one rule: only those who had proved themselves belonged, and no outsider could ever see the hideout. He had not proved himself; he was tolerated. And the old man was hunted. Years before, Ezequiel had come to his rodas as a boy and been sent away, too proud, too eager to hurt; now he searched the woods with dogs. Every time, the old man slipped away and left him looking a fool. The young men laughed about it for days. Kalunga did not laugh. He was old now, and each escape cost him more.

**IV**

What changed him was not the fighting. It was the music.

He had sung all his life, in the cane and at the kettles, the songs his mother had taught him in a language he did not understand. One night after the roda the boys took him to a Candomblé ceremony in Cachoeira, at the terreiro of Mãe Otília. He went to be polite. And there, among the women singing, was Rosária, a house servant from the estate, an apprentice of the Boa Morte sisters, and she was singing his mother's songs.

He stood very still. He did not know what the words meant. He had never known. But his mouth knew them, and before he could decide anything he was singing too, and Rosária turned and looked at him, and he felt, absurdly, that he had been caught at something.

They became friends. Only friends. He told himself it was because of the songs.

After that night his body began to sing. He could not have said how. The ginga that had felt like a dance became a rhythm, and the rhythm became a way of being always ready, and one night in the clearing the kicks began to come out of the rhythm by themselves. He began to sing in the roda, and Kalunga, who had sent away one angry boy long ago, listened to this other angry boy singing the songs of a woman sold south, and decided to keep him.

It happened without ceremony, because Kalunga hated ceremony. He was accepted into the clan with a joke. And Bastião, who had called him the milk brother since they were children, slapped him on the back and said:

"Então agora a gente é irmão de café?" So now we're coffee brothers?

Everyone laughed, Kalunga too. Coffee for the senzala, milk for the big house. He laughed with them, and that night, walking home through the cane, he understood that he had a family again, and was so frightened by the happiness of it that he did not sleep.

**V**

At the August festa of the Boa Morte in Cachoeira, in 1863, Ezequiel and his friends came into the crowd drunk and off duty. Rosária was singing. One of them took her by the arm.

The capoeira boys were already moving. It was quick and dirty, a street fight in a festa, and capoeira won it; and before anyone could say who had done what, they had melted into the crowd. Ezequiel got up from the cobbles with blood in his mouth and looked around and saw only faces.

Afterwards, by the river, away from the drums, Rosária asked him where he had learned to fight like that, and he said "dancing", and she laughed, and that was how it began between them.

But someone told Ezequiel who had been in that fight. From then on, Ezequiel watched him.

**VI**

He thought about running; everyone did. There were quilombos in the hills, people whispered. He made plans at night so detailed he could feel the ground under his feet. In the morning Ezequiel was always there, at the edge of the field or on the path, saying nothing, looking. He was free now; he went where he liked. And sometimes, when no overseer was near, he would come close and say quietly:

"You'll die a slave. You know that? You'll die on this estate with your back bent. I'm free. Look at me."

He never answered. He had learned from his mother to keep his head down, and he kept it down, and the not answering cost him more than any answer would have. And he did not run. He told himself it was because of the dogs and the men brought back dead, and that was true. It was also true that he was afraid of what he would be without the estate, the only world he knew, and that second fear he did not admit even to Bastião.

One thing consoled him, and he was ashamed of it. Tonico, grown now and working beside his father, sometimes had to give Ezequiel an order, and Ezequiel obeyed slowly, with open contempt, and the old master did not always take his son's side. Watching from the cane, he could not have said whose humiliation he enjoyed more.

**VII**

It came at last in the dry season of 1864, behind the cane, with no one to stop it.

He never knew afterwards who had started it. It seemed to him that it had always been happening, for years, and that this was only the moment it became visible. They faced each other: two of Kalunga's students, the one he had sent away and the one he had kept.

Ezequiel was stronger, and he knew more, and he fought as he whipped, without stopping, as if each blow were owed him. He was winning. He was winning easily. There was blood in the young man's mouth and his ribs burned and he was thinking, very clearly, as if someone else were thinking it for him: so this is how it ends; he was right; I'll die here. And suddenly, without his deciding anything, out of the rhythm, out of the old man's endless ginga, his hands were on the ground and his heel was going round, and Ezequiel was lying in the dirt and neither of them understood what had happened.

He stood over him. He was breathing so hard he could hardly speak, and still the words came, as though he had been saving them all his life:

"Not black enough to fight. Not white enough to be a master."

He felt then a triumph so complete that for a moment the whole world seemed to belong to him: the cane, the sky, the man in the dirt. The boy who had been taught to keep his head down had won by putting it on the ground.

But walking back to the senzala, he noticed that his hands were shaking, and then he noticed that he was smiling, and the smile frightened him. He had wanted Ezequiel to suffer. He had enjoyed it. For one moment, standing over him, he had understood exactly what Ezequiel felt at the tree, and he did not want to understand it, and he could not stop understanding it.

**VIII**

In 1865 the news of the war reached the estate. The Emperor needed soldiers in Paraguay, and the planters were pressed to contribute, and a slave who went to the war would come back free. The Coronel decided to offer some of his men.

He stepped forward before the Coronel had finished speaking.

It was a bet, the kind his mother had taught him to make: a small chance of freedom against the certainty of slavery for ever.

Tonico was not among the men. Of course not. The master's son was never going to be sent while there were slaves to send. And Ezequiel was not among them either. He was free; he could not be offered. The master would not lose his capitão do mato. And so the freedom the young man was going to risk his life for, Ezequiel already had, and it kept him safe at home. When he understood this, he laughed aloud, alone, in the cane, and could not stop, and it was not laughter at all.

Then everything went at once, as it had seven years before. Rosária was sold. Bastião would not go to fight another man's war, and one night he was gone into the hills, toward the quilombos, and the next week Velho Kalunga was no longer seen in the woods, and nobody on the estate knew whether they had gone together.

On the last night Mãe Otília called him to the terreiro and put a guia over his head, dark blue beads for Ogum, the iron god who opens roads.

"Ògún yè!" she said.

"Ògún yè," he answered.

In the morning he walked out behind the Coronel's cart, toward Salvador and the ships, humming the song his mother used to sing in the market. He had sung it all his life and never known what it was about: a woman who promised her child to a tree, and had to pay. He did not look back at the estate. He had nothing left there but the words she had given him the night his father died, the only inheritance he carried: *Orí ni baba, Orí ni ìyá.* Your head is your father. Your head is your mother.
