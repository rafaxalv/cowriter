# Character Bible

Oct 1, 2026 · Rafael · reorganized Oct 26, 2026 into short bullets; the events live in Chapters.

## How to use this bible

Who each character is, in short bullets: role, personality, motives, relationships, the real piece of history they carry, and pointers to the chapters where their events happen. The events themselves, with all their details, are in Chapters. The history is real; the lives are invented.

  - **Neither he nor the woman from Spain is ever named.** Everyone around him calls him something different. The reader never learns his "true" name, just as the family lost it. Working names, for planning only and never in the book text: Benedito (him) and Carmen (the woman from Spain).
  - **Grandma's story is the spine:** born enslaved, freed by the war in Paraguay, wins the lottery, buys farms, "buys" a Spanish girl and marries her, dies, and the sons gamble it all away. Every invented detail fits around these beats.
  - **Real people appear as cameos only.** Invented characters do the plot work; real figures (Rebouças, Dom Obá, Ana Néri, Caxias) pass through. The lists are a menu, not a cast list.
  - **Grounding:** the Zuavos baianos material comes from the *Dados gerais* source doc (Bento; Kraay); other anchors are marked *to verify* in the fact map at the end.
  - **Ages are working assumptions** so the timeline holds together.
  - **Chance, not morals.** Much of what happens is luck. The book never judges; it shows reasons, fears and hopes.
  - **No heroes, no villains.** Everyone has decent and ugly sides. Racism also runs among Black people: Africans and *crioulos*, *pretos* and *pardos* (see Ezequiel).

## Benedito, the man without a name

  - Born c. 1846, a *crioulo* (Brazil-born enslaved) on a sugar estate in the Recôncavo of Bahia; his mother was born in Africa.
  - Why Brazil-born: the Atlantic trade ended in 1850, so an African-born man fit to fight in 1865 would be in his thirties or smuggled. A Brazil-born son of an African mother gives both worlds: he speaks Portuguese and hums Yoruba songs he cannot translate.
  - Belongs to no country that will claim him. After the war he never goes back to Bahia; he stays on the Mato Grosso frontier, at Corumbá, and later Jacadigo (ch. 10, 14).

### Parents

  - **Luzia** (the masters' name; her Yoruba name is lost, like his). Nagô (Yoruba).
      - Healer, midwife and singer with an extraordinary voice; Yoruba plant knowledge, learned with ritual song.
      - Trusts the orixás and luck; money is meant to flow, so she never saves. "The orixás will open the way."
      - Teaches him to keep his head down, to trust luck, her songs, and how to treat wounds and fevers (it saves him in the war).
      - Silent about the master's nights; hides them from Inácio and her son.
      - Arc: the sale shakes her faith but does not break it ("How could the orixás allow such an atrocity?"); she learns that the orixás alone are not enough, that luck comes and goes and you have to fight for what you want ("Deus ajuda quem cedo madruga") (revised Nov 2, 2026); she saves for the first time to go back to her son and buys her own freedom c. 1866, too late: he is already at the war. Even free she cannot travel back: she is still saving for the fare. Tonico, looking for her out of guilt, finds her c. 1868 before she has the money, pays the journey and brings her back to Cachoeira as a paid nurse; she has a few good years there and a good death c. 1873, at peace, the Boa Morte sisters singing around her (decided Nov 2, 2026). She keeps her faith but stops waiting for luck.
      - Chapters: sold as a girl, ganhadeira in Salvador, the Malê Revolt, sold to the estate c. 1843, meets Inácio, his birth (ch. 1); healer, singer, sold south c. 1858 (ch. 2); free healer in Vassouras, found by Tonico, her first letter, a paid nurse in Cachoeira near the estate, where Tonico can support her and she writes twenty or thirty more letters to her son, handing the bundle to Tonico before she dies, her good years and peaceful death there c. 1873, buried by the Boa Morte sisterhood, Rosária's (decided Nov 2, 2026) (Interlude); the letter reaches him (Bastião brings it, ch. 12; read in ch. 13).
  - **Inácio** (working name). Enslaved boiling-house worker, Luzia's companion, his father.
      - Teaches him the sugar chain at the mill: the seed of the farms he will run.
      - Chapters: nursed by Luzia after a burn (ch. 1); dies of cholera in 1855 (ch. 2). The boy is born all black (preto), which ends the gossip about Luzia and the master (ch. 1–2).

### Personality

  - Watchful, quiet, good at numbers. Proud in a way he learned to hide.
  - Superstitious; trusts luck more than people, because hard work never made a difference.
  - Funny with a few close friends, cold with everyone else.
  - Vain about clothes, starting with the zouave uniform (ch. 4).
  - After Tonico, his friends are the capoeira boys and the terreiro: the time he is closest to his African community (ch. 3).
  - A fighter, hungry for freedom after his father's death and his mother's sale; dreams of running away, held back by fear of being caught and killed (ch. 3).
  - A healer, like Luzia: he repeats her steps aloud and sings her songs as he works, a ritual begun at his father's sickbed (ch. 2). In the war it makes him one of the healers, respected, often spared from the front line (ch. 6).
  - A mark in one eye for life, from a childhood accident with Tonico (ch. 2).
  - While Tonico's friend he escaped work often; from 1858 the work is full, and his bonds with the other enslaved grow as he ages (ch. 2–3).
  - Sings well: he learned singing at work beside his family, every day, most of the day (ch. 1–2). Skeptical of Kalunga's ginga, then in love with it: once his body can sing he becomes a dangerous fighter; his move is the rabo de arraia (ch. 3).
  - Loves music and dance (batuques, samba de roda): where he feels free. Athletic, a capoeirista; in the war capoeira becomes a weapon (ch. 3, 5).
  - Violence is the first language he learned: not angry day to day, but explosive when disrespected ("moleque" is enough).
  - His anger slowly decreases with Kalunga's lessons but is always present (ch. 3); it comes back after the war (ch. 10).
  - At the festa he truly hates Ezequiel and wins in anger; only later does he understand the complexity of Ezequiel's situation, and that his and Tonico's provocations never helped (ch. 3, decided Nov 2, 2026).
  - Free and rich, he is demanding: his wishes are orders. Jealous of his wife (ch. 16, 17).
  - Good with the land and with men: works beside his workers, knows their language, finds smarter ways to run the farm (ch. 14).
  - In the first years at Liberdade he works harder than anyone and no job is beneath him; he finds, almost by accident, what changes the farm: salt, counting and branding, cacimbas, hides, the sick horses, Descalvados, the fence (ch. 14, 15). Inspired by Rafael's grandfather Rivaldo.
  - Sees through the Pires, though they can manipulate his wife and sons (ch. 15).
  - Loses every argument out loud to Carmen's quotes; reads in her ledgers that the cattle pay for the culture that despises them, and says nothing (ch. 16, 6).
  - Never really loved Carmen: liked and admired her; Rosária was the real connection (ch. 10, 13). Suspects his sons' spoiled ways are the white part of their blood, and thinks of Ezequiel (ch. 15).
  - Wants to teach his sons the farm, as Inácio taught him, and to sing with them, as Luzia did; they prefer their mother's world. Resents them as spoiled, weak and unaware of how hard the world is, like the worst of Tonico; feels they are not even his friends (ch. 15, 17).
  - In old age his memory fails, never named as a disease (ch. 17).
  - The key to him: innocent of what he is accused of, guilty of what nobody knows (Acosta Ñu, refusing to help Damião; ch. 6, 9).

### What he wants

  - As a boy: to be free, as Luzia promises.
  - 1865: to go to war, the first door to freedom he has seen; he puts himself forward the moment the Coronel offers enslaved men (ch. 3).
  - During and after the war: to find his mother and buy her freedom (ch. 10, 12).
  - After the lottery: to be a senhor nobody gives orders to, and to save the woman from Spain as he could not save his mother; he gives her no choice (ch. 13).

### Wounds and traumas

  - First wound: Inácio's death and Luzia's lament, the saddest song he has ever heard (ch. 2).
  - Second wound: losing his mother; he searches the lists of the freed until her letters come (ch. 2, 10, 12).
  - Freedom bought with his body, offered by his master, even though he chose to go (ch. 3).
  - The cholera camps (ch. 6).
  - Acosta Ñu: the boy with the false beard; the memory he never tells (ch. 9), which returns at São João (ch. 16).
  - Hit in the head at Humaitá (1868): headaches ever after, slowly worse, a real problem only in old age, feeding his anger and confusion (ch. 7, 10, 15, 17).
  - Accused of what he tried to stop (Damião's whipping of the officer, his rampage in the village): no trial, only the stain (ch. 5, 9). Echoes the family's oral tradition about Brazilian soldiers and Paraguayan women.
  - His wife and sons reject his African culture (ch. 16).
  - Bastião killed by the Pires' men, and nobody believes him; his own family joins the gaslighting: the loss that starts his decline (ch. 17).
  - Dies alone: every trusted friend gone before him (Bastião, Firmino, the veterans; no news of Tonico), though his wife and children are with him (ch. 17).

### Names others give him

  - The master's family: "the boy". Luzia: a secret Yoruba name. The army roll: a registered name we never see. Comrades: "Zuavo". Newspapers: "the lucky Black man". His wife: "senhor", and in rare private moments something the reader never hears. The narration: only *he*.
  - Padre Felício's list holds his real name and is lost (ch. 6, 9). He tells his wife his true name before he dies; the reader never hears it (ch. 17).

## Theme: luck, fortune and the gambling inheritance

  - Luck is a worldview, not a curse: a rational belief for people whose effort never paid off, and luck sees no race.
  - The lottery win is the trap: it proves his faith "right", and he teaches his sons that money comes from the sky (ch. 12).
  - Luzia stops waiting on luck; her letters reach him just as the lottery proves his faith right. The shock leads to The Purchase: he decides instead of waiting (ch. 13).
  - Each generation: he sees luck as justice; the woman from Spain trusts only what she can count (luck against ledgers is one of their recurring arguments); the sons gamble to cover losses, then out of pride, to sit as equals at the white men's table (ch. 18); grandma plays small and regularly, a ritual of hope (Epilogue).
  - Signs and dreams: the Ogum guia, his mother's songs, a dream that points to a number (ch. 10).
  - Language: *vício do jogo* or *mania*, never "addiction". The book never moralises against gambling.
  - Real aspects: imperial and provincial lotteries; the 1874 *Lei do Sorteio* (to verify); the lottery as one of the few ways up for poor and Black Brazilians.

## Inheritance: what passes down

Shown through scenes and repeated lines, never explained. Some chains break or mutate, by chance.

  - **Faith in luck.** "The orixás will open the way" (Luzia) → "Luck will open the way" (him) → "The cards will open the way" (a son, ch. 18) → grandma's lottery (Epilogue).
  - **Violence.** "Why does he have to be so angry?": the boy about the master (ch. 2), his son about him (ch. 16). Ezequiel shows the chain at its darkest (ch. 2). Grandma "could be really mean" (prologue).
  - **Fear of losing everything.** Luzia sold (ch. 2); his distrust starts with the lottery (ch. 12), grows on the farm (ch. 15), peaks at the gates and blurs with his memory (ch. 17). Paranoid, but not always wrong (ch. 15, 18). Grandma fires a shotgun at a noise on the roof and accuses people of stealing her things (prologue).
  - **Music.** Luzia's songs: in the market and over Inácio (ch. 2), in the war, on the farm; rejected by his family (ch. 16); one son hums one without knowing it (ch. 17, 18). The songs and lines (Olúrónbí, the funeral lament, Orí ni baba, Atotô, Ògún yè) are in research/music/luzia-songs.md. What reaches grandma: to decide.
  - **Healing.** Luzia the healer and midwife (ch. 1–2) → Benedito, who nurses his father and becomes a healer in the war (ch. 2, 6) → grandma, a nursing assistant (Prologue, Epilogue). One of the few good things passed down; never explained.
  - **Broken, not inherited:** keeping his head down. He drops it in the war and never lowers it again (ch. 5); on the farm that pride becomes the senhor's anger.

## Before the war: Bahia, 1846–1865

Slavery from the inside: an ordinary sugar estate where cruelty is routine and kindness is unreliable.

  - **Coronel Joaquim Araújo de Menezes**, the master, a *senhor de engenho* in the Recôncavo.
      - Pious, polite, in debt; thinks of himself as a "good master".
      - Forces himself on Luzia and other women; never recognises Ezequiel.
      - Real aspect: the decline of Bahian sugar, the internal slave trade south; owners donating enslaved men, freed to enlist, in the war.
      - Chapters: ch. 1–2 (sells Luzia), ch. 3 (offers enslaved men to the army), dies c. 1867–68 (Interlude).
  - **Dona Mariana**, the master's wife, Tonico's mother.
      - Teaches him prayers and letters "as a game", then stops: a kindness he resents and treasures.
      - Real aspect: the "affectionate" household slavery behind the myth of mild Brazilian slavery.
      - Chapters: ch. 1 (Tonico's birth), ch. 2.
  - **Antônio (Tonico) de Menezes**, the master's son, his age; his milk brother.
      - Weak and charming. True friends as small boys; estranged after Luzia's sale: not enemies, not friends.
      - Takes on the estate as he grows, working with his father, rarely around; Ezequiel defies him, and Benedito blames him for doing nothing (ch. 3).
      - Never a candidate for the war; watches his milk brother leave and stays. A loyal friend after all.
      - Real aspect: the exemption of the elite; the "milk brother" bond.
      - Chapters: ch. 1–2, 3; fires Ezequiel after finding in the accounts how well his father paid him (c. 1868), goes looking for Luzia out of guilt, finds her and brings her back to Cachoeira as a paid nurse, near the estate so he can support her from time to time; she hands him her letters before she dies, always knows where Bastião hides and never tells (Interlude); reads of the lottery and goes to Bastião; takes Bastião down the river as his captured runaway past the capitães do mato who know him, then leaves him with a pass naming Tonico as owner, Luzia's bundle of letters, and his own, to be read first: it breaks the news of her death, then apologises for never helping, he was a boy and afraid; he cannot leave the estate; he signs as the milk brother, knowing he was never a coffee brother (Interlude; read in ch. 13). Knows his insulting letter sent Ezequiel to burn the hideout and kill Kalunga; understands he should have killed Ezequiel and regrets offering him a peaceful way out: his one failure that can never be repaired (Interlude, decided Nov 2, 2026). Never goes to Liberdade; after the letter there is no news of him again (decided Oct 30, 2026).
      - Goes to Bastião's hiding place unarmed and alone; never part of the Kalunga clan (decided Nov 2, 2026); catches the same-day steamer with Bastião and gives him the pass, the letters and money for the journey (Interlude).
  - **Bastião**, best friend, two or three years older, enslaved on the estate; Velho Kalunga's student.
      - Knew Benedito as a boy but not closely; teased him, jokingly, as the master's son's pet (ch. 2).
      - Sees his anger when Luzia is sold and reaches out; brings him into the senzala's secret world, the roda, the terreiro, Kalunga (ch. 3).
      - Calls him "the milk brother" as a tease (ch. 2); when Benedito joins the Kalunga clan: "Does this make us the coffee brothers?" (ch. 3). The coffee brothers are only the two of them; Tonico's letter closes the joke: he signs as the milk brother, never a coffee brother (ch. 13).
      - At first teases him in the roda for not knowing its traditions; the teasing turns into friendship, and he stands by him against Ezequiel. With Kalunga, Benedito's first family after Luzia (ch. 3).
      - Capoeirista, joker, storyteller.
      - Real aspect: capoeira as resistance; quilombos in the Recôncavo.
      - Smart, a very good fighter, taught by Kalunga; knows his way around.
      - Killed in the 1890s defending Liberdade when the Pires' men steal its cattle; Benedito buries him and cannot prove who did it (ch. 17).
      - Chapters: ch. 2–3; refuses to go to the war and runs to a quilombo (ch. 3); kills Ezequiel when he burns the hideout and kills Kalunga, c. 1868; then lives alone (Interlude); cannot read: Tonico brings him the newspaper and a pass, and he crosses Brazil carrying Luzia's letters and Tonico's, told to hand over Tonico's first (Interlude), arriving in Corumbá c. late November 1877, four or five months after the win (ch. 12); stands with him when he challenges Albino for the woman from Spain (ch. 13); one of the most trusted men at Liberdade (ch. 14); dies before him, and Benedito buries him (ch. 17).
  - **Velho Kalunga**, an old capoeirista, a Black man who escaped a farm long ago and lives free.
      - Funny, not solemn: jokes all the time, teases, makes hard things easy. The boys tell legends about him half as jokes (a capitão do mato killed with one kick, dogs that never find his trail, a prince in Africa, a ghost); he neither confirms nor denies, and laughs.
      - Teaches only while joking: every sentence a joke and a lesson; spicy, ironic, philosophical, or silly (farts, Ezequiel's hard dick). Lessons in small pieces: the ginga as instinct; tricking the opponent; the acting game; the mind game; the ginga as an art. "The strongest kicks are delivered with a smile." The clan's creed: the art protects freedom, never imposes on others as the lords did (ch. 3).
      - His clan's rule: only those who have proven themselves worthy belong, and no outsider may ever come to the hideout (ch. 3).
      - Goes from place to place helping others train in secret; Bastião's master, then Benedito's. Teaches the ginga, the rhythm and the songs before the kicks: "Your body can't sing yet." Benedito's master.
      - His jokes are weapons: the rumour about Ezequiel's first whipping is his, and it spreads through the Recôncavo (ch. 3).
      - Real aspect: capoeira as resistance; runaways living free in the margins.
      - Middle-aged and strong when he taught Ezequiel (c. 1845) and sent him away; old by Benedito's time, and Ezequiel's hunt grows harder to escape. In his late sixties in 1868, when Ezequiel, led by the man he maimed, sets the hideout on fire and kills him with many of the clan; the clan breaks up (Interlude, decided Nov 2, 2026).
      - Chapters: ch. 2 (sends Ezequiel away), ch. 3; stays in the woods when the war comes; killed by Ezequiel c. 1868 (Interlude).
  - **Rosária**, enslaved house servant and apprentice of the Boa Morte sisterhood in Cachoeira (she helps and sings at its festa; the sisters are older freed women); a friend from the terreiro, then his first love.
      - Clever and religious. Her sale mirrors Luzia's.
      - Real aspect: family separation through sale; the Irmandade da Boa Morte in Cachoeira.
      - They meet at Mãe Otília's terreiro when he joins her song; friends first; the romance starts after the fight at the festa in Cachoeira (ch. 3).
      - Chapters: ch. 3; sold to Salvador (ch. 3). Bastião brings word in 1877: she is married, with children, a life of her own in Salvador (ch. 12).
  - **Ezequiel**, overseer and capitão do mato; pardo; born enslaved c. 1834, freed c. 1858.
      - The master's unacknowledged son and Tonico's half-brother; nobody says it aloud.
      - Wants his father to see him as special; the master uses it to control him.
      - Enjoys cruelty and is feared for it: a man of colour who enforces slavery to stay above it.
      - A bully with his own friends, never punished; humiliated by the capoeira boys at the festa in Cachoeira (ch. 3).
      - Taught briefly by Velho Kalunga as a boy and sent away for his pride and violence (ch. 2); hunts him as capitão do mato (ch. 3).
      - After his first whipping, Kalunga's joke about him follows him through the Recôncavo, to the festa and past his firing (ch. 3, Interlude).
      - Taunts Benedito with his freedom; refuses Tonico's orders as Tonico takes on the estate (ch. 3).
      - Spared from the war because he is no longer enslaved (and protected by the master): the freedom Benedito must fight for keeps Ezequiel at home, part of their rivalry (ch. 3).
      - Real aspect: the colour hierarchy (free people of colour as overseers and capitães-do-mato).
      - Chapters: his birth (ch. 1); the goiabeira night, the informer years, the pee prank (ch. 2); freed c. 1858, the first whipping, beaten by Benedito (ch. 3); fired by Tonico c. 1868 with the letter that smells of urine; tortures the man whose hand he took into showing the hideout, burns it and kills Kalunga; killed by Bastião (Interlude, decided Nov 2, 2026).
  - **Mãe Otília**, a Candomblé priestess in Cachoeira.
      - Priestess of the terreiro where he meets Rosária; comes to know him there (ch. 3).
      - Gives him a guia for Ogum; he wears it through the whole war.
      - Real aspect: Candomblé surviving under Catholicism; police repression.
      - Chapters: ch. 3.

## The war: Paraguay, 1865–1870

He goes south with the Zuavos baianos, the all-Black companies of Bahian volunteers, later merged into line battalions. Violence runs both ways (Damião; Albuquerque, Benites). On the battlefield the field is level; in the camp it is not.

### Real figures (cameos)

  - **Quirino Antônio do Espírito Santo**: old Black veteran of Independence, founder of the first zouave company; a father figure; dies in Montevideo, Nov 1865 (ch. 4). Carries Henrique Dias's memory.
  - **Captain Marcolino**: zouave captain and capoeirista; climbs Benedito's back to plant the flag at Curuzu; history forgets the man underneath (ch. 5).
  - **Cândido da Fonseca Galvão, later Dom Obá II**: teaches him to read with old newspapers in the camps of early 1866, before his discharge in March 1866 (ch. 4). Service to the Emperor as a claim to citizenship.
  - **André Rebouças**: seen on the ship *São Francisco*; the first Black man with authority he has seen (ch. 4).
  - **General Osório**: speaks before Tuiuti (ch. 5); disbands the zouave companies (ch. 6).
  - **Duke of Caxias**: seen from afar; his stricter order, or its rumour (ch. 7).
  - **Ana Néri**: Bahian nurse; works beside him in the cholera (ch. 6).
  - **Count d'Eu**: praised the zouaves as "the finest troops"; leads the final hunt for López (ch. 9).

### Comrades (invented)

  - **Gregório "Doutor"**: free-born Black tailor, volunteer; reads, argues politics, believes in citizenship. Writes Benedito's first letters (ch. 4); his death of cholera ends Benedito's faith in the nation (ch. 6). Real aspect: the Voluntários da Pátria.
  - **Ramon Benites**: white gaucho cavalryman; charismatic, racist one minute, loyal the next; Benedito's moral rival inside his own army (ch. 8). Real aspect: violence against Paraguayan civilians; tension among the allies.
  - **Roque Júnior, "Sansão"**: enslaved paulista sent to the war; enormous, thin shins; Benedito first sees him in the camps in the south, a stranger with no story yet (ch. 4).
      - His mother says his father is Pata Seca, the enslaved man used to father children on a São Paulo fazenda; never confirmed; she named him after him. Has brothers and sisters he never met and looks for them in the camps (ch. 6).
      - Gravely wounded at Curupaiti, carried in by Benedito's stretchers (ch. 5); Benedito nurses him and calls him "a rare specimen"; he recovers fast (ch. 6). Saves Benedito's life from Sargento Mbareté in the assault on Humaitá, 16 July 1868, after Benedito is hit in the head, and carries him out: "Now we're even" (ch. 7).
      - Goes back to São Paulo after the war; Benedito never hears of him again (ch. 10).
      - Real aspect: the exploitation of enslaved people's reproduction after the end of the trade (1850); the legend of Pata Seca. \[Roque Júnior: FICTIONAL\]
  - **Padre Felício**: chaplain; keeps the only list with Benedito's real name, which is lost (ch. 6, 9).
  - **Constantino**: young cadet captured at Curupaiti (inspired by the real Bigode); back after two years at the Ybycuí foundry, he tells the Paraguayan side (ch. 7; his return date, 1869, may fit ch. 8 better).
  - **Damião** (Joca Cabinda merged into him, decided Nov 2, 2026): enslaved and freed to serve, like him, brave and bitter. After Curuzu he whips a Paraguayan officer to death the way masters whip slaves, and Benedito is accused with the rest (ch. 5); he deserts and is flogged, and Benedito refuses to help him (ch. 6); in a taken village his freedom becomes the power to do to others what was done to him. Benedito's dark mirror: what he could become, and one answer to "what is freedom?" (ch. 9). Left tied in the burning village; Benedito never learns if he lived (ch. 9).

### The other side

  - **Sargento Mbareté** ("strength" in Guaraní): Paraguayan sergeant, a poor man fighting for someone else's glory; Benedito's mirror (ch. 5, 7). Real aspect: Paraguayan war propaganda (*Cabichuí*).
  - **Ceferina**: a *residenta*; only a short, random encounter in occupied Asunción (ch. 8). Real aspect: Paraguay's demographic catastrophe; the women who rebuilt it.
  - **The boy at Acosta Ñu**: child soldier, about 12, never named (ch. 9). Real aspect: Acosta Ñu, now Paraguay's Children's Day.
  - **Captain Albuquerque**: white Brazilian officer; humiliates and flogs Black soldiers, steals their pay; a stronger enemy than any Paraguayan (ch. 5, 7). Real aspect: racism in the army; unpaid wages.

## After the war: luck, land and the limits of money (1870–1890s)

Setting: Corumbá and the Jacadigo farms on the Bolivian border. The war freed him but did not pay him; the lottery makes him rich but not accepted.

  - **Euclides Bastos**: white comrade from the war; decent but blind. Vouches for Benedito in jail; his land claim goes through while Benedito's stays "under review" (ch. 10). [FICTIONAL] (decided Nov 1, 2026)
  - **Seu Bento**: elderly free Black lottery-ticket seller; his most honest friend; tells him to save; vouches for him against the agent (ch. 10, 12). Real aspect: the lotteries.
  - **Dr. Aurélio Teles**: white lawyer and notary, honest and correct; every deed still calls him *preto* (ch. 14); the land is lost anyway (ch. 18). Real aspect: the 1850 Land Law and the bureaucracy of exclusion.
  - **The Pires family**: old cattle ranchers next door, there before the war; the patriarch is a colonel of the Guarda Nacional, "Coronel Pires"; rich in land and slaves but short of cash and living on credit (decided Nov 2, 2026); wanted the Barão de Vila Maria's empty lands and were slowly occupying them when Benedito bought them; offer to buy him out with false concern; the pack of Benedito, Firmino and Bastião ends any thought of force (ch. 14). Polite in public, hostile in private; fake nobility; slave owners; Liberdade, which owns no one, keeps up with them only while he and his trusted men work themselves hard (ch. 14). Manipulate the wife and sons, never him; lend to the sons. At the teas they philosophize about what it means to be rich (money is not enough; true wealth is culture, manners, a name), quoting Gracián, Seneca, Horace, Chesterfield, Buffon and Maricá: posh, hypocritical, and a provocation rather than a plan (ch. 15, 6b–6f). Real aspect: exclusion of rich Black men; credit networks (ch. 14, 15, 17, 18).
  - **Otávio** (minor): a Corumbá merchant's son; grows up with the sons in the Pires boys' shadow, later cheats them at cards (ch. 15, 18). Real aspect: the white elite of Corumbá.
  - **Firmino**: war comrade freed by the war; a few short encounters in the war (ch. 4–9); in Corumbá they help each other and sometimes work together (ch. 10); his most loyal, wage-paid worker and right hand; dies slowly of an old war wound in the 1880s; his death leaves the farm without its old hands (ch. 14, 15, 17). Real aspect: freed veterans looking for work and land.

## Carmen, the woman from Spain

  - Never named; the narration calls her the woman from Spain. Born c. 1858 in a fishing village near Vigo, Galicia; leaves at about 18 (c. 1876).
  - Why Galicia: the region that sent the most emigrants to Brazil and the River Plate; steamers from Vigo, river steamers up to Asunción and Corumbá.
  - Her world mirrors his: Spain still had slavery in Cuba (Moret Law 1870, abolition 1886), and poor boys went to Cuba while rich families paid the exemption (*quintas*). Her childhood runs through the 1868 revolution, Amadeo I, the First Republic and the Carlist War.

### Family

  - **Rosa Lousada**, mother, lacemaker; a "widow of the living"; lets her go and regrets it (ch. 11). Real aspect: the women left behind (Rosalía de Castro).
  - **Manuel Lousada**, father, a fisherman gone to Buenos Aires; never appears (ch. 11).
  - **Xosé Lousada**, brother, conscripted to Cuba, dies of yellow fever (ch. 11).
  - **Don Faustino**, the village priest; teaches her to read; introduces the recruiter (ch. 11).

### Personality

  - Sharp-tongued, stubborn, practical. Reads, does sums, remembers every humiliation. Catholic, superstitious about the sea, homesick for rain.
  - From object to owner of her own life: bought twice, by the traffickers and by him; she takes over the household money and the farm accounts (ch. 16).
  - Wins the argument that the sons must learn to read; he accepts it, but resents the poshness that comes with it (ch. 16).
  - Arrives with European racism; being sold teaches her his life, but it does not make things simple (ch. 11, 15).
  - Her dream: a respectable marriage, a husband of her choosing, to be a *señora* with a house (ch. 11). (decided Nov 1, 2026)
  - Counts everything: learned from her mother's lacemaking piecework; men believe in luck, women count (ch. 11). (decided Nov 1, 2026)
  - Tells him her whole story over many stolen meetings at Albino's, February–July 1877, finishing just before his win; he once gives her a lottery ticket, and she finds it silly (ch. 10, 11). (decided Nov 2, 2026)
  - Fear of Black men planted by Madame Angélique's warning; afraid of him at first sight, undone by talking, back later (ch. 10, 11, 15). (decided Nov 1, 2026)
  - Saved and bought at once: grateful, then hurt; their marriage has warmth and distance, ups and downs (ch. 13, 14, 15).
  - Once rich: proud of appearances, wants her sons to look like nobility, uneasy that they are partly Black, sides with the white boys; the Pires lure her in: teas at their house, then, after he forbids her to go, at Liberdade, where at least he can watch (ch. 15). In bed she puts a pillow between them, a sign for him not to come close: the fence between the farms becomes the fence between them (ch. 15, 9). They make her feel ignorant; she pretends, then reads, at night, to catch up; and she uses their quotes on Benedito, in front of the sons, to make him feel ignorant in turn (ch. 15). At home she wins every argument with them (Gracián on the boys and the roundup, Seneca on his locks); her own ledgers show that the cattle pay for all of it, and she says nothing (ch. 16, 6).
  - Traumas: the deception, the passage, the fear of the brothel, losing Ruchla, never writing home the truth (ch. 11, 14).

### The relationship with him (decided Nov 1, 2026)

  - The most complex relationship in the book. Both were property: "not free" binds them, but means different things (him: not owned, dignity, land; her: choosing a husband, respectability).
  - Before the lottery: a kind, safe companion, never a candidate; brave, polite, strong, skilled, but poor. She admires him, never connects; never understands his songs, not like Rosária (ch. 10).
  - He probably never loved her: he liked her, she was beautiful, few women around, and she was white (whitening, his view) (ch. 13).
  - Her "yes" at the altar: calm, the best of bad options; free in law, not in fact. The bond is debt and gratitude (ch. 13).
  - Warm in the first years, cold later; gratitude wears slowly into resentment, no single breaking point (ch. 14, 15).
  - Learns his life by being sold, then relapses into European racism once rich and courted by the elite (ch. 15).
  - Learns of Rosária once, from Bastião, and never forgets it (ch. 15).
  - At his death her grief is real: she loved him in her way, and it surprises her (ch. 17).
  - The widow: takes over with sons who cannot and will not run the farm; fights with her ledgers and loses; her hem money is a small comfort, not what saves the family, which survives without the fortune (ch. 18).

## Her passage: Vigo → Buenos Aires → Corumbá (c. 1876–1877)

A free European woman could not legally be a slave in Brazil; trafficked women were sold anyway, with no papers and no law that reached them. The family legend of "buying a Spanish girl" is literally true (ch. 13).

  - **Señor Alvarado**, the *gancho*: charming recruiter with letters "from girls who married well" (ch. 11). Real aspect: the ganchos; the *trata de blancas*.
  - **Ruchla (Raquel)**: Jewish girl from Austrian Galicia, her closest friend; sold in Buenos Aires; searched for for years (ch. 11, 14). Real aspect: the *polacas* (later the Zwi Migdal).
  - **Capitán Iriarte**: the steamer's captain; bribed, once kind (ch. 11). Real aspect: corrupt ports.
  - **Doña Engracia**: older Galician woman emigrating legally (ch. 11). Real aspect: ordinary emigration beside trafficking.
  - **Madame Angélique**: French madam in Buenos Aires; keeps her untouched to sell her dear (ch. 11). Real aspect: the Buenos Aires brothel economy.
  - **Seu Albino**: Portuguese tavern owner in Corumbá, a trafficker in secret; crude, cruel, a good host; passes her off as his niece; wants one big private sale; always threatens her with the brothel, and it works (ch. 11). After Benedito's first offer he hides her completely and raises the price; at the showdown he haggles, then sells her for two contos, "double what you ever dreamed of" (ch. 13). Real aspect: Portuguese dominance of commerce and trafficking.
  - **Lucília**: free Black laundress at Albino's; her first friend; teaches her Portuguese, then is kept away from her while Albino hides her (ch. 11, 13); godmother of her first son (ch. 11, 13, 14). Real aspect: free Black women's work and *compadrio*.

## The sons

  - Born c. 1878–1890; rich, mixed-race (*mestiços*), accepted by neither side; grow up in the years of abolition and the Republic.
  - Spoiled and wounded: never learn the land; suffer racism however rich; proud and angry; use money to compensate (ch. 16).
  - Prefer their mother's world (reading, manners, good clothes, coffees with the elite children, riding) to their father's farm and songs; think he works too much and smells of cow shit (ch. 16).
  - Lose everything at the card table to the white boys they grew up with (ch. 18). The *jogo do bicho* (Rio, 1892) stays in the background, for grandma's time.
  - Four sons (working names, decided Nov 2, 2026), oldest to youngest: Afonso, Inácio, Manuel, José (renamed from Xosé (decided Nov 2, 2026)).
  - **Afonso**: the heir, named by his mother; raised by her and Lucília; wants the white elite's acceptance (ch. 14, 16). The son beaten at São João, afraid of his father all his life (ch. 16, 18); never lets a beard grow until after his father's death (ch. 16, 18). Loses everything at the last card game, told from inside him (ch. 18).
  - **Inácio**: named after his father's father. The follower (decided Nov 2, 2026): copies Afonso in everything, and gambles to keep up with him (ch. 16, 18).
  - **Manuel**: named after her father, who went to Buenos Aires and stopped writing. The dreamer (decided Nov 2, 2026): quiet and bookish, the one who believes the Pires' quotes; goes to study in Cuiabá or Rio, which costs cattle (ch. 16, 18).
  - **José**: the youngest, named after her brother Xosé, lost in Cuba; his father's favourite. (His garimpo story was removed (decided Nov 2, 2026).)
  - **Others / daughters**: grandma's line; who carried the story down to her: to decide.

  - **The fall (1890s):** the sons, the Pires family, card tables; grandma's line.
