# Character Bible

Oct 1, 2026 · Rafael

## How to use this bible

This is the master reference for every character in the book. The history is real; the lives are invented. Each character exists to carry one real piece of 19th-century Brazil (or Spain, or Paraguay) into the story.

  - **Neither he nor the woman from Spain is ever named.** Everyone around him calls him something different: his master, his mother, the army, the newspapers. The reader never learns his "true" name, just as the family lost it. That gap is one of the book's themes. Working names, for planning only and never in the book text: Joaquim (him) and Carmen (the woman from Spain).
  - **Grandma's story is the spine:** born enslaved, freed by the war in Paraguay, wins the lottery, buys farms, "buys" a Spanish girl and marries her, dies, and the sons gamble it all away. Every invented detail has to fit around these beats.
  - **Real people appear as cameos only.** The invented characters do the plot work, and real figures (Rebouças, Dom Obá, Ana Néri, Caxias) pass through scenes. Each row says which real fact the character brings in. The tables are a menu, not a cast list: during writing we choose who appears, who is only mentioned and who is left out.
  - **Grounding:** the Zuavos baianos material comes from the *Dados gerais* source doc (Bento; Kraay). The other historical anchors come from general knowledge and are marked *to verify* in the fact map at the end.
  - **Ages are working assumptions** so the timeline holds together. Change them freely.
  - **Chance, not morals.** Much of what happens is luck: a sale, a fever, a recruiter's mood, a draw, a card. The book never judges or teaches. It shows the reasons, fears and hopes behind each decision and lets the reader weigh them.
  - **No heroes, no villains.** Every character, Black or white, Brazilian, Spanish or Paraguayan, has decent and ugly sides and contradictions. Racism also runs among Black people: Africans and *crioulos*, *pretos* and *pardos* (see Ezequiel).

## The protagonist: the man without a name

He is born in Brazil around 1846, a *crioulo* (Brazilian-born enslaved person) in the Recôncavo of Bahia, the sugar region around Salvador. His mother was born in Africa. Born in Brazil, he has the same rootlessness as everyone else in the book: he belongs to no country that will claim him. After the war he never goes back to Bahia: he stays on the Mato Grosso frontier, and his farms are later at Jacadigo, near Corumbá, on the Bolivian border (today Mato Grosso do Sul).

**Why Brazil-born and not African-born?** The legal Atlantic slave trade to Brazil ended in 1850 (Eusébio de Queirós law). A man fit to fight in 1865 who had been born in Africa would have to be in his thirties, or else smuggled in illegally. A Brazil-born son of an African mother gives us both worlds. He speaks Portuguese but hums his mother's Yoruba songs without knowing what they mean.

### Parents

  - **Mother, called Luzia by the masters (her Yoruba name is lost, like his).** She is Nagô (Yoruba), taken from the Bight of Benin as a girl. In Salvador she is a *ganhadeira*: an enslaved street seller who hands most of her earnings to her owner and gives the rest away. She is a healer who believes she is close to the orixás and to luck: destiny, she says, lives in each person's orí (to verify), so money is meant to flow, as gifts, offerings and help for whoever asks. She never saves. She was in Salvador as a young woman during the **Malê Revolt of 1835**, the uprising of Muslim Africans. After it, Africans were watched, deported and whipped. She teaches him to keep his head down and to trust luck: the orixás will open the way, she tells him, and one day they will both be free. That faith in luck is the first thing she gives him, and he keeps it all his life. The master forces himself on her at night; she bears it in silence and hides it from her son. On the estate she becomes its nurse, healer and midwife, with Yoruba plant knowledge. He learns from her how to clean and bind wounds, stop fevers and keep the sick alive; it saves him, and others, in the war. As a girl she learned healing and ritual singing together, and her voice is extraordinary: admired on the estate, in the Cachoeira market, even by the master's family. She teaches him her songs. Her voice is his strongest memory of her. When he is about 12, older now and replaced in the master's attentions by a younger woman, she is sold south to pay his debts (the internal slave trade toward the coffee farms), healer or not. He never sees her again. Her arc, an important growth: the sale breaks her faith. Why would the gods do such a thing? She rebels against them and, for the first time in her life, starts to save, with one goal she has held since he was a child: to buy her freedom and go back to her son. It takes years (she joins a junta, a savings circle). Once she has enough, an official scams her and sells her a freedom worth nothing (placeholder, a short episode; details to come). She starts again and buys her freedom in Vassouras, in the Paraíba Valley. She no longer waits for luck: she drives her own and makes her own destiny. When Tonico, travelling south, finds her, she is already free and saving for the journey north; she has never known her son went to war. She writes her last letter to her son herself, with a little help from Tonico, who is impressed, and tells him how she stopped counting on luck. From Tonico she learns her son is at the war, and she dies shortly after, a free woman, before she can travel (c. 1869). *This is his second wound; his father's death was the first.*
  - **Father: Inácio.** Inácio (working name), an enslaved boiling-house worker and Luzia's companion, is the man he calls father. During the harvest the boy spends days beside him at the mill and boiling house and learns how a sugar estate runs: the steps of production, the calendar, who gives orders, what a crate of sugar is worth. It is the seed of the farms he will run at Jacadigo. He dies in the cholera epidemic of 1855, when the boy is 9. Inácio is his father. Gossip about Luzia and the master follows the boy anyway, and other enslaved boys use it to taunt him.

### Personality

He carries a mark in one eye for life, from a childhood accident: Tonico, helping him clear land, hits him with a piece of wood while they play (Chapter 2). Watchful, quiet and good at numbers. He is proud in a way he learned to hide. Superstitious, and he trusts luck more than people: he has seen hard work make no difference. Funny with a few close friends and cold with everyone else. He is vain about clothes, a trait that starts with the zouave uniform. The key to him: he is innocent of what he is accused of, and guilty of what nobody knows about (Acosta Ñu, refusing to help Joca escape; more to develop).

He loves music and dance — the batuques and sambas de roda of the Recôncavo are where he feels free. He is very athletic and an avid capoeirista, a game learned in secret with Bastião, played to drums, clapping and song, never yet the berimbau. In the war his capoeira becomes a weapon. Slavery also left its mark: violence is the first language he learned. He is not an angry man day to day, but he is explosive, and an outburst comes fast when he feels disrespected (being called moleque is enough). Once free and rich he is demanding: his wishes are orders, the way he was once given orders. He is jealous of his wife and quick to suspect any man who looks at her (Chapters, ch. 23). Good with the land and with men: still strong, he works beside his workers, knows their language and finds smarter, easier, more profitable ways to run the farm; this makes him respected, and makes the slave-owning neighbours fear him as a competitor (Chapters, ch. 21). The Pires can manipulate his wife and sons, but not him; he sees through them. In old age his memory fails; the Pires and his family use it to gaslight him, and he doubts even himself. Never named as a disease: the reader should not know whether his memory is wrong, the Pires are lying or his wife doubts him for no reason (Chapters, ch. 24). His most loyal man is Firmino, a war comrade (Chapters, ch. 21). The roda is where his force turns into play. Violence freed him; it will also poison his family.

### What he wants (goals)

His goals change with his life, and each one pulls him into the next chapter of it. Chance keeps changing the board under him.

  - **As a boy:** to be free, as Luzia promises him: the orixás and luck will open the way. From her he learns to count on luck.
  - **1865:** when he learns the army frees enslaved men who serve, he wants to go. It is the first door to freedom he has ever seen. He makes sure he is the one offered as Tonico's substitute, and the master thinks it was his own idea.
  - **During and after the war:** to find his mother and buy her freedom. He looks for her name in every list of the freed.
  - **After the lottery:** to be a senhor nobody gives orders to, and to save the woman from Spain as he could not save his mother. He gives her no choice, and in saving her he does to her what was done to him.

### Traumas carried

  - First wound: his father's death in the cholera of 1855, and the lament Luzia sings over him, the saddest song he has ever heard; it follows him all his life. Second wound: separation from his mother. He keeps looking for her name in every list of the freed, until Tonico's letter (c. 1877) tells him she is dead.
  - Going to war in a white boy's place, even though he wanted to go. Freedom bought with his body.
  - Cholera camps: watching men die in hours.
  - Acosta Ñu (1869), where the allies fought Paraguayan boys disguised with false beards to look older (Clodfelter, Warfare and Armed Conflicts, 4th ed., 2017, p. 322). This is the memory he never tells. It comes back years later at a São João festival (see below).
  - Accused of what he tried to stop. He arrives too late at Joca's whipping and after Damião's rampage has begun; he stops what he can, and is still accused of rape and killing, simply because he belongs to the Black troop. For now these remain accusations: no trial, no punishment, only the stain. Your family's oral tradition about Brazilian soldiers and Paraguayan women echoes here: the reader sees how such a story can attach to a man who did not do it. He lives with it as anger and silence. *(See Ramón Benites in the war section.)*
  - **His wife and kids reject his African culture. They are not interested in Capoeira or the songs he sings. His ways and traumas make it harder for him to build a good relationship with them and its makes it even harder from them to accept/like his culture. The are small aspects of racism even within the family.**

### Key later event: the moustaches of São João

Around 1886–1888, a June night on the farm. One of his sons, about 7 or 8, comes home from the *festa de São João* dressed as a *caipira* (country bumpkin), with a moustache and beard painted on with burnt cork, as children still do at the festas juninas today. He is laughing, holding a paper lantern and smelling of bonfire smoke.

The father sees the painted beard on a small boy's face in the firelight and is back at Acosta Ñu. He snaps and beats the child, hard, without a word of explanation. Then he scrubs the boy's face until the skin is raw.

  - **What the reader knows and the family doesn't:** the reader saw the boy at Acosta Ñu. The wife and children never learn why. He never explains, so the beating looks like pure cruelty.
  - **The wife:** she pulls the child away. After years of building the farms together, she no longer recognises him, and it feels like a betrayal of the partnership they had. For the first time she stands against him in front of the children. The distance that opens that night is the deepest in their marriage, and it never fully closes.
  - **The son:** he grows up afraid of his father and never understands. The night he loses everything is the first night he allowed himself to have a beard - which is some time after his fathers death.
  - **Him:** he never goes to a São João again. Every June he stays in the dark while the bonfires burn on the neighbouring farms. Only the reader understands.
  - **How it's written (blended style):** no explanation in the text. The scene cuts straight from the painted beard to a single image of Acosta Ñu, then back to the boy crying. The reader connects the two.
  - **Real aspects surfaced:** the *festas juninas* (bonfires, *quadrilha* dances, children dressed as *caipiras*) as a living tradition, and the war's trauma passed silently to children who never saw it.
  - *To verify:* that drawn-on moustaches and beards for children were already part of São João in the 1880s, and the false beards at Acosta Ñu are reported in Clodfelter's statistical encyclopedia (p. 322), a secondary reference. A primary source (1869 war reports or witnesses) is still to find. If the 1880s detail doesn't hold, use charcoal face-painting or another costume detail from the period.

### Names others give him (a narrative device)

At home the master's family calls him "the boy". His mother has a secret Yoruba name for him. The army roll gives him a registered name we never see. His comrades call him **Zuavo**. Later, newspapers call him "the lucky Black man" and his wife, who never says his name, calls him "senhor". In rare private moments she calls him something else, which the reader never hears. The narration only ever says *he*.

## Theme: luck, fortune and the gambling inheritance

The family's relationship with luck runs through the whole book, from the protagonist's lottery ticket to the sons' card tables, and on to grandma, who played the lottery all her life. Luck brings the family both its fortune and its ruin.

  - **Not a curse but a worldview.** Luck is a rational belief for people whose effort never paid off. And luck sees no race: the lottery wheel and the cholera do not check his colour. He learned it first from Luzia, who trusted the orixás and luck with everything she had; his decision to go to war is a bet on luck, and the lottery win is the fruit of that belief. He knows some luck brings more luck, but never takes it for granted. Luzia herself turns against luck after she is sold and makes her own destiny; her last letter tells him so, and it reaches him just after the lottery has proved his faith right. He expected a mother proud of his luck and finds a different woman: a kind of cultural shock. Its first effect is The Purchase: he decides instead of waiting for luck, and decides the colour of his family too. Slavery, unpaid army wages and a land claim left "under review" taught him that work changes nothing and luck sometimes does. The book never moralises against gambling; it shows why people kept believing in it.
  - **The win is the trap.** The lottery proves his faith in luck "right". From then on he trusts chance more than planning, and teaches his sons that money comes from the sky, not from keeping it.
  - **Each generation has its own relationship with luck.** **He** sees luck as justice, a judge above the masters. **The woman from Spain** trusts only what she can count and control: she has seen how a single sale can decide a whole life. Luck against accounts is one of the recurring arguments of their marriage. **The sons first gamble to cover the farm's losses: they never learned the land, and luck is the easy way out their father showed them. They keep gambling out of pride and the desire to be like the white elite: to sit as equals at the white men's table. They resent their father, but they do not want to be poor; they lose everything because they cannot bear to look small.** Gambling is the one place where their money buys them equality, for one night. **Grandma** plays small, regularly, as a ritual of hope. She is the bridge to the present in the epilogue.
  - **Signs and dreams.** Luck is tied to faith: the Ogum *guia*, his mother's songs, and dreams turned into numbers (a dream points to a lottery number).
  - **Language:** characters say *vício do jogo* or *mania*, never "addiction".
  - **Real aspects surfaced:** imperial and provincial lotteries; the 1874 military recruitment by lottery (*Lei do Sorteio*, to verify); the lottery as one of the few ways up for poor and Black Brazilians, then and now.

## Inheritance: what passes down

Four behaviours pass from Luzia to him, to his sons, and down to grandma. They are shown through scenes and repeated lines, never explained: a thought or sentence comes back in another mouth decades later, slightly changed; the reader notices, the characters never do. Inheritance is not fate: some chains break or mutate, by chance.

1\. Faith in luck. Luzia gives her money away and trusts the orixás: "The orixás will open the way." He bets on the war and on the lottery: "Luck will open the way." A son stakes the farm on a hand of cards from a marked deck: "The cards will open the way." Silvio turns garimpeiro, waiting for the stone that will change everything: "The ground will open the way." Grandma plays the lottery all her life.

2\. Violence. As a boy he is beaten and mistreated by the master and thinks: "Why does he have to be so angry?" He becomes violent himself (outbursts when disrespected, the São João beating), and his sons think the same words about him. Ezequiel shows the chain at its darkest: he watches the landlord beat his mother for a sound, then beats a silent man for the same sound, and enjoys it. Grandma: "she could be really mean" (prologue).

3\. Fear of losing everything. Luzia is sold and later scammed. For him it starts with the lottery: once everyone knows he is rich, he is exploited, loses his privacy and stops trusting anyone. On the farm, rich and Black, he trusts no one: the white neighbours hate a rich Black man, and Black people envy him. He hides his wealth, fires his gun into the sky at night to scare off thieves, and accuses his sons of stealing from him. Peak: he hosts fugitives at his farm for a while, then sends them all away in anger, out of suspicion and jealousy (Chapters, ch. 23). He is paranoid, but not always wrong: the white boys who grow up with his sons, whose bad signs everyone calls his paranoia, are the men who later take everything (Chapters, ch. 21 and 25). Over time it gets blurry: he is often right, but as his memory fades the Pires and his own family use it to gaslight him, and he doubts even himself (Chapters, ch. 24). The sons lose the farm anyway. Grandma repeats him without knowing it: the shotgun fired into the sky at a noise on the roof, the accusations that someone has stolen her things (prologue).

4\. Music. Luzia's Yoruba songs, sung over Inácio and in the market. He hums them through the war and on the farm. His wife and children reject them; one son hums one without knowing what it is. What reaches grandma: to decide.

Not inherited, broken: keeping his head down. He learns it from Luzia to survive. Capoeira, learning to fight and choosing the war change him; in the war he stops lowering his head and stands up against what other soldiers do (Joca's whipping, Damião's rampage, the abuse of Paraguayan women). He never lowers it again, and on the farm that pride turns into the explosive anger of the senhor.

## The war: Paraguay, 1865–1870

He goes south in 1865–66 with the **Zuavos baianos**, the all-Black companies of Bahian volunteers. After heavy losses they are merged into ordinary line battalions. Most of his comrades are invented. Real officers appear as people he serves under or meets once. Violence in this war runs both ways: Black soldiers do to white men what was done to them (Joca, Damião), and white soldiers and officers do it to Black men (Albuquerque, Benites). On the battlefield the field is level: a bullet does not check colour, and Black and white fight in the same mud. In the camp it is not: Black soldiers are flogged more, their pay is stolen, and no ex-zouave rose above captain.

### Damião: a dark mirror (invented)

### Enslaved like him, sent to the war as a substitute like him, a good soldier and a brave one. After a battle, when their troops take a Paraguayan village (option: the sack of Piribebuy, 12 August 1869, four days before Acosta Ñu; to verify), Damião feels freer than ever. He kills white men and boys, rapes women and girls, eats and drinks all he wants. His freedom is the power to do to others what was done to him: violence as a language, spoken at full volume for the first time.

  - ### **Why he matters.** He is what the protagonist could become, and one honest answer to the book's question "what is freedom?". The protagonist understands his logic, and hates it, because the women and girls remind him of his mother and of the master's nights.
  - ### **The problem.** Drunk, Damião goes too far and puts everyone in danger. Options: he turns on a mother and daughter the protagonist is hiding; he shoots at a Brazilian officer, risking reprisals on the whole Black company; or he sets fire to a building with wounded inside.
  - ### **The fight.** The protagonist was not there when it began. He arrives later, fights him, capoeira against brute force, and stops it. He is still accused of rape and killing, simply because he belongs to the Black troop.
  - ### **The ending (to decide).** He kills him; or he ties him up and leaves him behind in the burning village, so chance decides whether Damião lives, and he never learns which; or he hands him to the officers, and Damião is flogged or shot, which he carries as guilt. Option: Damião reappears years later in Corumbá.
  - ### **How to write it.** No graphic description: the violence is seen in its aftermath and in the protagonist's reaction. The reader gets Damião's reasons, never the narrator's approval. White soldiers do the same in the same war (Ramon Benites), so the atrocity belongs to the war, not to a race; otherwise the scene repeats the racist propaganda that called Black soldiers *macacos*.
  - ### **Real aspect surfaced.** Violence against Paraguayan civilians in the final campaign; what enslaved men carried into the war with them.
  - ### **Option.** Merge him with Joca Cabinda, who already has the bitterness and the desertion.

## After the war: luck, land and the limits of money (1870–1890s)

The war freed him but did not pay him. The lottery makes him rich, but it cannot make him accepted. These characters test what money can and cannot buy for a Black man in the last decades of the Empire. Setting: Corumbá and the Jacadigo farms on the Bolivian border (Mato Grosso, today Mato Grosso do Sul).

## The woman from Spain

**No name. Like him, she is never named; the narration calls her the woman from Spain (working name for planning: Carmen).** She is born around 1858 in a fishing village on the Galician coast near Vigo, and leaves Spain at about 18 (c. 1876). Galicia is the best fit: it was the region of Spain that sent the most emigrants to Brazil and the River Plate, and Vigo had direct steamers to Rio and Buenos Aires. From Buenos Aires, river steamers went up the Paraná and Paraguay rivers to Asunción and Corumbá.

### What was happening in Spain

Her whole childhood happens during upheaval: the **1868 Glorious Revolution** (Queen Isabel II exiled), a foreign king (Amadeo I), the short **First Republic (1873)**, the **Third Carlist War (1872–76)** and the Bourbon Restoration (1874). In Galicia that means poverty, bad harvests and mass emigration. Her story mirrors his:

  - **Spain still had slavery in Cuba.** The Moret Law of 1870 freed children born to enslaved mothers, a year before Brazil's equivalent (1871). Spain did not abolish slavery in Cuba until 1886.
  - **The poor fought for the rich there too.** Conscription (*quintas*) sent poor boys to the Ten Years' War in Cuba (1868–78), while wealthy families paid a fee to exempt their sons. It is the same bargain that sent him to Paraguay.

### Personality and arc

Sharp-tongued, stubborn and practical. She reads, does sums, and remembers every humiliation. She is Catholic, superstitious about the sea and homesick for rain.

  - **From object to owner of her own life.** She is bought twice: first by the traffickers, then by him. She eventually takes control of the household's money and the farm accounts.
  - **Her own prejudice, and its limits.** She arrives with European racism. Being sold in Buenos Aires is the first time she is treated as goods, and she comes to recognise his life in hers, but that does not make it simple. She never had a choice: he bought her from her trafficker and married her. Before the lottery she had told him she would rather be sold to a rich man than end up in a brothel, meaning as a maid or a servant. Years later he throws it back at her: "I am a rich man. I saved you." She answers: "I meant as a maid or a servant, not a sex slave. You didn't save me, you own me. You made me your slave; you never gave me the choice." The argument breaks his image of himself as her saviour and puts him in the place of the master, an inner conflict he does not know how to deal with. She is grateful to be out of Albino's house and hopes he will not be too bad. It sours as he turns out harsh and strict, his wishes orders. Still, it is a marriage with ups and downs, not only coldness. They understand each other in some things (the price on their bodies, exile, numbers, secrets) and not at all in others (his faith in luck against her ledgers, his culture, his silences, her past in Buenos Aires). The children grow up inside both the warmth and the distance.
  - Pride and appearances. Once rich, she becomes materialistic and proud of how rich she looks: clothes, furniture, the house, how the family is seen in Corumbá. She wants her sons to look like nobility. They are partly Black, and it bothers her; she is a bit racist. She does not force them to work the land and does not like to see them doing heavy work: it makes them look like slaves, and that disgusts her. This is part of the rift with him, who learned the land with his hands. She is always in favour of the white boys who grow up with her sons (the Pires sons and Otávio): for her they are a way to get closer to their parents, to be among the noble. At first she is young and innocent, and the couple is fairly happy (Chapters, ch. 21); the pride grows slowly. The Pires lure her in: they see she wants to be included, use her to learn about the farm, and turn her against her husband, suggesting she is not treated as she should be and showing her the life she is missing.
  - **Traumas:** the deception; the passage; the fear of the brothel; losing Ruchla; never being able to write home the truth. She tells the children she came "to marry a rich man", and that is the version your grandma heard.

## Her passage: Vigo → Buenos Aires → Corumbá (c. 1876–1877)

A free European woman could not legally be a slave in Brazil. Trafficked women were bought and sold anyway, with no papers and no law that reached them. She is simply trafficked, from house to house, and finally to him. He meets her at Albino's tavern before the lottery, where everyone takes her for Albino's niece, and likes her; only she tells him the truth; they bond over not being free. After the win, Albino refuses to sell her to a Black man and raises the price as news of the prize spreads. After reading Luzia's letter he forces Albino, with a simple threat (he has no proof, but Albino does not want trouble), to accept the original price, one conto (see Chapters, ch. 20). In his mind he is saving her, as he could not save his mother. It is a decision, made after reading Luzia's letter, not luck. It is also a choice about colour: married to a white woman, his children will not be as Black, and as cursed, as he is (his view, shaped by the whitening ideology of the time, never the narrator's). The colour of his family will not be left to luck. In hers she is saved and bought at once: spared the brothel, but given to a man she did not choose. He gives her no choice, and so, without seeing it, he enslaves her. This is the seed of most of their conflicts. The family legend of "buying a Spanish girl" is literally true.

## The sons (waiting for your insights)

The sons are born around 1878–1890. They grow up rich, mixed-race (*mestiços*), and accepted by neither side, in the years of **abolition (1888)** and the **Republic (1889)**. They lose everything gambling. A strong real anchor: the ***jogo do bicho*** ("animal game"), an illegal lottery, was invented in Rio in 1892 by the Barão de Drummond; in the book it stays in the background, for grandma's time. They lose everything at the card tables of Corumbá, a dark echo of the lottery ticket that made their father rich.

Spoiled and wounded. They refuse to work the land, and their mother lets them. They grow up spoiled and cut off from reality. Still, they are Black, however rich, and they suffer racism; they use money to make up for it. They are proud, and angry that they cannot be respected because of their race. That pride and anger drive the fall: they lose the farms and the money at the card table, not in the jogo do bicho. It starts because they never learned the land: the farms lose money, and they gamble to cover it, betting on luck as their father did. At the card table, white men who pretend to be their friends (the Pires sons and Otávio, the white boys they grew up with) play with marked cards, let them win now and then, and slowly eat away the fortune, until a last game where the sons go all in on what they think is a great hand, and lose it all.
