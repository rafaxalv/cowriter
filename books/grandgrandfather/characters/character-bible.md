# Character Bible

Oct 1, 2026 · Rafael · reorganized Oct 26, 2026 into short bullets; the events live in Chapters.

## How to use this bible

Who each character is, in short bullets: role, personality, motives, relationships, the real piece of history they carry, and pointers to the chapters where their events happen. The events themselves, with all their details, are in Chapters. The history is real; the lives are invented.

  - **Neither he nor the woman from Spain is ever named.** Everyone around him calls him something different. The reader never learns his "true" name, just as the family lost it. Working names, for planning only and never in the book text: Benedito (him) and Carmen (the woman from Spain).
  - **Grandma's story is the spine:** born enslaved, freed by the war in Paraguay, wins the lottery, buys farms, "buys" a Spanish girl and marries her, dies, and the sons gamble it all away. Every invented detail fits around these beats.
  - **Real people appear as cameos only.** Invented characters do the plot work; real figures (Rebouças, Dom Obá, Ana Néri, Caxias) pass through. The lists are a menu, not a cast list.
  - **Grounding:** the Zuavos baianos material comes from the *Dados gerais* source doc (Bento; Kraay); other anchors are marked *to verify* in the fact map at the end.
  - **Ages are working assumptions** so the timeline holds together.
  - **Chance, not morals.** Much of what happens is luck. The book never judges; it shows reasons, fears and hopes.
  - **No heroes, no villains.** Everyone has decent and ugly sides. Racism also runs among Black people: Africans and *crioulos*, *pretos* and *pardos* (see Ezequiel).

## Benedito, the man without a name

  - Born c. 1846, a *crioulo* (Brazil-born enslaved) on a sugar estate in the Recôncavo of Bahia; his mother was born in Africa.
  - Why Brazil-born: the Atlantic trade ended in 1850, so an African-born man fit to fight in 1865 would be in his thirties or smuggled. A Brazil-born son of an African mother gives both worlds: he speaks Portuguese and hums Yoruba songs he cannot translate.
  - Belongs to no country that will claim him. After the war he never goes back to Bahia; he stays on the Mato Grosso frontier, at Corumbá, and later Jacadigo (ch. 10, 18).

### Parents

  - **Luzia** (the masters' name; her Yoruba name is lost, like his). Nagô (Yoruba).
      - Healer, midwife and singer with an extraordinary voice; Yoruba plant knowledge, learned with ritual song.
      - Trusts the orixás and luck; money is meant to flow, so she never saves. "The orixás will open the way."
      - Teaches him to keep his head down, to trust luck, her songs, and how to treat wounds and fevers (it saves him in the war).
      - Silent about the master's nights; hides them from Inácio and her son.
      - Arc: the sale breaks her faith; she turns against the gods, saves for the first time, is scammed, starts again and buys her own freedom. She stops waiting for luck and makes her own destiny.
      - Chapters: sold as a girl, ganhadeira in Salvador, the Malê Revolt, sold to the estate c. 1843, meets Inácio, his birth (ch. 1); healer, singer, sold south c. 1858 (ch. 2); free healer in Vassouras, her letter, her death c. 1869 (Interlude); the letter reaches him (ch. 17).
  - **Inácio** (working name). Enslaved boiling-house worker, Luzia's companion, his father.
      - Teaches him the sugar chain at the mill: the seed of the farms he will run.
      - Chapters: nursed by Luzia after a burn (ch. 1); dies of cholera in 1855 (ch. 2). The boy is born all black (preto), which ends the gossip about Luzia and the master (ch. 1–2).

### Personality

  - Watchful, quiet, good at numbers. Proud in a way he learned to hide.
  - Superstitious; trusts luck more than people, because hard work never made a difference.
  - Funny with a few close friends, cold with everyone else.
  - Vain about clothes, starting with the zouave uniform (ch. 4).
  - After Tonico, his friends are the capoeira boys and the terreiro: the time he is closest to his African community (ch. 3).
  - A fighter, hungry for freedom after his father's death and his mother's sale; dreams of running away, held back by fear of being caught and killed (ch. 3).
  - A healer, like Luzia: he repeats her steps aloud and sings her songs as he works, a ritual begun at his father's sickbed (ch. 2). In the war it makes him one of the healers, respected, often spared from the front line (ch. 6).
  - A mark in one eye for life, from a childhood accident with Tonico (ch. 2).
  - Loves music and dance (batuques, samba de roda): where he feels free. Athletic, a capoeirista; in the war capoeira becomes a weapon (ch. 3, 5).
  - Violence is the first language he learned: not angry day to day, but explosive when disrespected ("moleque" is enough).
  - Free and rich, he is demanding: his wishes are orders. Jealous of his wife (ch. 20, 22).
  - Good with the land and with men: works beside his workers, knows their language, finds smarter ways to run the farm (ch. 20).
  - Sees through the Pires, though they can manipulate his wife and sons (ch. 20).
  - In old age his memory fails, never named as a disease (ch. 23).
  - The key to him: innocent of what he is accused of, guilty of what nobody knows (Acosta Ñu, refusing to help Joca; ch. 6, 9).

### What he wants

  - As a boy: to be free, as Luzia promises.
  - 1865: to go to war, the first door to freedom he has seen; he puts himself forward the moment the Coronel offers enslaved men (ch. 3).
  - During and after the war: to find his mother and buy her freedom (ch. 11, 17).
  - After the lottery: to be a senhor nobody gives orders to, and to save the woman from Spain as he could not save his mother; he gives her no choice (ch. 19).

### Wounds and traumas

  - First wound: Inácio's death and Luzia's lament, the saddest song he has ever heard (ch. 2).
  - Second wound: losing his mother; he searches the lists of the freed until her letter comes (ch. 2, 11, 17).
  - Freedom bought with his body, offered by his master, even though he chose to go (ch. 3).
  - The cholera camps (ch. 6).
  - Acosta Ñu: the boy with the false beard; the memory he never tells (ch. 9), which returns at São João (ch. 21).
  - Accused of what he tried to stop (Joca's whipping, Damião's rampage): no trial, only the stain (ch. 5, 9). Echoes the family's oral tradition about Brazilian soldiers and Paraguayan women.
  - His wife and sons reject his African culture (ch. 20).

### Names others give him

  - The master's family: "the boy". Luzia: a secret Yoruba name. The army roll: a registered name we never see. Comrades: "Zuavo". Newspapers: "the lucky Black man". His wife: "senhor", and in rare private moments something the reader never hears. The narration: only *he*.
  - Padre Felício's list holds his real name and is lost (ch. 6, 9). He tells his wife his true name before he dies; the reader never hears it (ch. 23).

## Theme: luck, fortune and the gambling inheritance

  - Luck is a worldview, not a curse: a rational belief for people whose effort never paid off, and luck sees no race.
  - The lottery win is the trap: it proves his faith "right", and he teaches his sons that money comes from the sky (ch. 17).
  - Luzia turns against luck; her letter reaches him just as the lottery proves his faith right. The shock leads to The Purchase: he decides instead of waiting (ch. 17, 19).
  - Each generation: he sees luck as justice; the woman from Spain trusts only what she can count (luck against ledgers is one of their recurring arguments); the sons gamble to cover losses, then out of pride, to sit as equals at the white men's table (ch. 24); grandma plays small and regularly, a ritual of hope (Epilogue).
  - Signs and dreams: the Ogum guia, his mother's songs, a dream that points to a number (ch. 12).
  - Language: *vício do jogo* or *mania*, never "addiction". The book never moralises against gambling.
  - Real aspects: imperial and provincial lotteries; the 1874 *Lei do Sorteio* (to verify); the lottery as one of the few ways up for poor and Black Brazilians.

## Inheritance: what passes down

Shown through scenes and repeated lines, never explained. Some chains break or mutate, by chance.

  - **Faith in luck.** "The orixás will open the way" (Luzia) → "Luck will open the way" (him) → "The cards will open the way" (a son, ch. 24) → "The ground will open the way" (Silvio, ch. 24) → grandma's lottery (Epilogue).
  - **Violence.** "Why does he have to be so angry?": the boy about the master (ch. 2), his son about him (ch. 21). Ezequiel shows the chain at its darkest (ch. 2). Grandma "could be really mean" (prologue).
  - **Fear of losing everything.** Luzia sold and scammed (ch. 2, Interlude); his distrust starts with the lottery (ch. 17), grows on the farm (ch. 20), peaks at the gates (ch. 22), blurs with his memory (ch. 23). Paranoid, but not always wrong (ch. 20, 24). Grandma fires a shotgun at a noise on the roof and accuses people of stealing her things (prologue).
  - **Music.** Luzia's songs: in the market and over Inácio (ch. 2), in the war, on the farm; rejected by his family (ch. 20); one son hums one without knowing it (ch. 23, 24). The songs and lines (Olúrónbí, the funeral lament, Orí ni baba, Atotô, Ògún yè) are in research/music/luzia-songs.md. What reaches grandma: to decide.
  - **Healing.** Luzia the healer and midwife (ch. 1–2) → Benedito, who nurses his father and becomes a healer in the war (ch. 2, 6) → grandma, a nursing assistant (Prologue, Epilogue). One of the few good things passed down; never explained.
  - **Broken, not inherited:** keeping his head down. He drops it in the war and never lowers it again (ch. 5); on the farm that pride becomes the senhor's anger.

## Before the war: Bahia, 1846–1865

Slavery from the inside: an ordinary sugar estate where cruelty is routine and kindness is unreliable.

  - **Coronel Joaquim Araújo de Menezes**, the master, a *senhor de engenho* in the Recôncavo.
      - Pious, polite, in debt; thinks of himself as a "good master".
      - Forces himself on Luzia and other women; never recognises Ezequiel.
      - Real aspect: the decline of Bahian sugar, the internal slave trade south; owners donating enslaved men, freed to enlist, in the war.
      - Chapters: ch. 1–2 (sells Luzia), ch. 3 (offers enslaved men to the army), dies c. 1867–68 (Interlude).
  - **Dona Mariana**, the master's wife, Tonico's mother.
      - Teaches him prayers and letters "as a game", then stops: a kindness he resents and treasures.
      - Real aspect: the "affectionate" household slavery behind the myth of mild Brazilian slavery.
      - Chapters: ch. 1 (Tonico's birth), ch. 2.
  - **Antônio (Tonico) de Menezes**, the master's son, his age; his milk brother.
      - Weak and charming. True friends as small boys; estranged after Luzia's sale: not enemies, not friends.
      - Takes on the estate as he grows; Ezequiel defies him (ch. 3).
      - Never a candidate for the war; watches his milk brother leave and stays. A loyal friend after all.
      - Real aspect: the exemption of the elite; the "milk brother" bond.
      - Chapters: ch. 1–2, 3; banishes Ezequiel, finds Luzia (Interlude); sends her letter (ch. 17).
  - **Bastião**, best friend, enslaved on the estate.
      - Capoeirista, joker, storyteller.
      - Real aspect: capoeira as resistance; quilombos in the Recôncavo.
      - Chapters: ch. 3; refuses to go to the war and runs to a quilombo (ch. 3).
  - **Rosária**, enslaved house servant; a friend from the terreiro, perhaps his first love (to develop).
      - Clever and religious. Her sale mirrors Luzia's.
      - Real aspect: family separation through sale; the Irmandade da Boa Morte in Cachoeira.
      - They meet at Mãe Otília's terreiro when he joins her song; friends first (ch. 3).
      - Chapters: ch. 3; sold to Salvador (ch. 3). Option: he later thinks he sees her among the Boa Morte sisters (where: to decide, since he never returns to Bahia).
  - **Ezequiel**, overseer and capitão do mato; pardo; born enslaved c. 1834, freed c. 1858.
      - The master's unacknowledged son and Tonico's half-brother; nobody says it aloud.
      - Wants his father to see him as special; the master uses it to control him.
      - Enjoys cruelty and is feared for it: a man of colour who enforces slavery to stay above it.
      - Taunts Benedito with his freedom; refuses Tonico's orders as Tonico takes on the estate (ch. 3).
      - Spared from the war because he is no longer enslaved (and protected by the master): the freedom Benedito must fight for keeps Ezequiel at home, part of their rivalry (ch. 3).
      - Real aspect: the colour hierarchy (free people of colour as overseers and capitães-do-mato).
      - Chapters: his birth (ch. 1); the goiabeira night, the informer years, the pee prank (ch. 2); freed c. 1858, the first whipping, beaten by Benedito (ch. 3); banished by Tonico, the revenge of the man whose hand he took (Interlude).
  - **Mãe Otília**, a Candomblé priestess in Cachoeira.
      - Priestess of the terreiro where he meets Rosária; comes to know him there (ch. 3).
      - Gives him a guia for Ogum; he wears it through the whole war.
      - Real aspect: Candomblé surviving under Catholicism; police repression.
      - Chapters: ch. 3.

## The war: Paraguay, 1865–1870

He goes south with the Zuavos baianos, the all-Black companies of Bahian volunteers, later merged into line battalions. Violence runs both ways (Joca, Damião; Albuquerque, Benites). On the battlefield the field is level; in the camp it is not.

### Real figures (cameos)

  - **Quirino Antônio do Espírito Santo**: old Black veteran of Independence, founder of the first zouave company; a father figure; dies in Montevideo, Nov 1865 (ch. 4). Carries Henrique Dias's memory.
  - **Captain Marcolino**: zouave captain and capoeirista; climbs Benedito's back to plant the flag at Curuzu; history forgets the man underneath (ch. 5).
  - **Cândido da Fonseca Galvão, later Dom Obá II**: teaches him to read with old newspapers (ch. 5); option: a Rio visit in the 1880s (ch. 20). Service to the Emperor as a claim to citizenship.
  - **André Rebouças**: seen on the ship *São Francisco*; the first Black man with authority he has seen (ch. 4).
  - **General Osório**: speaks before Tuiuti (ch. 5); disbands the zouave companies (ch. 6).
  - **Duke of Caxias**: seen from afar; his stricter order, or its rumour (ch. 7).
  - **Ana Néri**: Bahian nurse; works beside him in the cholera (ch. 6).
  - **Count d'Eu**: praised the zouaves as "the finest troops"; leads the final hunt for López (ch. 9).

### Comrades (invented)

  - **Gregório "Doutor"**: free-born Black tailor, volunteer; reads, argues politics, believes in citizenship. Writes Benedito's first letters (ch. 4); his death of cholera ends Benedito's faith in the nation (ch. 6). Real aspect: the Voluntários da Pátria.
  - **Joca Cabinda**: enslaved, freed to serve, older and bitter. Whips a Paraguayan officer to death (ch. 5); deserts and is flogged, and Benedito refuses to help him (ch. 6). Real aspect: freed slaves in the army; desertion.
  - **Ramon Benites**: white gaucho cavalryman; charismatic, racist one minute, loyal the next; Benedito's moral rival inside his own army (ch. 7, 8). Real aspect: violence against Paraguayan civilians; tension among the allies.
  - **Padre Felício**: chaplain; keeps the only list with Benedito's real name, which is lost (ch. 6, 9).
  - **Constantino**: young cadet captured at Curupaiti (inspired by the real Bigode); back after two years at the Ybycuí foundry, he tells the Paraguayan side (ch. 7; his return date, 1869, may fit ch. 8 better).
  - **Damião**: enslaved and freed to serve, like him, brave; in a taken village his freedom becomes the power to do to others what was done to him. Benedito's dark mirror: what he could become, and one answer to "what is freedom?" (ch. 9; options in its Notes).

### The other side

  - **Sargento Mbareté** ("strength" in Guaraní): Paraguayan sergeant, a poor man fighting for someone else's glory; Benedito's mirror (ch. 5, 7). Real aspect: Paraguayan war propaganda (*Cabichuí*).
  - **Ceferina**: a *residenta*; love or debt (ch. 8). Real aspect: Paraguay's demographic catastrophe; the women who rebuilt it.
  - **The boy at Acosta Ñu**: child soldier, about 12, never named (ch. 9). Real aspect: Acosta Ñu, now Paraguay's Children's Day.
  - **Captain Albuquerque**: white Brazilian officer; humiliates and flogs Black soldiers, steals their pay; a stronger enemy than any Paraguayan (ch. 5, 7). Real aspect: racism in the army; unpaid wages.

## After the war: luck, land and the limits of money (1870–1890s)

Setting: Corumbá and the Jacadigo farms on the Bolivian border. The war freed him but did not pay him; the lottery makes him rich but not accepted.

  - **Seu Bento**: elderly free Black lottery-ticket seller; his most honest friend; tells him to save; vouches for him against the agent (ch. 11, 12, 17). Real aspect: the lotteries.
  - **Dr. Aurélio Teles**: white lawyer and notary, honest and correct; every deed still calls him *preto* (ch. 18); the land is lost anyway (ch. 24). Real aspect: the 1850 Land Law and the bureaucracy of exclusion.
  - **Luiz Gama** (real, option): Black abolitionist lawyer; challenges him over the woman he bought (ch. 20). Real aspect: legal abolitionism.
  - **The Pires family**: old cattle ranchers next door. Polite in public, hostile in private; fake nobility; slave owners who cannot undersell Liberdade. Manipulate the wife and sons, never him; lend to the sons. Real aspect: exclusion of rich Black men; credit networks (ch. 18, 20, 23, 24).
  - **Otávio** (minor): a Corumbá merchant's son; grows up with the sons in the Pires boys' shadow, later cheats them at cards (ch. 20, 24). Real aspect: the white elite of Corumbá.
  - **Firmino**: war comrade freed by the war; his most loyal, wage-paid worker and right hand; his death (to decide) leaves the farm without its old hands (ch. 20, 23). Real aspect: freed veterans looking for work and land.

## Carmen, the woman from Spain

  - Never named; the narration calls her the woman from Spain. Born c. 1858 in a fishing village near Vigo, Galicia; leaves at about 18 (c. 1876).
  - Why Galicia: the region that sent the most emigrants to Brazil and the River Plate; steamers from Vigo, river steamers up to Asunción and Corumbá.
  - Her world mirrors his: Spain still had slavery in Cuba (Moret Law 1870, abolition 1886), and poor boys went to Cuba while rich families paid the exemption (*quintas*). Her childhood runs through the 1868 revolution, Amadeo I, the First Republic and the Carlist War.

### Family

  - **Rosa Lousada**, mother, lacemaker; a "widow of the living"; lets her go and regrets it (ch. 13). Real aspect: the women left behind (Rosalía de Castro).
  - **Manuel Lousada**, father, a fisherman gone to Buenos Aires; never appears (ch. 13).
  - **Xosé Lousada**, brother, conscripted to Cuba, dies of yellow fever (ch. 13).
  - **Don Faustino**, the village priest; teaches her to read; introduces the recruiter (ch. 13).

### Personality

  - Sharp-tongued, stubborn, practical. Reads, does sums, remembers every humiliation. Catholic, superstitious about the sea, homesick for rain.
  - From object to owner of her own life: bought twice, by the traffickers and by him; she takes over the household money and the farm accounts (ch. 20).
  - Arrives with European racism; being sold teaches her his life, but it does not make things simple (ch. 15, 20).
  - Saved and bought at once: grateful, then hurt; their marriage has warmth and distance, ups and downs (ch. 19, 20).
  - Once rich: proud of appearances, wants her sons to look like nobility, uneasy that they are partly Black, sides with the white boys; the Pires lure her in (ch. 20).
  - Traumas: the deception, the passage, the fear of the brothel, losing Ruchla, never writing home the truth (ch. 13–16, 20).

## Her passage: Vigo → Buenos Aires → Corumbá (c. 1876–1877)

A free European woman could not legally be a slave in Brazil; trafficked women were sold anyway, with no papers and no law that reached them. The family legend of "buying a Spanish girl" is literally true (ch. 19).

  - **Señor Alvarado**, the *gancho*: charming recruiter with letters "from girls who married well" (ch. 13). Real aspect: the ganchos; the *trata de blancas*.
  - **Ruchla (Raquel)**: Jewish girl from Austrian Galicia, her closest friend; sold in Buenos Aires; searched for for years (ch. 14, 15, 20). Real aspect: the *polacas* (later the Zwi Migdal).
  - **Capitán Iriarte**: the steamer's captain; bribed, once kind (ch. 14). Real aspect: corrupt ports.
  - **Doña Engracia**: older Galician woman emigrating legally (ch. 14). Real aspect: ordinary emigration beside trafficking.
  - **Madame Angélique**: French madam in Buenos Aires; keeps her untouched to sell her dear (ch. 15). Real aspect: the Buenos Aires brothel economy.
  - **Seu Albino**: Portuguese tavern owner in Corumbá, a trafficker in secret; crude, cruel, a good host; passes her off as his niece; wants one big private sale (ch. 16, 19). Real aspect: Portuguese dominance of commerce and trafficking.
  - **Lucília**: free Black laundress at Albino's; her first friend; godmother of her first son (ch. 16, 19, 20). Real aspect: free Black women's work and *compadrio*.

## The sons

  - Born c. 1878–1890; rich, mixed-race (*mestiços*), accepted by neither side; grow up in the years of abolition and the Republic.
  - Spoiled and wounded: never learn the land; suffer racism however rich; proud and angry; use money to compensate (ch. 20).
  - Lose everything at the card table to the white boys they grew up with (ch. 24). The *jogo do bicho* (Rio, 1892) stays in the background, for grandma's time.
  - **First son**: raised by his mother and Lucília; the heir; wants the white elite's acceptance (ch. 20). To add: name, character, what he gambled on.
  - **Silvio**: his father's favourite; cocky, lucky at cards until he isn't; scams people, looks for easy money; becomes a garimpeiro (ch. 24). To add: his fate; where he digs (the diamond garimpos of Mato Grosso).
  - **The São João son**: beaten at the festival, afraid of his father all his life (ch. 21, 24). Which son: to decide.
  - **Others / daughters**: grandma's line; who carried the story down to her: to decide.

## Who is around at each stage (a menu, not a cast list)

  - **Bahia (1846–1865):** Luzia, Inácio, Coronel Menezes, Dona Mariana, Tonico, Bastião, Rosária, Ezequiel, Mãe Otília. Mentioned: the Malê rebels, the 1857 *ganhadores* strike.
  - **War (1865–1870):** Quirino, Marcolino, Dom Obá, Gregório, Joca Cabinda, Damião, Ramon Benites, Padre Felício, Constantino, Mbareté, Ceferina, the boy at Acosta Ñu, Captain Albuquerque. Passing through: Rebouças, Osório, Caxias, Ana Néri, the Count d'Eu, López.
  - **Free, not equal (1870–c. 1877):** Seu Bento, Paraguayan workers, the clerks and police of Corumbá; far away, Tonico and Luzia (Interlude).
  - **Her road (1858–1877):** Rosa, Manuel and Xosé Lousada, Don Faustino, Alvarado, Ruchla, Doña Engracia, Capitán Iriarte, Madame Angélique, Seu Albino, Lucília.
  - **Fortune (c. 1877–1889):** Firmino and the other veterans at Liberdade, the Pires sons and Otávio, Seu Bento, the lottery agent, Dr. Teles, the Pires family, Lucília, Dom Obá, Luiz Gama; the Emperor and Princess Isabel only as news.
  - **The fall (1890s):** the sons, the Pires family, card tables and garimpos (Silvio); grandma's line.

### Open questions

  - Settled: after the war he stays in Mato Grosso; the farms are at Jacadigo, near Corumbá. Still open: exact farm locations, names and dates.
  - Did he go to war from Bahia (which makes the zouave arc work) or from somewhere else?
  - Did the farms use enslaved labour before 1888? It is the hardest choice in the book.
  - Was she Galician? Grandma said "Spanish". Andalusia or the Canary Islands are the alternatives.
  - When did he die, and how old were the sons?
  - Which son or daughter was grandma's parent?
  - Which lottery did Corumbá buy in the 1870s, and how was a winner on the frontier paid? Working version in Chapters, ch. 17 (Loner 2014).
