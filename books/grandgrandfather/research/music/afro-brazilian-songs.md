# Famous songs of the enslaved, to borrow

Oct 30, 2026 · research for Rafael

Rafael's brief: well-known songs sung by enslaved people, not necessarily from the Recôncavo, that the book can borrow. Luzia's own repertoire (Yoruba: Olúrónbí, the funeral lament, Orí) is in [luzia-songs.md](luzia-songs.md). Copy the lyrics in later from the links; the reading tools here do not reproduce song texts. Quote only short lines in the book unless the text is public domain.

## Brazilian

  1. **Vissungos (Minas Gerais).** Work songs of Afro-descendant miners in São João da Chapada (Diamantina), in a Bantu-based language; some went with particular stages of the work, and they were also a coded speech whites could not follow. Aires da Mata Machado Filho recorded 65 from 1928 (*O negro e o garimpo em Minas Gerais*, 1943). Clementina de Jesus, Doca da Portela and Geraldo Filme recorded them on the album **O Canto dos Escravos** (1982), the best-known way to hear them. [DOCUMENTED] Use: the songs Benedito's family sings at work, borrowed from Minas (the book's own choice; a Bantu song next to Luzia's Yoruba ones also shows the estate's mix of nations).
  2. **"Pai João" (lundu, 19th century, anonymous).** A satirical song in the voice of an enslaved African: he was someone in his own land; here his master calls the Black man a drunk and a thief while he drinks and steals himself and is rewarded for it. Printed in *Canções populares do Brasil* (score on Musica Brasilis, from the BnF / Gallica). [DOCUMENTED; exact date to verify] Use: the boys' mocking song about the master, ch. 2–3; old enough to be public domain.
  3. **"Marinheiro só" (samba de roda / chula, Recôncavo).** Traditional, sung in the rodas of the Recôncavo; made famous by Clementina de Jesus. [Traditional; 19th-century age to verify] Use: the samba de roda at the festa (ch. 3).
  4. **Jongo pontos (Paraíba Valley, Rio).** Jongo (caxambu), a Bantu drum dance from the coffee fazendas; its pontos (sung riddles) spoke in code about masters and overseers. [DOCUMENTED] Use: Vassouras, Luzia's last years (Interlude).
  5. **Capoeira corridos.** "Zum zum zum, capoeira mata um" (warning of danger, the wasp), "Paranauê", "Marinheiro só" again. All traditional, but recorded only in the 20th century; their 19th-century age is unknown, and the berimbau is not yet in the book's roda. [Age: to verify; use sparingly, with no claim of date]

## Not Brazilian (for reference only)

  - **US spirituals**: "Go Down Moses", "Swing Low", "Steal Away", "Wade in the Water" (first printed 1901; its link to escapes is legend). 19th-century texts, public domain, famous, but English-language and Protestant: they would feel foreign in Bahia. Not recommended for the book; useful only as a model of double meaning (faith and escape), which the vissungos and jongo pontos also carry.
