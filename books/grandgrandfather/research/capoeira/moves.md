# Capoeira moves (for ch. 3)

## What capoeira looked like in his time

  - No berimbau yet in the book's roda (drums, clapping and song). Played in secret on the estate; in cities, gangs trained on Sunday mornings: first empty hands, then sticks, then blades (Plácido de Abreu, Rio, 1886). [DOCUMENTED for Rio]
  - Nineteenth-century names (Abreu, 1886): **chifrada** and **topete a cheirar** (headbutts), **lamparina** (slap), **moquete** (punch), **caçador** (a take-down along the ground, sweeping the opponent's feet), **banho de fumaça** (a throw), **passo de siricopé** (a jump after a feint), **negaça** (the feint itself). [DOCUMENTED]
  - Its roots: the Angolan engolo, a kicking game whose inverted, hands-on-the-ground kicks capoeira kept. [DOCUMENTED]

## The core moves (names as known today)

  - **Ginga**: the constant swaying base step; everything starts from it.
  - **Esquiva / negativa**: dodges, low to the ground.
  - **Aú**: the cartwheel, to escape and reposition.
  - **Cabeçada**: the headbutt, the oldest and most feared blow of the street.
  - **Rasteira**: the sweep that takes the legs from under the opponent; with the meia lua de compasso, the most iconic move.
  - **Meia lua de frente / armada / queixada**: circular kicks.
  - **Martelo**: a straight "hammer" kick.
  - **Rabo de arraia** ("stingray's tail"): the old name for the spinning, hands-down heel kick; today usually called **meia lua de compasso**. The body drops, hands on the ground, eyes on the target between the arms; the straight leg whips round and the heel strikes the head. One of the most powerful kicks in capoeira; a player's skill is judged by how fast and hard he does it; if it lands properly it can be fatal. Popular in nineteenth-century capoeira; in 1909 Ciríaco beat a Japanese jiu-jitsu champion in Rio with a rabo de arraia. [DOCUMENTED]

## For the story: the move he cannot land

  - **Candidate: the rabo de arraia.** Hard to time, needs strength, balance and the nerve to put your head down in front of the enemy; failed, it leaves you exposed on the ground. Perfect for a long apprenticeship of failures and one decisive success.
  - It fits the book's images: he must lower his head to win, the boy taught by Luzia to keep his head down turns that into a weapon.
